A $1,500 TV and a $100 soundbar is a tonal mismatch you can hear within ten seconds of turning the volume up. Modern TVs are thinner than they were a decade ago, which means the speakers have less cabinet volume and produce thinner sound. A soundbar fixes that without the wiring mess of a traditional surround system.

The harder question is which one. The category runs from $80 stereo bars that plug into a TV's HDMI port to $1,600 Dolby Atmos rigs with rear speakers and a wireless subwoofer. Get the wrong one and you end up with dialogue you can't make out, bass that rattles the wall behind the TV, or surround channels that never quite convince you someone is behind you.

## Channels explained: 2.0, 2.1, 3.1, 5.1, and Dolby Atmos

The number before the dot is how many distinct speaker channels the bar contains. The number after the dot is whether there's a subwoofer (.1) or not (.0). Larger numbers mean more speakers, which usually means more directional sound.

**2.0** is two channels, left and right, no subwoofer. The simplest form — a stereo bar that improves on built-in TV speakers but doesn't add bass or surround. The Bose TV Speaker ($280) and Roku Streambar ($130) sit here. 2.0 is fine for a kitchen TV or a bedroom; the dialogue boost is the only meaningful upgrade over TV speakers.

**2.1** adds a wireless subwoofer. The bar handles left/right, the sub handles low frequencies (typically below 100 Hz). This is where you start to feel explosions and bass-heavy music. The Sony HT-S2000 ($400) and Samsung HW-B550 ($300) are 2.1 picks. A 2.1 system is the right call for apartments and smaller rooms where you want sound without overwhelming the space.

**3.1** adds a dedicated center channel. The three channels are left, right, and center — that center channel is the one that carries dialogue in most movies. With a 3.1 system, dialogue is clearer at lower volumes and you can actually hear what people are saying during a busy action scene. The Sony HT-G700 ($500) and Samsung HW-Q60C ($500) are midrange 3.1 bars.

**5.1** is full surround — left, right, center, two rear channels, and a subwoofer. The rear speakers are usually separate satellites that you wire to the sub (the sub becomes the wireless hub for everything else). The Samsung HW-Q800C ($700), Vizio M-Series 5.1 ($500), and JBL Bar 5.1 ($600) all ship with rear speakers. A 5.1 system is the floor for serious home theater buyers who watch a lot of action, horror, or concert films.

**Dolby Atmos** adds height channels. A 5.1.2 system has five surround channels, one sub, and two upward-firing speakers that bounce sound off the ceiling to create the illusion of overhead audio (rain, helicopters, planes). A 5.1.4 adds four height channels. The Sonos Arc ($899), Samsung HW-Q990C ($1,500), and Vizio Elevate ($1,000) are Atmos picks. Atmos only works if the content is encoded for it — Netflix, Max, Disney+, and 4K Blu-rays all carry Atmos soundtracks.

For most buyers, 3.1 with a good sub is the sweet spot. For serious home theater, 5.1.2 is the upgrade that actually changes how movies sound.

## Subwoofer: the part that makes movies feel like movies

The subwoofer handles everything below about 100 Hz — bass, explosions, the low end of music. A soundbar with no sub sounds thin; a soundbar with a small sub sounds OK; a soundbar with an 8" wireless sub makes the floor vibrate during the Marvel intro.

Wireless subs are paired at the factory, so the bar and the sub talk to each other automatically. You plug each into a wall outlet and you're done. Run the auto-calibration routine in the bar's app and the sub levels itself to the room.

A few soundbars — Sonos Arc, Bose Smart Soundbar 900 — don't ship with a sub. You buy the sub separately (Sonos Sub $799, Bose Bass Module 700 $749). Skip the sub and these bars still sound good; add the sub and they sound great.

Subwoofer size matters. 6" subs fill a small room; 8" subs fill a living room; 10" and 12" subs fill a basement or open-plan space. Larger subs also reproduce lower frequencies cleanly — a 10" sub gets to 25-30 Hz, a 6" sub is rolling off by 40 Hz.

If you live in an apartment or share walls with neighbors, a smaller sub or a sub with a low-volume mode matters. The Sonos Sub and the Samsung HW-Q990C sub both have night modes that tame the deep bass without killing the rest of the sound.

## Sound formats: Dolby Atmos, DTS:X, Dolby Digital, and why it matters

The format you listen in matters as much as the speaker count. Four are in active use:

- **Dolby Atmos.** Object-based surround — each sound is a spatial object that can move anywhere in the room. Available on Netflix, Max, Disney+, Apple TV+, and most 4K Blu-rays.
- **DTS:X.** The DTS competitor to Atmos. Less widely supported on streaming but common on Blu-rays. Samsung high-end bars support DTS:X; LG, Sony, and Vizio typically don't.
- **Dolby Digital and DTS.** Compressed 5.1 surround, supported by every soundbar. This is what broadcast TV and most cable still delivers.
- **Stereo PCM.** Two-channel uncompressed audio, what older content and most music uses.

For Atmos support, the bar needs HDMI eARC (the enhanced audio return channel) on its HDMI port. eARC carries the full Atmos signal back from the TV to the bar over a single HDMI cable. Standard ARC tops out at Dolby Digital Plus, which is compressed Atmos — better than nothing, not as good as full-fat Atmos.

If your TV has HDMI eARC (most 2019+ TVs do), get a soundbar that supports eARC. If your TV only has regular ARC, you're fine with any bar that supports Dolby Digital Plus.

## HDMI eARC vs ARC vs optical: the cable that carries the signal

How the bar talks to the TV is the most common install problem. Three options, in order of preference:

**HDMI eARC** is the best. One HDMI cable from the bar to the TV's eARC port (usually HDMI 3 or 4 on most TVs, look for the "eARC" or "ARC" label). It carries Atmos, DTS:X, Dolby Digital, and stereo. It also lets the TV remote control the bar's volume over CEC.

**HDMI ARC** is the older standard. Same cable, same port, but limited to Dolby Digital Plus and stereo. Lose the ability to pass full Atmos from the TV's built-in apps.

**Optical (Toslink)** is the cable from a decade ago. No HDMI handshake, no CEC volume control, maxes out at Dolby Digital. If your TV doesn't have an ARC-capable HDMI port, optical works but plan to use two remotes.

The Sonos Arc, Bose Smart Soundbar 900, Samsung HW-Q990C, and Sony HT-A7000 all require eARC for full Atmos. If your TV is older than 2019, check whether it has an HDMI port labeled ARC. If not, you'll need an HDMI audio extractor ($30-50) or a new TV.

## Smart features: Alexa, Google Assistant, AirPlay, Chromecast

Most modern soundbars are also smart speakers. The four platforms in active use:

- **Amazon Alexa.** Built into Sonos Beam (Gen 2), Bose Smart Soundbar 600/900, Sonos Arc, and most Sony and Samsung bars. Voice commands work with the bar's built-in far-field mics.
- **Google Assistant.** Built into Sony, Vizio, and some JBL bars. Equivalent functionality to Alexa.
- **Apple AirPlay 2.** Stream audio from iOS devices and Macs. Available on Sonos, Bose, Sony, Samsung, and most premium bars.
- **Chromecast built-in.** Stream from Android and Chrome. Available on Sony, Vizio, and JBL.

Voice assistants are convenient for volume and playback but double as always-listening microphones. Sonos and Bose offer hard-mute switches for the mics; some cheaper brands don't. If privacy matters, look for a hard-mute button on the bar.

Multi-room audio — playing music through the bar in the living room and a Sonos One in the kitchen at the same time — only works if the bar's ecosystem supports it. Sonos is the gold standard. Bose and Yamaha have similar setups. Samsung and Sony are locked into their own ecosystems.

## The brand matrix: who makes what

**Sonos** is the design pick. The Beam (Gen 2, $449) is a compact Dolby Atmos bar — the right choice for rooms under 200 sq ft. The Arc ($899) is the flagship Atmos bar; pair it with the Sonos Sub ($799) and Sonos Era 300 rears ($449 each) for a 7.1.4 setup at roughly $2,600. Sonos app is the smoothest multi-room system in the category.

**Bose** is the dialogue specialist. The Smart Soundbar 600 ($500) is the entry Atmos pick. The Smart Soundbar 900 ($900) is the premium model. Bose's PhaseGuide technology produces wider stereo sound than bars physically its size. The Bose Bass Module 700 ($749) and Surround Speakers 700 ($500/pair) round out a full setup.

**Samsung** is the Atmos value leader. The HW-Q600C ($400) is a 3.1 entry. The HW-Q800C ($700) is a 5.1.2 midrange. The HW-Q990C ($1,500) is a 11.1.4 flagship with rear speakers and a sub. Samsung bars pair especially well with Samsung TVs via Q-Symphony — the TV's speakers and the bar play together for a wider soundstage.

**Sony** is the movie-pick. The HT-S2000 ($400) is a 3.1 bar; the HT-G700 ($500) is a virtual 7.1.2. The HT-A7000 ($1,000) is the flagship Atmos bar with built-in up-firing drivers. Sony's Vertical Surround Engine does the best job of creating overhead sound from a single bar.

**Vizio** is the budget champion. The M-Series 5.1 ($500) ships with a sub and rear speakers — no other brand includes those at $500. The Elevate ($1,000) is a flagship Atmos bar with rotating speakers that physically aim upward when Atmos is playing.

**JBL** is the party pick. The Bar 5.1 ($600) has detachable wireless rear speakers you can take to a different room. The Bar 1000 ($1,200) is the upgraded Atmos version of the same idea. JBL bars are louder than most competitors at the same price.

**Polk, Yamaha, Klipsch** round out the category. The Polk Signa S4 ($400) is a 3.1.2 Atmos bar at the budget end. The Yamaha YAS-209 ($300) is a 2.1 bar with built-in Alexa. The Klipsch Cinema 600 ($700) is a 3.1 bar focused on dialogue clarity.

## How to pick the right soundbar for you

- **Bedroom or kitchen, just want clearer dialogue:** Bose TV Speaker ($280) or Sonos Ray ($300).
- **Living room, movies and TV, midrange budget:** Sonos Beam (Gen 2) ($449) or Samsung HW-Q800C ($700).
- **Living room, full home theater, Atmos:** Sonos Arc ($899) + Sonos Sub ($799) — roughly $1,700 total. Samsung HW-Q990C ($1,500) if you want everything in the box.
- **Big open-plan room, want it loud:** JBL Bar 1000 ($1,200).
- **Rear speakers are a dealbreaker:** Vizio M-Series 5.1 ($500) — only bar at this price that includes them.

## The bottom line

A 3.1 or 5.1 soundbar with a wireless sub fixes 90% of the gap between TV speakers and a real home theater for $500-800. Spend more on a Dolby Atmos setup only if your room is right for it (flat ceiling, enclosed space) and you watch enough Atmos content to justify it.
