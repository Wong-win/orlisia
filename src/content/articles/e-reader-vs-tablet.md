If you read more than an hour a day, the device you read on matters. Tablets are wonderful for many things and a poor compromise for reading books. E-readers are wonderful for books and a poor compromise for everything else. The choice is not actually close once you understand what each screen does to your eyes, your battery, and your attention.

This is the comparison we walk friends through when they ask whether to buy a $159 Kindle Paperwhite or a $499 iPad mini.

## E Ink versus LCD and AMOLED, in physical terms

E Ink is a reflective technology. Light bounces off the page the same way it bounces off a printed book. There is no backlight shining into your retina. The Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition uses a 7-inch E Ink Carta 1300 display at 300 pixels per inch, which is sharper than most printed paper. The Kobo Libra Colour adds a color filter layer that lets you read color book covers, comics, and highlighted text in color, with only a small hit to sharpness and contrast.

LCD and AMOLED are emissive technologies. They produce light that shines directly into your eyes. The iPad mini's 8.3-inch Liquid Retina display hits 326 ppi and 500 nits of brightness. The Samsung Galaxy Tab S10's 11-inch AMOLED hits 120Hz and 1,000 nits. Both look gorgeous for video and games. Both look harsh when you read on them at 10 p.m. for two hours.

The technical difference matters because of how the human eye processes light over time. Reflective light (E Ink) allows the iris to relax. Emissive light (LCD, AMOLED) keeps the iris constricted and triggers the same eye-strain pathways that office workers experience after a long monitor day. After two hours of reading on an iPad, most people notice dry eyes. After two hours on a Kindle, most people notice nothing.

## Reading comfort and eye strain

If you read more than an hour a day, eye strain compounds. We have watched friends try to use an iPad as their primary reader and last an average of three weeks before reaching for a pair of blue-light glasses and complaining about headaches. The blue-light filters on iPad and Samsung tablets help at the margin but do not solve the underlying problem, which is emissive light at close range for long sessions.

E Ink also wins on weight. The Kindle Paperwhite weighs 211 grams. The Kobo Libra Colour weighs 199.5 grams. The Kindle Oasis weighs 188 grams. The iPad mini weighs 293 grams, and the iPad Air weighs 462 grams. Hold an iPad up for two hours of one-handed reading and your wrist knows it. The Oasis has a built-in grip on the back that solves this.

Warm-light adjustment is now standard on both sides. The Kindle Paperwhite Signature lets you shift the front light from cool white to warm amber, and it can do so automatically based on the time of day. The iPad mini has True Tone, which warms the screen based on ambient lighting. Both reduce eye strain. Both are a poor substitute for moving to a reflective display.

## Battery life, in weeks not hours

An iPad mini will last about 10 hours of reading on a charge. A Kindle Paperwhite will last about 12 weeks of half-hour daily sessions. A Kobo Libra Colour will last about 7 weeks. A Boox Page will last about 6 weeks.

The gap is not marketing. E Ink only consumes power when the page turns. The Kindle Paperwhite's 1,900 mAh battery can power the display for weeks because the screen is essentially inert between page refreshes. LCD and AMOLED refresh the entire display 60 to 120 times per second, which is why they drain in hours.

For travel, this matters more than the screen. A Kindle on a two-week trip does not need its charger. An iPad needs a daily outlet or a battery pack. We bring a Kindle on every flight and have never worried about the battery, which is a freedom that compounds over years.

## Sunlight and outdoor reading

E Ink in direct sunlight looks like a printed page. LCD in direct sunlight looks like a faintly glowing mirror you have to tilt to read. AMOLED is somewhat better than LCD outdoors because of higher peak brightness, but neither beats E Ink at a beach, a park, or a porch.

The Kobo Sage and Boox Go 7 add stylus support, which lets you annotate and write in the margins. The reMarkable 2 is a pure writing tablet — no apps, no distractions, just a paper-like surface for notes and reading PDFs.

## Features beyond reading

Here is where the tablet earns its existence. The iPad mini runs every streaming app, every messaging app, every note-taking app, every game. The Kindle runs books. The Kobo runs books, articles from Pocket, and library loans through OverDrive. The Boox runs Android, which means it can run the Kindle app, the Kobo app, the Libby app, and Pocket — but Boox hardware is slower than an iPad and the E Ink refresh rate makes video unwatchable.

If you read, watch, work, and game on one device, the iPad wins. If you read and you want a distraction-free surface, the Kindle or Kobo wins. If you read PDFs and write margin notes for work, the reMarkable 2 or Boox wins.

## The brand matrix

Amazon's Kindle line is the easiest recommendation for most people. The $159 Kindle Paperwhite is the best balance of price, screen size, and battery life. The $189 Paperwhite Signature adds wireless charging and a light sensor. The $279 Oasis adds physical page-turn buttons and an aluminum body. All three integrate seamlessly with Amazon's bookstore and Kindle Unlimited.

Kobo's lineup is the better choice for library users and for people who do not want to live inside Amazon. The $179 Kobo Clara Colour is the smallest color E Ink reader. The $219 Libra Colour has a 7-inch screen and physical buttons. The $419 Kobo Elipsa is a 10.3-inch reading and writing tablet. Kobo devices integrate with OverDrive for library loans and Pocket for read-later articles.

reMarkable makes a single product, the reMarkable 2, a 10.3-inch paper tablet for reading PDFs and writing notes. No app store, no email, no notifications. Boox makes a broad lineup from the 7-inch Boox Page to the 13.3-inch Boox Tab Ultra, all running Android with Google Play access.

Apple's iPad mini at $499 remains the best small tablet for reading and everything else, though it is heavy and short on battery for a reading-only device.

## Storage, library access, and DRM

Most e-readers ship with 16 to 64 GB of internal storage. That sounds small, but text books at roughly 1 MB each mean a 32 GB Kindle holds about 25,000 books — more than anyone will read in a lifetime. Audiobooks eat storage at about 30 MB per hour, so heavy audiobook listeners should pay attention to storage size. The Kindle Oasis ships with 32 GB, the Kobo Libra Colour with 32 GB, the Boox Page with 64 GB, and the iPad mini with 128 to 512 GB.

Library access is the underrated feature that decides a lot. In the United States, OverDrive and Libby give you free access to your local library's digital catalog. Kobo integrates with OverDrive natively — borrow a book, sync, read, return, all from the device. Kindle does not, and you have to side-load library books via the Libby app and Adobe Digital Editions. If you rely on library lending, Kobo is the right pick.

DRM (digital rights management) is the reason you cannot move a Kindle book to a Kobo and vice versa. Amazon's DRM ties books to the Kindle ecosystem, Kobo's ties to Kobo, and Apple's iBooks ties to Apple. If your library is mostly free (Project Gutenberg, Standard Ebooks, library books), DRM does not matter. If you have paid $500 to a Kindle library over five years, switching costs you real money.

## How we recommend choosing

If you read more than an hour a day and you have another device for video and work, get a Kindle Paperwhite or a Kobo Libra Colour. The $159 to $219 buys you years of better sleep and fewer headaches. If you want library loans and color, get the Kobo. If you live inside Amazon, get the Kindle. If you read PDFs and write margin notes for work, get the reMarkable 2 or a Boox. If you want one device for reading, video, and work, accept the eye strain and buy the iPad mini.

## The bottom line

The right answer for most readers is an e-reader at $159 to $279, and a tablet is a poor second choice for books. We would pick the Kindle Paperwhite Signature for Amazon households, the Kobo Libra Colour for library users, the reMarkable 2 for note takers, and the iPad mini only when reading is one of three or four jobs the device must do.
