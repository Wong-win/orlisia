The staircase is the largest vertical surface in the house and the one we look at most without realizing it. It is also the surface we touch. We feel the railing under our hands every morning. We hear the treads under our shoes every evening. We see the line of light from the newel post every time we walk past the upstairs hall. Most staircases in houses built before 2000 are functional and ugly. A reno at $2,500 to $15,000 changes both at once.

This guide walks through the four common railing styles, the four common tread materials, three lighting patterns that integrate cleanly, the code requirements that trip people up, and the real cost tiers.

## Railing Styles

**Modern.** A horizontal bar top rail, vertical 1.5-inch square balusters on 4-inch centers, and a flush mount newel post. The look is the open-plan loft stair that runs in every new townhouse since 2015. Steel or aluminum balusters with a white oak or walnut handrail. Most builders quote $80 to $150 per linear foot installed.

**Traditional.** A turned wood baluster (colonial style), a profiled handrail (the 6010 or 6210 profile from L.J. Smith or House of Fara), and a box newel with a cap. Best in pre-1940 homes where the stair should match the trim. Most installers pull the profile from a catalog and assemble from stock. $45 to $95 per linear foot installed.

**Glass.** Tempered or laminated glass panels set in a channel or standoff mounts. The expensive look and the most photographed. A single 36-inch-tall glass panel runs $120 to $260 per panel; a full flight of 8 to 12 panels runs $1,200 to $2,800 in glass alone. Most glass railings need a top rail by code — a small aluminum or wood cap that saves the code requirement and looks intentional.

**Cable.** Stainless steel cable runs through drilled newels and balusters, with tensioners at each end. The most "architect" look in this list. Most jurisdictions allow cable railings under IRC code if the cables are on 4-inch centers and the top rail is the right height. The cable hardware runs $40 to $80 per linear foot; the labor to assemble runs the price to $100 to $200 per linear foot installed.

For most renos, the choice isn't the look — it's the way the rail meets the second floor. A railing that terminates at the wall needs a return or a newel post; a railing that lands on a knee wall needs a half-post; a railing at the open side of the upstairs hallway needs its own design moment.

## Tread Materials

**Carpet.** The cheapest, the quietest, and the easiest to stain. Most stair runners are 36 inches wide and run down the center of the treads, with the wood nosing exposed at the edges. A wool runner from The Bell House, Stark, or Studio A runs $30 to $80 per linear foot installed. A full carpet runner over pad adds about 60% to the install price but extends the carpet life by absorbing impact.

**Wood.** Solid oak, white oak, or walnut treads run $35 to $80 per tread, installed. Most stair carpenters can match the existing floor species in 10 business days. Engineered wood treads (the veneer-glued-onto-plywood ones from Stair-Treads.com) are cheaper at $25 to $50 per tread but cannot be refinished as many times.

**Stone.** Marble, travertine, or limestone treads transform the look but require structural reinforcement — a stone tread can weigh 40 to 80 pounds each and a full flight adds 600 to 1,200 pounds to the framing. Most older homes need a structural engineer's review before stone treads go in. $80 to $200 per tread installed.

**Tile.** Porcelain treads (the 12-inch plank tile from Daltile, MSI, or Bedrosians) bridge the carpet-stone gap. Easier to source than stone, lighter than stone, more durable than wood. Most installations run $50 to $120 per tread installed.

## Lighting Integration

Stair lighting serves two purposes. The first is safety — a lit stair at night is required by code in some jurisdictions and by good sense in every home. The second is mood — the look of a glowing stair handrail or LED tread lights changes the entire second-floor landing.

**Three patterns that hold up.**

- **Recessed step lights.** The 3-inch recessed LED from Nora, WAC, or ELCO Lighting — inserted into the wall at each riser, between 6 and 12 inches off the tread. The most subtle look. Most homes need 8 to 14 fixtures for a full flight; budget $35 to $90 per fixture plus an electrician.
- **LED tape under the nosing.** A continuous run of 24V LED tape (the commercial-grade Bridgelux or Luminus strips, sold by the meter from Karma Lighting or Flexfire) set in an aluminum extrusion below the nosing. Linear, continuous, modern. The strip itself runs $12 to $28 per linear foot; the dimmer and driver add another $200 to $400 per zone.
- **Tread-mounted light bars.** A small low-profile bar set into the tread itself or the riser. Looks like an airplane runway. The L&S Lighting range at $80 to $180 per bar is the common pick.

The lighting plan needs to be wired before the treads are installed. Adding lighting to an existing stair means opening up the wall, fishing wire, and patching drywall — that doubles the install cost in most cases.

## Code Requirements That Matter

A stair renovation fails an inspection when any of the following violates code. The numbers below reflect the 2021 IRC, which most jurisdictions still enforce; some localities (California, Massachusetts) layer additional requirements on top.

- **Tread depth.** 11 inches minimum measured from nosing to nosing (the nosing can project up to 1.25 inches, so the underlying tread can be 9.75 inches as long as the nosing plus tread adds to 11).
- **Riser height.** 7.75 inches maximum, 4 inches minimum. Maximum variation within a flight is 3/8 inch.
- **Handrail height.** 34 to 38 inches above the nosing.
- **Handrail graspability.** A round or oval handrail must be between 1.25 and 2 inches in cross-section; a rectangular rail must have a perimeter between 4 and 6.25 inches. The point is that an actual hand can wrap around the rail.
- **Baluster spacing.** No opening allows a 4-inch sphere to pass through. The sphere test is real — inspectors carry one.
- **Landings.** A landing (minimum 36 inches deep in the direction of travel) is required at the top and bottom of every flight, and required when a door opens onto the stair.
- **Lighting.** A switched light source at the top and bottom of the flight. Many jurisdictions require a 3-way switch so the lights can be turned off from either end.

Most of these numbers are non-negotiable. Glass railings have to use laminated glass that, when broken, stays in the frame. Cable railings have to be on 4-inch centers to pass the sphere test. A horizontal mid-rail is required by some jurisdictions on cable systems to prevent climbability.

## Cost Tiers

**Cosmetic reno, $2,500 to $5,500.** Keep the existing structure. Replace the balusters with modern square metal balusters (the ½-inch square from L.J. Smith at $9 to $14 each, plus a wood handrail and newel caps). Add a stain or paint to the existing treads and risers. Pull the carpet. 2 to 4 days of work for a carpenter, 1 day of painting.

**Partial reno, $5,500 to $12,000.** Replace treads and risers with solid oak, redo the railing system in a new style (cable, glass, or modern metal), add LED nosing tape. 1 to 2 weeks. The existing framing carries the new loads; no structural work needed.

**Full reno, $12,000 to $30,000.** Reroute the stair entirely — new footprint, new stringers, often a new sub-framing pattern. Add integrated lighting from scratch. Most full renos happen as part of a larger project (a kitchen reno, an addition, a basement finish). 3 to 6 weeks of work, often coordinated with a structural engineer.

Add 15% to 25% to any of these numbers for homes on the West Coast or in the Northeast, and subtract 10% to 15% in the Midwest or Southeast.

## Permits and Inspections

A stair renovation almost always requires a permit when the work touches the structural framing or changes the railing profile. The threshold varies by jurisdiction — many counties require a permit only for structural changes (new stringers, replacing treads with stone), while others require a permit for any change to the railing. A cosmetic refresh with no structural change typically does not need a permit.

The inspection costs between $75 and $300 in most jurisdictions and runs as a single site visit after the work is complete. Some jurisdictions call for two inspections — one rough-in after framing, one final after the treads and rail are installed. The inspector measures the rise, run, and baluster spacing against the IRC numbers above and signs off if everything passes.

A failed inspection means rework on the risers, balusters, or handrail at the carpenter's expense. Most carpenters absorb one round of rework in their original quote; the second round is on the homeowner.

## The bottom line

Match the railing style to the house era before you match it to Pinterest, and get the lighting wired into the wall before the treads go down — the rough-in is 80% of the work and impossible to add later.
