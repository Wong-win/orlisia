Two shirts can sit on the same rack at the same price and last five seasons and two seasons, respectively. The price tag tells you almost nothing. The brand tells you slightly more. The fabric content, the stitching density, the hardware weight, and the way the garment was cut and sewn are what actually matter.

This is a practical guide, not a textile engineering textbook. We will not ask you to thread-count a poplin or count stitches per inch under a loupe. We will give you the field tests that work in a normal dressing room, in ten minutes, on a normal Tuesday. These are the same tests merchandisers, stylists, and product photographers use to evaluate pieces before they ever appear on a website.

If you apply five of these tests consistently, you'll cut your rate of bad purchases meaningfully. Apply all of them and you will rarely, if ever, get surprised by a garment that does not last.

## Why two shirts at the same price can be worlds apart

Quality in clothing is a function of three things: the materials, the construction, and the design. The first two are technical. The third is aesthetic. A garment can have flawless stitching and a luxurious fabric and still look cheap because the proportions are wrong, the styling is dated, or the dye batch is off. Conversely, a beautifully designed garment can fall apart by month four because the brand cut corners on the back-of-house.

The garment industry is unusually opaque about this. Brands are not required to disclose fiber origin, fabric mill, finishing process, or even country of final assembly. A "Made in Italy" label is a useful signal, but it tells you where the stitching happened — not what was stitched or how. A $350 "Made in Italy" cashmere sweater at a luxury house can use yarn spun in Inner Mongolia and dyed in Prato. That does not make it bad. It means the label tells you less than the cashmere micron count does.

The practical move is to learn to evaluate the garment in your hands, regardless of where it was made or who was paid to put their name on it.

## Reading the fabric: fiber, weight, and weave

**Fiber content.** Look at the care label first. The blend percentages and the fiber names will tell you almost everything. A 100 percent cotton shirt is more durable than a 65/35 cotton-polyester one, but it will also wrinkle more. A 100 percent linen shirt is durable and breathable but will crease dramatically at the elbows and seat within an hour. A 100 percent merino wool sweater will outlast a 100 percent acrylic one by ten times, but it will also cost more and require more careful washing.

The honest answer is that no fiber is universally "best." Each has trade-offs. The bad answer is "polyester," which in most contexts is a sign of cost optimization. Polyester in a performance garment, a swimsuit, or a structured dress is appropriate. Polyester in a t-shirt is a cost compromise. Polyester in a sweater is a cost compromise at the expense of comfort and longevity.

**Weight.** Fabric weight is measured in GSM (grams per square meter) or, for denim, in ounces per square yard. A 180 GSM cotton tee is a comfortable year-round weight; 140 GSM is paper-thin and meant for layering; 220 GSM is heavy and stiff. A 12 oz denim is mid-weight, suitable for most climates; 16 oz is heavy and stiff; 8 oz is light and summery. As a general rule, a heavier fabric lasts longer because there is more fiber to wear through.

**Weave.** The weave structure affects how the fabric drapes and how it wears. A poplin shirt (tight, flat weave) reads formal. An oxford (basket weave) is casual. A twill trouser drapes more cleanly than a plain weave. A knit fabric is more forgiving than a woven one. None of these are inherently better, but a brand that understands the difference between an oxford and a poplin is signaling that they understand the basics.

**Hand feel.** This is the part you cannot substitute. Rub the fabric between your fingers. A quality wool feels dense, slightly springy, and warm. A quality cotton feels crisp or soft but never limp. A quality linen feels textured and cool. A cheap polyester-linen blend feels flat and slightly plasticky. If you do not know what to feel for, the next time you walk past a high-end menswear store in person, walk in and touch the items. Build a memory of what the good stuff feels like. Use it as a reference.

## Stitching, seams, and the anatomy of a garment

**Stitches per inch (SPI).** You can read SPI on a button placket, a side seam, or a cuff without any tools. Count the stitches in one inch. Quality tailoring runs 8 to 12 SPI. Cheap production runs 4 to 6. You will see the difference immediately. A high SPI means more yarn, more time, and a stronger seam. A low SPI means cost compromise and a seam that pulls under tension.

**Stitch type.** Look at the inside of the garment. A "chain stitch" or "lock stitch" is standard. A "flat-felled seam" (visible as a double row of stitching on a flat fold) is the gold standard for shirts and jeans. A "French seam" hides the raw edge inside the garment and is standard on quality blouses and silk pieces. Anything labeled "serged" with raw threads visible is fine on knitwear and casual garments; suspect on tailoring.

**Seam allowance.** Fold back the seam allowance at the hem or cuff. If you can see an inch or more of fabric folded inside, the garment can be let out. If the seam allowance is half an inch and serged to a quarter, it cannot. This is the practical answer to "can this be tailored?" — look at how much fabric is available inside the garment.

**Pattern matching.** On a striped or plaid shirt, the stripes should match at the side seam and across the pocket. On a windowpane check jacket, the lines should align at the front placket. Pattern matching is time-consuming and expensive. Its presence is one of the cleanest indicators that the brand invested in finishing. Its absence does not mean the garment is bad, but it means the brand was not trying.

## Hardware, linings, and details that reveal quality

**Buttons.** A mother-of-pearl button is not automatically better than a plastic one, but a thick, well-attached button is almost always better than a thin one. Look at the buttonhole. A tight, neat buttonhole with a small bar tack at either side is a quality sign. A loose, sagging buttonhole with no bar tack is a cost compromise.

**Zippers.** Look at the back of the zipper. "YKK" is the global standard, ubiquitous in both cheap and expensive garments. "Riri" is the Swiss luxury standard, usually on bags and jackets in the $300+ range. The zipper should run smoothly under tension, not just hanging free.

**Linings.** A full lining in a jacket is appropriate. A "half lining" is acceptable in a casual piece. A sleeve lining in polyester is appropriate. A sleeve lining in viscose or bemberg is a quality touch. A garment with raw, unlined seams inside is suspect — though not on knitwear, where it is normal.

**Interfacing.** In a tailored jacket, you should not be able to feel the interfacing through the outer fabric. Press the lapel between your fingers. If you feel a stiff, papery layer, the jacket has been glued rather than canvassed. Glued jackets are cheaper and will delaminate within a few seasons. Canvassed jackets are more expensive and last decades.

## The field test: how to shop like a pro

A practical in-store sequence:

1. **Read the label.** Fiber, fabric content, country of origin, care instructions. If the label is missing or vague, walk away.

2. **Feel the fabric.** Compare to a reference garment you already trust. If it feels suspiciously soft for cotton, it is likely treated for show. If it feels limp for wool, it has been over-processed.

3. **Check the seams.** Lift the inside of the garment. SPI, seam allowance, finish. Thirty seconds of inspection.

4. **Test the hardware.** Buttons, zipper, snaps. They should feel solid, not flimsy.

5. **Try it on.** Stand in natural light. Raise your arms. Bend at the waist. Sit down. If the garment pulls, gaps, or strains at the seams, the cut is wrong for you regardless of how well it was made.

6. **Look at the price relative to fabric weight and SPI.** A $30 oxford with 8 SPI is suspicious. A $300 oxford with 8 SPI is also suspicious. Match the quality to the price.

## The bottom line

You do not need to be a tailor to spot a quality garment. You need a reference garment, a ten-minute routine, and the willingness to leave the dressing room empty when a piece does not pass the tests. Focus on fiber, weight, SPI, finish, and hardware. Skip the brand name and skip the country-of-origin claim. A well-made cotton oxford from a less famous brand will outlast a poorly made one from a famous one, every time. The discipline is what separates a wardrobe that ages well from a wardrobe you keep replacing.
