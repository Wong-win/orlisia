The decision between a deck and a patio looks, from the curb, like a question about the look of the backyard. It is not. It is a question about what your lot will physically allow, what climate you live in, how much weight you want to carry on a permit, and how much maintenance you are willing to do every spring and fall. A wood deck in Minnesota and a paver patio in Phoenix are not the same decision with the same answer, and picking the cheaper option up front usually means re-picking it 12 years later.

In this guide we walk through the real cost per square foot (materials, labor, base prep), the maintenance calendars, the climate limits, and the brands you will actually find at the lumberyard or landscape yard. We draw on installed pricing from contractors in five U.S. regions and current 2026 retail rates for materials.

## Deck or patio: the underlying question

A deck is a framed, elevated surface. It needs footings, posts, beams, joists, and a decking surface. The structure is typically pressure-treated lumber, often augmented with steel hardware (Simpson Strong-Tie is the dominant brand), and the surface can be wood, composite, or PVC. Building codes require a permit for any deck more than 30 inches off the ground; below that, a "ground-level deck" can sometimes skip the permit but still needs a frost-proof base.

A patio is a surface laid on the ground. It can be poured concrete, concrete pavers, brick, natural flagstone, or tile — but in every case the most important layer is the base, not the surface. A 4-to-6-inch compacted crushed-stone base is what gives a patio its 25-year life. The surface you see is the cheaper part of the install.

The single biggest decision driver is elevation. If your yard is flat and your house is at grade, a patio is almost always the cheaper, longer-lasting choice. If your house is up 4 feet and you need somewhere to put a chair, a deck is the only practical answer.

## Wood decks: cedar, pressure-treated, and tropical hardwoods

A traditional wood deck is what most homeowners picture when they hear the word. The substructure is almost always pressure-treated Southern yellow pine — economical ($1.40 to $2.20 per board foot), strong, and chemically treated to resist rot. The visible surface is where the choice happens.

**Cedar** (Western red cedar, primarily) is the long-running favorite for the deck surface itself. It is naturally rot-resistant, light, and stable. A 5/4×6 cedar decking board runs $3.50 to $5.50 per linear foot at Home Depot or Lowe's in 2026. A 300-square-foot cedar deck realistically costs $7,500 to $11,000 installed in most markets — the surface itself is only about 25 percent of that number; the rest is footings, framing hardware, and labor.

**Pressure-treated pine** for the surface (rather than just the substructure) is the budget option at $2 to $3 per linear foot but requires annual sealing and has a 12-to-15-year lifespan before significant checking and splitting. Most homeowners who start with PT pine top the deck after year five with a stain or sealer, and replace individual boards every few years as they fail.

**Tropical hardwoods — Ipe, cumaru, tigerwood** — are the high-end option. Ipe (pronounced ee-pay) is the Brazilian walnut that has become the standard luxury decking species. It is incredibly dense, naturally fire-resistant (Class A rated), and lasts 40-plus years with minimal maintenance. The catch is price: $6 to $10 per linear foot for the boards alone, and Ipe installation requires carbide-tipped blades and pre-drilling, which adds labor. A 300-square-foot Ipe deck realistically runs $15,000 to $25,000 installed.

The honest maintenance calendar for wood: clean annually with a deck cleaner ($25 to $40 per gallon, brands like Olympic and Behr), and reseal or restain every 2 to 3 years. A cedar deck that is properly maintained gives 20 to 25 years of service. An Ipe deck left to silver out (untreated) gives 30-plus; with oil treatment every 2 to 3 years it stays the rich brown color indefinitely.

## Composite and PVC decking

Composite decking is what most decks built in 2026 are actually surfaced with. The category breaks into two camps.

**Wood-plastic composites (Trex, TimberTech, Fiberon, MoistureShield)** mix roughly 50 percent wood fiber with 50 percent recycled plastic. Trex Transcend ($4 to $6 per linear foot for the boards) is the volume leader and now offers a 25-year fade-and-stain warranty on the higher lines. TimberTech's Azek line is technically in the next category (PVC) but their composite lines (Pro and Edge) compete in the same pricing band. A 300-square-foot composite deck typically runs $9,000 to $14,000 installed.

**PVC decking (TimberTech AZEK, Wolf, Deckorators)** is 100 percent plastic, with no wood fiber at all. The advantage: zero organic material means zero mold, zero swelling, zero rot. The disadvantage: it gets hotter in direct sun (significantly — barefoot limits) and has a more plastic look in person than the marketing photos suggest. Pricing is similar to high-end composite, $5 to $8 per linear foot.

For both categories the practical maintenance is much lower than wood. You clean with soap and water once or twice a year. There is no sealing, no staining, no sanding. The trade-off is that you give up the look of real wood grain, the heat dissipation of real wood, and the ability to refinish the surface after 15 years. Scratches do not sand out; they tend to be permanent.

## Concrete patios: poured, stamped, and over-engineered

Concrete is what you build when you do not want to think about it for 25 years. The basics have not changed in a century: excavate 6 to 8 inches, compact a gravel base, build forms, pour 4 inches of 3,000-psi concrete with wire mesh or rebar, finish it.

A poured concrete patio runs $8 to $14 per square foot installed in most markets. The cheapest end is a broom-finished slab in a region with cheap concrete ($8 in central Texas, $10 in suburban Chicago). The high end is stamped concrete in a metro market ($14 to $18 per square foot), which mimics stone but does not feel like stone — most homeowners can spot stamped concrete within 10 feet.

The honest failure mode for concrete is cracking. A 4-inch slab will crack; it is a question of where and how. Control joints cut into the slab during install help. The 6-inch-thick "fiber-mesh reinforced" slabs cost another $1 to $2 per square foot and last longer but still crack. Concrete patios in freeze-thaw climates are particularly vulnerable: water gets in the crack, freezes, and pries it open.

One increasingly common upgrade is the "concrete paver" patio — not poured, but pavers — which we cover below.

## Paver patios: concrete, brick, and flagstone

This is the segment that has eaten the most of the "deck vs patio" decision over the last 20 years. Paver patios are repairable (a cracked paver replaces in 10 minutes), they handle freeze-thaw better than poured concrete, and they look better than stamped concrete.

**Concrete pavers** are 2.5-to-3-inch thick individual units from manufacturers like Belgard, Unilock, Techo-Bloc, and Pavestone. Installed cost runs $18 to $32 per square foot in most markets, with $22 being the common midpoint. The surface you see ($4 to $8 per square foot for utility pavers, $14 to $22 for "wet-cast" stone-look premium lines) is the smaller part of the cost; the base ($8 to $12 per square foot) is where the money goes.

**Brick** (real clay brick from Glen-Gery, Pine Hall, or reclaimed) is more expensive than concrete pavers — $20 to $35 per square foot installed — but has a 50-year color-fastness that concrete pavers cannot match. Brick is the right answer for Cape Cod and Mid-Atlantic colonials; less common in the South and West.

**Flagstone** (bluestone, limestone, travertine) is the premium category. Bluestone from Pennsylvania or limestone from Indiana runs $30 to $55 per square foot installed. A 350-square-foot limestone patio realistically runs $11,000 to $18,000 installed. The payoff: stone patios installed with proper base last 50-plus years with minimal maintenance.

The paver failure mode is jointing sand washing out, which causes pavers to shift and rock. Annual sweeping of polymeric sand ($25 to $40 per 50-pound bag, brands like Techniseal or Sakrete) into the joints fixes this. Plan to re-sand a paver patio every decade.

## Costs side by side

A 300-square-foot elevated cedar deck in a temperate market: $7,500 to $11,000 installed.
A 300-square-foot elevated composite deck (Trex or TimberTech): $9,000 to $14,000 installed.
A 300-square-foot poured concrete patio: $2,400 to $4,200 installed.
A 300-square-foot concrete paver patio: $5,400 to $9,600 installed.
A 300-square-foot limestone or bluestone patio: $9,000 to $16,500 installed.

These numbers reflect typical mid-market pricing — not the rock-bottom quote you'll find from a small crew working off Craigslist, and not the boutique landscape architect price either.

## Climate and use fit

Climate is the tiebreaker. A paver patio handles freeze-thaw well; a poured concrete patio cracks; a wood deck requires annual sealing and a watchful eye on joist hardware.

**Freeze-thaw regions** (Northeast, Upper Midwest, Mountain West, Canada): paver patio or PT/cedar deck. Avoid tile and poured concrete where possible. Use stainless steel or hot-dip galvanized joist hardware (Simpson Strong-Tie ZMAX or Triple Zinc line) — the standard galvanized hardware fails within 10 years in these climates.

**Hot, dry climates** (Arizona, Nevada, inland Southern California, Texas Hill Country): any surface works physically, but wood and composite decks get uncomfortably hot in summer. A paver patio with a light-colored limestone surface stays usable. Trex and TimberTech both publish surface temperature readings on their websites, and the dark colors routinely hit 150°F+ in direct sun at 95°F ambient.

**Hurricane and coastal regions** (Florida, Carolina coast, Gulf coast): the issue is wind and salt. Wood decks need marine-grade hardware and benefit from tropical hardwoods. Pavers handle storm surge well; a flooded paver patio drains and dries. Composite and PVC handle salt air better than wood.

**Wet and shady lots**: avoid anything that traps moisture. A ground-level PT deck on a weed barrier base is fine. A concrete or paver patio on a wet lot needs serious drainage planning — French drains or a dry creek bed at the uphill side.

## Maintenance time, not just cost

The cost number on the estimate only tells you what you pay up front. The annual maintenance calendar matters more than most buyers expect.

- **Cedar deck**: 4 to 6 hours per year — clean, brighten, seal.
- **Ipe deck**: 1 to 2 hours per year — oil.
- **Composite (Trex) deck**: 30 minutes per year — wash.
- **Poured concrete patio**: 1 to 2 hours per year — pressure wash, seal every 3 years.
- **Paver patio**: 2 to 3 hours per year — sweep jointing sand, occasional pressure wash.
- **Flagstone patio**: 1 to 2 hours per year — sweep, occasional re-level of any "rockers."

If you do not actually do the annual maintenance, plan on the surface failing at the low end of the lifespan range. Cedar decks that are not resealed rot within 8 years. Pavers that lose their jointing sand shift and pool water within 5.

## The bottom line

If your lot is flat and your budget is tight, a paver patio returns more outdoor living per dollar than any other option — and a poured concrete slab with a stone-look upgrade is the cheapest. If your house is elevated or your yard slopes, a composite deck (Trex Transcend or TimberTech) gives the lowest total cost of ownership over 25 years. Wood decks reward the homeowners who actually maintain them and punish those who do not.
