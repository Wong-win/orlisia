Walk into a backyard in 2026 and you are likely to find more dedicated cooking equipment than the apartment kitchens of a decade ago. The category has exploded. There is the primary grill — still, for most households, the workhorse — but flanking it you now see a flat-top griddle, a dedicated smoker, a wood-fired pizza oven, and increasingly a portable grill that follows the tailgate and the campsite. Each has its own fuel, its own learning curve, and its own price band. What follows is what each one actually does, what it costs, and where the brands earn their reputations.

## Gas grills: the everyday workhorse

For about 70 percent of U.S. households, the primary outdoor cooker is a propane gas grill. The reason is the same one that has kept gas dominant for 30 years: it lights, it heats, it cooks a burger in 8 minutes, and you do not have to think about it.

A mid-range 4-burner propane grill from Weber (the Genesis series, $900 to $1,300), Napoleon (the Rogue and Phantom series, $1,000 to $1,800), or Broil King (the Regal and Baron series, $900 to $1,500) is the practical sweet spot for most families. You get 400 to 600 square inches of primary cooking surface, a side burner, and porcelain-coated cast-iron grates that last 10-plus years. Construction is stainless or porcelain-enameled steel; burners are typically stainless or cast aluminum, the latter showing up on higher lines.

At the high end ($2,500 to $5,000), you get into the built-in segment — Lynx, Hestan, Twin Eagles, and the higher Napoleon Prestige models. These are heavier grills (200+ pounds), with sealed cabinets, infrared rotisserie burners, and lighting systems that survive 10 years of coastal weather. They are not three times better than a $1,200 Weber; they are incrementally better at very specific things (heat retention, evenness, longevity under heavy use).

The honest gas-grill lifespan is 8 to 15 years. Burners corrode or develop uneven flames around year 8 to 12, after which a $40 burner replacement kit (weber.com or napoleon.com sell direct) buys another 3 to 5 years.

The single biggest gas-grill mistake we see is undersizing. A grill that cooks four steaks in 12 minutes technically serves a family of four, but a family of four with guests over needs a grill that cooks eight steaks in 16 minutes. The Weber Genesis E-325 ($1,100) and Napoleon Rogue XT 425 ($1,200) are the practical mid-size picks. Anything smaller than 400 square inches of primary surface is a recurring source of frustration.

## Charcoal: the flavor-first alternative

A charcoal grill is more work than gas and produces notably better flavor — the difference is fat vaporizing onto hot coals, which does not happen on a gas burner. The brand lines fall in two tiers.

**Weber** dominates the kettle category. The iconic Weber Original Kettle ($140 for the 22-inch) is the default starter charcoal grill and remains the right answer for most buyers. The Master-Touch ($230) adds a hinged grate and ash catcher. Above it, the Performer ($370) adds a gas-fired igniter for starting — useful but optional.

**Kamado-style ceramic grills** are the second category. The Big Green Egg is the original and remains the volume leader at the high end. The BGE Large is $1,400 to $1,700 with nest and shelf; XL is $2,200 to $2,600. Kamado Joe (the Classic II at $1,500 and Classic III at $1,900) has chipped away at BGE market share with better gasket and divide-and-conquer grate systems. The Primo (the round, oval-shaped kamado from $1,600) and the Grill Dome and Saffire brands cover the rest of the field.

A charcoal kettle grill heats to 600°F and is great for direct grilling. A kamado — because of its thick ceramic walls — holds 700°F for hours and can drop to 225°F for overnight cooks, which is what makes them both a grill and a smoker in one box. We will come back to that.

The charcoal cooking process: 20 minutes to light, 30 to 45 minutes to reach stable temperature, then constant fire management. For weekend cooks who enjoy the process, charcoal is more satisfying than gas. For Tuesday-night hot dogs, gas wins.

A practical charcoal budget: 20 pounds of Kingsford Original briquettes at Home Depot runs $22, lasts 4 to 6 cooks. Hardwood lump (Better Wood, Royal Oak, Jealous Devil, $25 to $35 per 20 pounds) burns hotter, cleaner, and is what kamado owners prefer.

## Pellet grills: the set-it-and-forget-it category

Pellet grills are the newest major category. They burn compressed hardwood pellets fed by an auger into a fire pot, with a digital controller that holds temperature within 5°F of the set point — making them genuinely set-it-and-forget-it.

**Traeger** is the dominant brand, with the Pro series ($700 to $900), Ironwood ($1,200 to $1,500), and Timberline ($2,000+) covering most residential needs. The Timberline 850 at $2,200 is the volume pick for a serious backyard cook. Traeger's app connectivity, "WiFIRE" controller, and large hopper (24 pounds, giving 18 to 24 hours of cook time) are the benchmarks.

**Pit Boss** ($400 to $800) and **Z Grills** ($500 to $900) compete at lower prices. The build quality is noticeably below Traeger; the temperature accuracy is noticeably worse. For first-time pellet grill buyers on a $500 budget, the Pit Boss Sportsman 820 ($600) and the Z Grills 700E ($550) are reasonable starting points.

**Weber SmokeFire** ($700 to $1,000) is the gas-grill manufacturer's late entry; it has the Weber flavor-bar engineering pedigree but a smaller pellet hopper than competitors.

The honest pellet grill trade-off: they do low-and-slow (225°F for brisket) and roasting (chicken, pork shoulder) exceptionally well, but they struggle at high direct-heat searing. Most come with a "sear grate" accessory but max out around 500 to 550°F — fine for thin steaks, less effective on ribeyes. The pellet grill is best understood as a smoker-and-roaster with a grill attached, not the other way around.

For dedicated low-and-slow cooks at 225°F, expect $1 to $1.30 of pellets per hour of cooking (roughly $20 per 24-hour brisket). Compare to charcoal at $0.80 per hour of burning.

## Pizza ovens: countertop, freestanding, and the three brands that matter

The dedicated outdoor pizza oven is the category that has gone from a backyard curiosity to a mainstream purchase. The three brands that dominate are Ooni, Roccbox, and Gozney — each with a distinct take on the same basic box.

**Ooni** is the volume leader. The Ooni Karu 16 ($549) is the multi-fuel flagship — wood, charcoal, or optional gas burner, hitting 950°F in 15 minutes. The Ooni Koda 16 ($649) is gas-only and the right pick for someone who wants to start cooking in 15 minutes with no learning curve. The Ooni Volt 12 ($999) is the indoor-outdoor electric model, hitting 850°F without a fuel source. Ooni's smaller ovens (the Fyra 12 at $349, the Koda 12 at $499) are entry points but cook 10-inch pizzas only.

**Gozney** is the premium-position brand. The Gozney Roccbox ($599 for the base, $699 with the gas burner) is the original portable stone-deck pizza oven that helped create the category. The Gozney Dome ($1,299) is the freestanding flagship with a thick stone floor and dual-zone cooking, closer in feel to a real Italian pizza oven.

**Roccbox**, now owned by Gozney, is the same product line.

A pizza oven cooks a Neapolitan-style pizza in 60 to 90 seconds at 900°F+ — which means you have to learn to launch and turn pizzas every 15 seconds, or your toppings burn. The learning curve is real and steeper than any of these brands market it.

## Flat-top griddles: the category Blackstone built

Blackstone single-handedly created the outdoor flat-top category. A Blackstone 36-inch griddle ($400 to $500 with cabinet) is the right pick for a household that wants breakfast for six at once — pancakes, eggs, bacon, hash browns, all on a single 720-square-inch cold-rolled steel surface. The optional hardtop cover ($100) makes storage realistic.

The category has matured: **Weber Griddle** ($500 to $700) and **Cuisinart** ($400 to $600) offer legit alternatives with slightly better-built stands, wind-protected burners, and grease management systems. The Camp Chef Flat Top ($700) and the higher-end **Yoder** ($1,800 for the 36-inch) cover the prosumer and pro ends.

A griddle sears smash burgers, cooks Hibachi-style rice and vegetables, makes fajitas, and replaces a frying pan for almost anything you would cook on a Sunday morning. The downside is cleaning: after every cook, you scrape, oil, and wipe a hot surface. It is not labor-intensive, but it is non-negotiable.

## Dedicated smokers: offset, drum, and electric

If you want competition-style brisket at 225°F for 14 hours, a dedicated smoker outperforms even a great kamado. The category breaks three ways.

**Offset stick-burners** (Weber Smokey Mountain, Oklahoma Joe's, Lang, Yoder) burn wood or charcoal in a side firebox, with heat and smoke drawn across a separate cooking chamber. The Weber Smokey Mountain 22-inch at $500 to $600 is the volume pick — surprisingly capable for $500. Pitmasters move up to the Lang 36 or the Yoder Loaded Wichita at $4,000 to $6,000.

**Drum smokers** (Weber Smokey Mountain cousin the Gateway, $600 to $900) cook hot and fast with a stick burner inside a 55-gallon drum.

**Electric smokers** (Masterbuilt, $300 to $500) hold temperature but do not develop true smoke bark on brisket because they cannot run hot enough. They are the entry-level pick for someone who wants set-and-forget pulled pork and is not chasing competition.

## Accessories that actually earn their cost

Five accessories earn their space in the backyard without fail:

- **Instant-read thermometer** (Thermapen ONE, $110; ThermoPro TP19, $40). The single most impactful cooking upgrade any cook can buy — backyard or kitchen.
- **Grill grates** (GrillGrate, $120 for a set) for searing steaks on a gas grill that cannot otherwise hit 700°F+.
- **Pizza peel** ($25 to $60 for aluminum or wood, required with any pizza oven).
- **Grill light** ($25 to $50, magnetic mount) for evening cooks.
- **Smoke tube** ($30 to $50) for adding real smoke to a gas grill.

The grill cover ($30 to $80) is not optional if you want the grill to last more than 5 years outside.

## The bottom line

Pick one primary tool that matches how you cook most often — gas for weeknights, kamado or kettle for flavor-led cooks, pellet for set-and-forget smoking — and add a pizza oven or griddle only if you find yourself actually using the primary grill to its limit. The 12-piece backyard setup that gets used twice a year is a worse purchase than a great single grill that gets used twice a week.
