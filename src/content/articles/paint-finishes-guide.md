Paint sheen is the most consequential choice most homeowners make about paint and the least understood. The color is what people talk about, but the sheen is what determines whether the walls survive daily life, whether the room reads as soft or crisp, and whether every imperfection in the drywall is highlighted or hidden. Get the sheen wrong and the room looks off in ways that are hard to diagnose and expensive to fix.

Sheen — also called finish or gloss level — is the amount of light the paint film reflects once dry. Flat paints reflect almost nothing; high-gloss paints reflect almost everything. In between sit the workhorse finishes — matte, eggshell, satin, and semi-gloss — each with a specific job. This guide walks through the sheen spectrum, what each finish does, and where to use it in a real home.

## The Sheen Spectrum, Explained

Most major paint brands — Benjamin Moore, Sherwin-Williams, Farrow & Ball, Behr — use a similar scale of sheens, with names that vary slightly but correspond roughly to a measurable gloss level. At the bottom end is flat or matte, reflecting 0 to 5 percent of light. Eggshell reflects 10 to 25 percent; satin reflects 25 to 40 percent; semi-gloss reflects 40 to 65 percent; high-gloss reflects 65 to 85 percent or more.

The lower the sheen, the more forgiving the paint is of surface imperfections — roller marks, patches, uneven drywall mud — because the paint absorbs light and the eye cannot see what it cannot reflect. The higher the sheen, the more durable and washable the paint film is, because a smoother surface resists abrasion and stains.

The trade-off is built in: matte hides imperfections but cannot be scrubbed; gloss is bulletproof but reveals every flaw. The right sheen for any surface is the one that balances the need to hide against the need to clean.

## Matte and Flat: When You Want a Soft, Hushed Look

Flat and matte paints are the lowest sheens available. They reflect almost no light, which makes them ideal for ceilings and for adult rooms where the goal is depth and softness rather than durability. The pigment-rich surface absorbs light rather than bouncing it back, which is why matte walls make a room feel quieter and more relaxed — and why they are the standard for formal living rooms, dining rooms, and adult bedrooms.

The drawback is real: flat and matte paints are porous and cannot withstand scrubbing. They stain easily, and most marks can be touched up only by repainting the entire wall from corner to corner (a technique called "feathering out"). In a household with kids, pets, or cooking-heavy kitchens, matte is the wrong finish for the walls.

Modern matte formulations from Benjamin Moore (Aura Matte), Behr (Ultra Scuff Defense Matte), and Sherwin-Williams (Duration Home Matte) have closed the durability gap meaningfully. The new generation of matte paints holds up to wet scrubbing, repels stains, and tolerates spot cleaning. They are not as durable as eggshell or satin, but for many applications they are close enough that the softness wins the trade.

Use flat or matte on ceilings, in formal rooms, and on adult-bedroom walls where the goal is depth over durability. Use the new scuff-resistant matte formulations where you want the look of flat but the durability of eggshell.

## Eggshell and Satin: The Everyday Workhorses

Eggshell and satin are the workhorse sheens for residential walls. They reflect a small amount of light — enough to give the wall a soft, low-luster glow — but not so much that imperfections stand out. Both are washable, both are durable, and both are appropriate for most rooms in a typical home.

The distinction is mostly one of degree. Eggshell is the more matte of the two; satin has a slightly higher luster and is marginally more durable and washable. In practice, many homeowners and designers use the two interchangeably, choosing eggshell for a softer look and satin for rooms that see more traffic.

Eggshell is the right choice for living rooms, dining rooms, hallways, and adult bedrooms. The slight luster catches light from windows and fixtures in a way that flat paint does not, which is why a single coat of eggshell often looks more polished than two coats of flat. It hides minor drywall imperfections, it can be spot-cleaned, and most touch-ups blend invisibly.

Satin is the right choice for kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms, and children's bedrooms. The slightly higher sheen makes the surface genuinely scrubbable — crayon, grease, soap scum, and pencil marks can be removed with a damp cloth and a mild cleaner. In a kitchen, satin walls clean up after years of cooking splatter; in a bathroom, they hold up to humidity and the occasional splash.

For most homes, picking eggshell for living areas and satin for wet rooms or heavy-traffic zones covers every wall with the right balance of look and durability.

## Semi-Gloss and Gloss: Where Durability Wins

Semi-gloss and gloss are the highest sheens in residential paint. They reflect a lot of light, they form a hard, durable film, and they are the easiest paints to clean. They are also the least forgiving of surface imperfections — every drywall dip, every roller mark, every patch shows — and they read as visually louder than matte or eggshell finishes.

Semi-gloss is the standard finish for trim, doors, window casings, and cabinetry. It is also a strong choice for kitchens and bathrooms where washability matters more than look. The sheen contrast between a semi-gloss trim and an eggshell wall is a traditional design detail that defines a room's lines, and it has been used to that end since the 19th century.

Gloss and high-gloss finishes are reserved for specific applications: front doors, statement trim, banisters, and feature walls. A gloss-painted door reads as furniture-grade and is the easiest finish to clean. Gloss on a wall is dramatic and modern but requires a perfectly smooth substrate — every imperfection telegraphs. Most homeowners should not paint entire walls in gloss, but a gloss accent panel or a gloss door is a reliable design move.

In kitchens, semi-gloss on the cabinets has replaced painted wood as the default in upscale work for the past decade. A semi-gloss cabinet finish can be wiped down with soap and water, holds up to daily cooking, and looks crisp and contemporary. The trade-off is that the cabinets must be sprayed or rolled extremely carefully to avoid leaving roller texture in the finish.

## Specialty Finishes and How to Use Them

Beyond the standard sheen scale, a few specialty finishes are worth knowing.

**Washable matte** (also called scrubbable matte) is the new class of paint from Benjamin Moore, Behr, and Sherwin-Williams that combines the look of flat with the durability of eggshell. It is the right choice for family rooms, playrooms, and high-traffic hallways where flat looks better but eggshell is more practical.

**Spa finishes** are ultra-low-sheen, slightly textured paints designed to absorb sound and hide flaws on ceilings. Behr's Popcorn and Benjamin Moore's Waterborne Ceiling Paint are two examples. They are the right finish for ceilings with imperfect drywall, knockdown texture, or popcorn texture.

**Eggshell enamel** is a hybrid finish — the look of eggshell with the hardness of enamel. It is the standard for high-moisture rooms and for cabinets and trim where a softer sheen than semi-gloss is desired.

**Chalk paint** is a decorative finish with a soft, matte, slightly powdery appearance. It is typically used for furniture refinishing rather than walls, and it requires waxing or sealing to be washable. It is a specialty look, not an everyday wall finish.

**Limewash and mineral paint** are ultra-matte, breathable finishes for plaster, brick, and other masonry walls. They are popular for Mediterranean, Spanish, and modern farmhouse aesthetics. They are not scrubbable and they age visibly — that is part of the appeal, but it is worth knowing before committing.

## Testing Sheens Before Committing

The fastest way to mis-specify a sheen is to assume a paint chip tells the truth. It does not. The same color in matte, eggshell, and satin reads as three different colors in the same room — the sheen changes how much light the wall returns to the eye, and that shift can move a gray from warm to cool and a white from creamy to stark.

Before buying full gallons, sample two or three sheens on the same wall in 1-by-1-foot patches separated by a few inches. Look at them in daylight and again under the room's actual lighting — recessed downlights, pendants, sconces, whatever the room uses. Live with the samples for a day or two. The sheen that looks right at noon may look flat by evening, and the one that felt boring on the chip can be the most flattering once it covers the whole wall.

A second test: try to wipe the sample with a damp cloth. Most retailers leave sample boards out for exactly this reason. The finish that survives a quick scrub is the finish that will survive a year of life in the room.

## The bottom line

Paint sheen is a balance between how the wall looks and how it holds up. Use flat or matte on ceilings and in adult rooms where softness matters more than durability. Use eggshell for living areas and adult bedrooms; use satin for kitchens, bathrooms, and kids' rooms where scrubbability is a priority. Reserve semi-gloss for trim, doors, and cabinets. Reach for specialty finishes only when the standard scale does not solve the specific problem — high-traffic hallways that need washable matte, ceilings that need spa finish, accent panels that need gloss. Most walls in most homes are best served by eggshell or satin, and most trim by semi-gloss. Pick by room, not by color, and the room will look and perform the way it should.
