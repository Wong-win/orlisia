Gravel paths and patios look like the cheapest outdoor surface — until the gravel migrates into the lawn, the weeds colonize the center, and the path disappears into a rutted dirt track by year three. A path that still looks correct in year five and year ten is a function of three things: choosing the right gravel for the use case, building a real base underneath, and edging it on the sides so the gravel has nowhere to go. We break down the gravel types, the four edging materials that actually hold, the base-prep depths and weed-barrier choices, and the installed cost per square foot so the decision comes down to your climate and how much foot traffic the path will see.

## The four gravel types that matter for paths

A surprising amount of what gets sold as "gravel" is just one of four things, and the wrong choice in any of them makes the path a maintenance problem.

### Pea gravel

**Pea gravel** is the small, smooth, rounded stone about the size of a pea (3/8 inch nominal). It is the gravel most people picture when they imagine a garden path. It is also the gravel most likely to migrate, get stuck in shoe treads, and end up in the lawn.

Pea gravel cost: **$5 to $9 per square foot installed** (material + base prep), or roughly $80 to $130 per cubic yard of material alone. A cubic yard covers about 100 square feet at a 3-inch depth.

**Where it works**: low-traffic decorative paths, between raised beds, in cottage garden settings, around patios where the surface is mostly visual.

**Where it does not work**: a primary walking path between driveway and back door, a side yard used daily for trash cans, anywhere a heavy wheelbarrow or lawn mower rolls over it. The rounded shape means each stone moves independently under load.

### Decomposed granite (DG)

**Decomposed granite** is crushed granite (or sometimes crushed limestone) weathered into a coarse sand-and-fine-gravel mix that compacts hard when wet. It is the right surface for paths you will actually walk on regularly.

There are three sub-types:

- **Resin-stabilized DG** (also called "polymer DG" or "Gator Stone"): the fines are mixed with a binder that locks the surface hard. This is what you want for ADA-compliant paths and wheelchair access. Cost: **$7 to $12 per square foot installed**.
- **Decomposed granite with stabilizer added at install**: similar to above, mixed on-site. Cost: **$6 to $10 per square foot installed**.
- **Plain DG (unbound)**: the cheapest, but it tracks and rutted under heavy use. Cost: **$4 to $7 per square foot installed**.

DG requires installation in 2-inch lifts, each compacted with a 200-pound plate compactor (rentable from Home Depot for $90 to $120 per day) and lightly wetted. Done correctly, a DG path handles foot traffic and even light vehicle traffic without ruts.

### Crushed stone (a.k.a. crushed limestone, crushed granite, crushed miscellaneous base)

This is the angular, sharp-edged gravel used for driveways and base layers. The angular shape means each stone locks against its neighbors, making it almost immune to rutting. For paths, you use it as the **base** beneath a finer surface (pea gravel, DG, or pavers), not as the wearing surface itself — it is too rough to walk on barefoot and tough on lawn mower wheels.

**Crushed limestone #57** (3/4-inch angular crushed stone) is the standard base layer. **$3 to $5 per square foot** installed at 4-inch depth, or $35 to $55 per cubic yard for material alone.

### River rock

**River rock** is the smooth, rounded stone in 1.5-to-3-inch sizes, mostly decorative. Use it as ground cover around shrubs (with landscape fabric underneath to suppress weeds), not as a path surface — the rounded stones roll underfoot and the surface never stabilizes. Cost: **$5 to $8 per square foot installed** as ground cover.

The single most common mistake homeowners make with river rock is using it as a path surface. It looks great on day one and terrible by year two as the stones migrate and the surface becomes uneven.

## Edging that holds: the four options that actually work

Edging is the difference between a path that still looks correct in year five and a path that has spread 18 inches wider in each direction. The function of the edge is mechanical: it contains the gravel and resists the lateral spread from foot traffic and frost heave. Here are the four types we recommend.

### Metal landscape edging (steel or aluminum)

The cleanest, most modern edge. **Permaloc EnviroEdge** (steel, brown or black powder-coated), **Master Mark** (black plastic with steel spine), **Col-Met** (steel in 8-foot lengths), and **Versa-Lok** (brown anodized aluminum) are the brands contractors specify.

A 4-inch-deep steel edge set at grade with stakes every 16 inches holds pea gravel in place for a decade. Cost: **$4 to $7 per linear foot** for material, **$8 to $15 per linear foot** installed.

**Best for**: modern and contemporary homes, straight path runs, anywhere the edge needs to disappear visually.

### Cobblestone or brick edging

A traditional landscape edge: a soldier course of brick (Pine Hall, Glen-Gery) or a single row of Belgian block (cobblestone) set on a compacted base. Holds forever, looks correct on colonial and craftsman homes, and is forgiving of curves.

Cost: **$25 to $45 per linear foot installed** for brick, **$30 to $55 per linear foot** for cobblestone.

**Best for**: colonial, craftsman, English cottage, and historic properties.

### Stone edging (natural flagstone or quarried stone)

A dry-stacked flagstone edge or a quarried bluestone edge is the most expensive but most natural-looking. The wide variety of stone types (bluestone, limestone, slate, basalt) means you can match it to any climate.

Cost: **$30 to $55 per linear foot installed** for local flagstone, **$45 to $80 per linear foot** for imported bluestone.

**Best for**: naturalistic, Japanese-influenced, or mountain-lodge garden designs.

### Bender board (composite or wood)

A 1/2-inch-by-4-inch composite or redwood bender-board edge is the budget option. Use it for informal cottage paths; do not expect it to hold a long-term edge against pea gravel under heavy use. Cost: **$3 to $6 per linear foot** installed.

**Best for**: vegetable garden paths, temporary edging, low-budget decorative paths.

## Base prep: the layer that matters

The base is most of the work and most of the cost. Skipping it is why most gravel paths fail within 3 years.

### The standard base sequence for a path

1. **Mark the path.** Spray paint or rope on the ground. Width matters: a primary walking path should be 36 to 48 inches wide; a secondary or decorative path can be 24 to 30 inches.
2. **Excavate 6 to 9 inches** below final grade. For a primary walking path on a yard that drains reasonably well, 6 inches is enough. For clay soils with poor drainage, go 8 to 10 inches.
3. **Lay 4 to 6 inches of crushed stone base** (crushed limestone #57 or crushed miscellaneous base). Compact in 2-inch lifts with a plate compactor.
4. **Install the edging** along both sides of the path, with stakes or in concrete haunching.
5. **Lay landscape fabric** (4-to-5 oz non-woven, brands like **DeWitt Pro 5** or **Easy Gardener**). Use a woven slit-film fabric for vehicle traffic (DeWitt Pro 5, Tenax Geo).
6. **Lay 2 to 3 inches of the surface gravel** (pea gravel, DG, or crushed stone fines). Compact lightly.
7. **Top up seasonally.** A pea gravel path loses about 1/4 inch of material per year from foot traffic. Plan to top up every 2 to 3 years.

### Drainage considerations

A gravel path needs to drain, not just contain. The most common failure mode on a flat site is water pooling at the edges and migrating under the surface fabric, which carries fines down into the base layer and slowly clogs it.

For flat or low-lying sites, plan on a **1 to 2 percent cross-slope** (1/8 to 1/4 inch fall per foot across the path width) to shed water to one side. For very poorly drained sites, install a 4-inch perforated pipe (corrugated HDPE, $1.50 to $3 per linear foot) in a gravel envelope below the base layer to carry sub-surface water away.

For a path that runs adjacent to a foundation, slope the path **away** from the foundation at 2 percent or more (1/4 inch per foot). Water against a foundation wall from a poorly drained path is the source of more basement water problems than any other landscape feature.

### Weed control without chemicals

Landscape fabric is the base layer's defense against weeds, but it is not perfect. Weed seeds blow in on top of the fabric and germinate in the surface gravel layer.

The honest answer is that **no landscape fabric prevents all weeds**. The realistic maintenance is 15 to 30 minutes per 100 linear feet of path per month during the growing season, hand-pulling or hoeing the weeds that come through. If a path must be truly weed-free without maintenance, use pavers instead — pea gravel paths will always have some weeds.

The strongest non-chemical weed control layers in 2026 are:

- **Pre-emergent herbicides** (Preen, Concern Weed Prevention Plus, or generic prodiamine). Applied twice a year, in early spring and after fall cleanup, these prevent most weed seeds from germinating.
- **Boiling water or horticultural vinegar** for spot treatment. Effective on annual weeds but not perennial roots.
- **Layered cardboard under landscape fabric**, for the first 2 to 3 years while a path establishes.

## USDA zones and gravel path fit

The right gravel changes by climate.

**USDA zones 8 to 11** (most of Florida, southern Texas, Southern California, Phoenix): any gravel works, but pea gravel becomes uncomfortably hot for bare feet at noon in summer. Decomposed granite and crushed stone stay cooler to walk on. Avoid dark gravels (black lava, basalt) for paths in full sun — surface temperatures reach 130°F to 150°F in summer.

**USDA zones 5 to 7** (most of the continental U.S. Mid-Atlantic through Midwest, Pacific Northwest, Mountain West): the workhorse climate for gravel paths. Anything works, with decomposed granite or crushed stone being the lowest-maintenance choice.

**USDA zones 3 to 4** (upper Midwest, Mountain West, Northeast, northern New England): frost heave will move pea gravel and river rock more than crushed stone or DG. Edging must be set to **full frost depth** (typically 36 to 48 inches in zone 4) or pinned through the frost zone, otherwise the edge will lift over winter and the gravel will escape. Steel landscape edging works well; bender board does not.

## Cost per square foot summary (installed, mid-2026 U.S. pricing)

- **Pea gravel path**: $5 to $9 per sq ft
- **Decomposed granite (unbound)**: $4 to $7 per sq ft
- **Decomposed granite (stabilized)**: $7 to $12 per sq ft
- **Resin-bound DG (SurePave, Gator Stone)**: $9 to $14 per sq ft
- **Crushed limestone #57 base only**: $3 to $5 per sq ft
- **River rock (ground cover, not path surface)**: $5 to $8 per sq ft

Costs assume a 4-foot-wide straight path, 6-inch excavation, 4-inch base, and any of the four edging options. Add 30 to 50 percent for curved paths, narrow working conditions, or haul-in access that requires a wheelbarrow more than 50 feet.

## Brand picks by material

- **Base stone**: any local quarry's crushed miscellaneous base (CMB) or #57 limestone. For coastal California: **Vulcan Materials** or local quarry aggregate. For Northwest: **Cascade Geology** or local quarry.
- **Pea gravel**: 3/8-inch washed river pea from **Vigoro** (bagged retail) or any local landscape yard in bulk (cheaper per cubic yard).
- **Decomposed granite**: **Humboldt Earthbag**, **Vigoro DG**, or any regional quarry — every region has a domestic DG source.
- **Landscape fabric**: **DeWitt Pro 5** non-woven, **Easy Gardener Landscape Fabric**, **Tenax**. Avoid the cheap 1.5-to-2 oz non-woven from big-box stores — it tears within 2 years.
- **Edging**: **Permaloc**, **Master Mark**, **Col-Met**, **Suncast** (for residential low-end).
- **Compaction**: rented plate compactor (**Wacker DPU 4045Ye** is the pro standard), or a hand tamper for very small paths.

## The bottom line

For a primary walking path between driveway and back door, decomposed granite with stabilizer (or resin-bound DG on top of a 4-inch crushed-stone base) gives the lowest maintenance and the most stable surface over 10 years at $7 to $12 per square foot installed. For a decorative path between raised beds or a cottage-garden walkway, pea gravel with steel landscape edging is the cheapest correct answer. The base prep — 4 to 6 inches of compacted crushed stone and a 4-to-5 oz landscape fabric — matters more than the surface material you choose on top of it.
