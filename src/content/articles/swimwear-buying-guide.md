A working swimwear kit is one of the cheapest and most overlooked parts of an adult wardrobe. Most people own two or three swimsuits in various states of disintegration: an elastic-strangled bikini bought on vacation in 2019, a tank that lost its shape after one summer, a "nice" one-piece reserved for occasions that never come. The category is small enough that getting it right is doable in a single shopping trip. The category is also forgiving enough that mistakes do not cost much. We think the right approach is to own two or three suits that actually fit, in styles that work for your body and your actual swim life, and stop buying anything else.

This guide covers the working decisions — one-piece versus bikini, the fabric that holds up, fit by body type, and the brand picks at each price point. The numbers reflect what you will pay in real checkout from each brand's website.

## The swimwear mindset

The single biggest mistake with swimwear is buying for a fantasy life — the European beach vacation you take once a decade, the cocktail party by the pool you attend once in five years. Buy for the swim you actually do. For most adults that means a backyard pool, a community club, a hotel on a family trip, and the occasional beach weekend. The kit you need is small and practical.

The second mistake is treating swimwear as disposable. A $48 bikini from a fast-fashion site costs the same as a $98 bikini from a brand that uses recycled fabric and reinforced stitching, but lasts one-tenth as long. The math does not favor cheap.

The third mistake is buying for size now rather than fit. Swim fabric stretches under its own weight when wet. A suit that is comfortable standing up may be sheer and unsupported in the water. Buy snug, not comfortable.

## One-piece vs bikini: an honest comparison

The choice between one-piece and bikini is largely aesthetic, but there are practical differences worth understanding.

A one-piece swimsuit covers more, supports more, and stays in place better in the water. It is the right pick for lap swimming, water parks, beach volleyball, and any activity that involves moving in the water. It is also the right pick for anyone who wants more coverage or who finds bikini bottoms uncomfortable. The downsides are limited tan lines and the difficulty of going to the bathroom in a one-piece (this matters more than you think on a family beach day).

A bikini separates are easier to wear, easier to mix-and-match, and better for sun exposure. A high-waisted bikini with a supportive band is the modern default. The downsides are staying in place during actual swimming, the need to match tops and bottoms if you buy sets, and the fact that a string-tie bikini is genuinely uncomfortable for anyone with a real bust.

The honest answer: most adults benefit from owning one of each. A one-piece for active swimming and the days you want more coverage. A bikini for sunbathing and casual pool time. Two pieces, plus a cover-up and a hat, is a complete summer kit.

## The fabric that holds up

Swimwear fabric is the single most important variable in how long a suit lasts. The category is dominated by four materials, and only two of them are worth buying.

Recycled nylon (often labeled ECONYL) is the new standard. Nylon is the most durable, soft, and shape-retentive of the swim fabrics. Recycled nylon, made from discarded fishing nets and other waste, performs identically to virgin nylon and has a meaningfully lower footprint. Most premium swim brands have moved to recycled nylon as default. The price difference is minimal.

Spandex (also called elastane or Lycra) is what gives swim fabric its stretch and recovery. Quality swim fabric is a blend of 80 to 90 percent nylon and 10 to 20 percent spandex. Anything below 10 percent spandex feels restrictive. Anything above 20 percent feels too stretchy and breaks down faster.

Polyester is the cheaper, less comfortable alternative. It does not hold dye as well, fades faster in chlorine and sun, and feels rougher on the skin. The cheaper fast-fashion swimsuits are polyester. Avoid for anything you intend to wear more than twice.

Cotton-blend swim fabric is a non-starter. Cotton absorbs water, loses shape, takes forever to dry, and fades in chlorine. The only legitimate use is a cover-up.

A reliable suit is 78 to 88 percent recycled nylon and 12 to 22 percent spandex, with reinforced stitching at the straps, seams, and stress points. If the label does not specify the fabric blend, the suit is probably not worth buying.

## Fit by body type

The honest truth about fit is that body type matters less than proportions and personal preference. That said, a few patterns are useful to know.

For a smaller bust, a bikini top with structure — underwire, molded cups, or a halter — will look better than a bandeau or triangle. The Andie one-piece at $118 and the Summersalt one-piece at $98 are made for support. Avoided straps.

For a larger bust, look for wide straps, full lining, and a structured top. The Cupshe one-piece at $48 and the Beach Riot top at $78 are the right picks. Avoided triangle tops and string-ties.

For a longer torso, look for one-pieces labeled "tall" or with adjustable straps. Most standard one-pieces will pull down at the shoulders. The Summersalt Sidestroke at $98 and the Andie one-piece at $118 both have adjustable straps and longer torsos.

For a shorter torso, look for high-cut legs and cropped one-pieces. The Aerie off-the-shoulder one-piece at $54 and the Triangl bikini at $78 (the high-waisted cut) are the working options.

For curvier hips and a narrower waist, look for high-waisted bikini bottoms with adjustable side ties. Avoided boy shorts and low-rise cuts.

For an athletic build with less defined waist, a one-piece with ruching or a belt detail will create shape. The Beach Riot Kassia at $92 is a notable option.

These are starting points, not rules. The single most useful thing you can do is try on actual swimwear in a real fitting room before buying online. Most brands offer free returns, and the few dollars you save by buying online do not outweigh the cost of a suit you never put on.

## The brands worth buying

Five brands have earned a place in the swimwear kit at different price points and aesthetics. There are many more, but these are the ones we have seen hold up.

Andie is the New York-based brand that built its reputation on the "real-women" fit model. Their one-piece at $118, their high-waisted bikini at $60 per piece, and their "Amalfi" suit at $148 are the working staples. The fabric is recycled nylon with 20 percent spandex. The cuts are designed for a real range of busts, torsos, and hips. Sizing is detailed and honest. This is the right brand for the everyday kit.

Summersalt is the direct-to-consumer brand that disrupted the category with its inclusive fit system. Their Sidestroke one-piece at $98 and their "Cabana" bikini at $48 per piece are the recognizable pieces. The price-to-quality ratio is the best in the category. Sizing is more inclusive than most. The look is sporty and grown-up.

Beach Riot is the California brand for fashion-forward swimwear. Their Kassia one-piece at $92 and their Cali bikini at $74 are the recognizable pieces. The cuts are trendier, the prints are bolder, the price is mid-range. Worth visiting if you want a suit that is more interesting than a solid.

Triangl is the Australian brand known for its neoprene bikini and distinctive color-blocking. Their standard bikini at $78 and their "Mimi" set at $98 are the working pieces. Neoprene holds its shape beautifully and the look is recognizable. The downsides are the long shipping from Australia and the limited size range.

Aerie is the right entry-level brand. Their swim line starts at $42 for bikini separates and $54 for one-pieces. The fabric is decent (recycled nylon blend), the fit is improving, and the price is accessible. This is the right brand for the first or second suit, or for anyone whose size or style preferences are still settling.

For luxury, add Eres (Parisian, $200 to $600, exceptional fabric and fit) and Lisa Marie Fernandez ($250 to $700, fashion-forward, structured) to the list. For surf and active swim, add Billabong, Roxy, and Seea at the $60 to $130 range.

## Building the kit on a real budget

A working swim kit fits in a small drawer and costs $200 to $500 depending on brand and how many pieces you buy.

The minimum kit is one well-fitting one-piece and one bikini (top and bottom). The recommended brands above at the mid-tier will run $200 to $300 total. The kit covers 95 percent of swim scenarios.

The expanded kit adds a second one-piece in a darker color (chlorine hides ink well) and a second bikini top in a different style. The expanded kit covers 100 percent of scenarios and runs $350 to $500.

The cover-up and hat are separate categories but worth mentioning. A $40 to $90 linen or cotton cover-up from Madewell, J.Crew, or Amazon's Quince line covers the between-water moments. A sun hat at $30 to $80 covers the rest.

## Care and maintenance

Rinse every suit in cold water after each wear. Roll in a dry towel to remove excess water. Hang dry in shade — UV degrades spandex faster than anything else. Wash in cold water with a gentle detergent every three to four wears. Avoid hot tubs, which destroy spandex in a single session. Replace any suit that loses shape, gets a hole, or becomes sheer at the seat. That last sign means the fabric is gone, not stretched.

## Common mistakes

Buying for the beach vacation, not the real swim life. Most swim days are pool days. The suit that works at the community pool works at the beach.

Buying the wrong size. Snug now means comfortable later. Stretchy in the fitting room means sheer in the water.

Wringing out wet suits. Wringing breaks the spandex fibers. Roll in a towel, do not squeeze.

Storing wet suits in a plastic bag. Trapped moisture breeds bacteria and degrades elastic. Hang or lay flat to dry first.

## The bottom line

A working swim kit is two suits, fits in a drawer, and costs $200 to $500. Buy recycled nylon with at least 15 percent spandex, in styles that actually fit your body, from brands that use reinforced stitching. Andie, Summersalt, Beach Riot, Triangl, and Aerie are the brands worth knowing at the mid-tier. Replace it when the fabric gives out — usually after two to three summers of regular use. The point of the kit is to wear a suit you feel good in without thinking about it.
