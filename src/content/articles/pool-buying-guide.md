A swimming pool is one of the most expensive things you can put in a backyard that does not increase the appraised value of the house by anywhere close to what it costs. Appraisers give back roughly 50 to 70 percent of an install cost in added home value in markets where pools are common, and closer to 25 to 40 percent in markets where they are not. The reason families still buy them is not financial. It is the daily texture of having one: the kid who swims after dinner, the friend who comes over on a Saturday, the four months out of the year when the backyard is genuinely the most-used room of the house.

What follows is how to choose between the five major categories — above-ground, semi-inground, vinyl liner in-ground, fiberglass in-ground, and gunite/concrete in-ground — what each costs in 2026, how they fail, and what maintenance actually looks like across the first decade.

## Above-ground pools: the practical starter

An above-ground pool is the lowest-commitment way to put a body of water in a backyard. The Intex and Bestway soft-sided frame pools, the Intex Ultra Frame and Prism Frame series, get you 12-foot to 24-foot round pools at $400 to $1,800 delivered. They install in a weekend, hold 4,000 to 14,000 gallons of water, and last 5 to 10 years before the liner loses elasticity.

The semi-permanent above-ground category — with steel or resin walls, real vinyl liners, and 48-inch to 54-inch walls — runs $2,500 to $6,500 for the pool kit plus $500 to $1,500 for install. Brands to know here: **Intex** (the Prismatic Frame series in larger sizes), **Bestway** (the Power Steel and Hydrium lines), **Doheny's** for steel-walled above-grounds, **Wilbar** (maker of most of the entry-level kits sold under different labels), and **Aqualeader** for the resin-walled category.

A semi-inground above-ground pool (installed partially in the ground, partially above) is the same kit but with the lower course buried. Most towns allow up to 24 to 36 inches of burial with a permit but not a full in-ground install. A semi-inground install runs $4,000 to $9,000 including excavation, with cost savings vs. in-ground at the finish (no concrete deck required).

The maintenance reality: above-ground pumps, filters, and accessories are smaller and cheaper. Expect $20 to $40 in chemicals per month during swim season (May through September in most of the country), plus electricity for the pump at $25 to $40 per month. Total operating cost for an above-ground pool realistically runs $500 to $900 per swim season.

Above-ground pool failure modes: the liner goes first (5 to 10 years), then the pump (5 to 8 years), then the filter. None of these are catastrophic repairs. A replacement liner from LinerWorld or InTheSwim runs $250 to $700 for most sizes.

## Vinyl liner in-ground pools: the volume pick

A vinyl liner in-ground pool is what most U.S. middle-class families end up with. The structure is steel, polymer, or aluminum wall panels bolted together on a concrete footer, with a custom vinyl liner dropped in. Water volume and shape are essentially unlimited, with most installs falling in the 12×24 to 18×36 rectangle range.

A vinyl liner in-ground pool realistically runs $55,000 to $85,000 installed in most markets. The biggest variables are excavation difficulty (rocky soil adds $5,000 to $15,000 to the cost), deck material (concrete is fine, pavers add $8,000 to $15,000), and the equipment package (basic pump/filter at $2,000, upgraded variable-speed pump and saltwater at $5,000 to $8,000).

The brands you will talk to: **Anthony & Sylvan** (the largest U.S. installer, east-of-Mississippi-heavy), **Blue Haven** (the second-largest national installer), **Shasta Pools** (southwest regional leader), **Riley's Above Ground** and regional builders for smaller projects. Each region has 2 to 5 dominant local builders that handle 80 percent of installs.

The honest strengths of vinyl liner pools: lowest up-front cost among in-grounds, fastest install (2 to 4 weeks vs. 8 to 12 for gunite), soft surface that is comfortable to walk on, easily customizable shape and depth. The honest weaknesses: the liner requires replacement every 8 to 12 years at $4,500 to $7,500 for the liner and labor. The walls can corrode if your soil has high iron or unusual chemistry — a coastal builder will know your area. The resale question is real: vinyl liner pools in some markets sell for less than gunite pools because some buyers treat them as a $6,000 deferred maintenance issue.

## Fiberglass in-ground pools: the rising category

Fiberglass pools are one-piece shells manufactured in a factory (San Juan, Viking, Latham, Leisure Pools, River Pools are the major brands), delivered on a flatbed truck, and lowered into an excavated hole. The install takes 3 to 5 days versus 3 to 8 weeks for vinyl liner.

A fiberglass in-ground install runs $65,000 to $105,000 in most markets, depending on access (truck delivery requires a 14-foot wide access route for the larger shells), excavator work, and the deck.

The honest strengths of fiberglass: very fast install, smooth surface (no liner maintenance, no resurfacing), lower chemical use (the gelcoat does not harbor algae the way plaster does), and salt-water compatible without modification. Latham's 25-year structural warranty on most shells is the market leader.

The honest weaknesses: limited shapes and sizes (manufacturers offer 80 to 150 molds per brand, not unlimited custom geometry), the shell cannot be modified after install, and the high water table problem — a fiberglass shell in a high water table area can float out of the ground if the pool is drained for service. Most builders address this with a sump pump at install but it is a real consideration in Florida, the Gulf Coast, and river-lot properties.

The Rio, San Juan, and Viking fiberglass pools in the 12×26 to 14×30 range are the volume picks. The 14×30 San Juan model with tanning ledge and built-in spa section runs about $55,000 to $80,000 installed in the South.

## Gunite and concrete pools: the custom option

Gunite (or shotcrete) pools are the fully custom category: pneumatically sprayed concrete over a steel rebar framework, hand-finished and tiled or plastered. They are how you get the lazy-L shape, the beach entry, the infinity edge, the depth transitions, the attached spa, or any other geometry.

A gunite pool realistically runs $80,000 to $140,000 installed for a basic 14×28 shape, and $150,000 to $400,000 for high-end custom designs with built-in spas, water features, automatic covers, and significant hardscape. The range is wide because the labor and finish costs scale sharply with custom geometry.

The brands: there is no national gunite install brand. Local custom pool builders dominate, with the regional leaders being **Thursday Pools** (gunite in the Southeast), **Gib-San Pools** in Ontario, **California Pools and Landscape** in southern California, and **Southwest Pools** in Texas. Pick a builder by reputation and portfolio, not brand.

The honest strengths: any shape, any depth, any tile or plaster finish; tile or quartz finish outlasts plaster but costs more; salt water compatible; the highest resale multiplier in most markets (50 to 70 percent of install cost in coastal and warm-climate markets).

The honest weaknesses: 8 to 12 week install time; pool plaster needs resurfacing every 10 to 15 years at $7,000 to $12,000; higher chemical use than fiberglass because plaster is reactive; tile line problems after 20 years; and the cost — gunite is the most expensive category without an offsetting lifespan advantage.

## Equipment that goes with any pool

Beyond the pool itself, three categories of equipment make or break daily usability.

**Pumps and motors**. A variable-speed pump (Pentair IntelliFlo, Hayward TriStar VS, $1,500 to $2,500 installed) saves $500 to $800 per year in electricity over a single-speed pump. If you are spending $50,000-plus on a pool, the upgrade is worth it.

**Sanitation**. Salt systems (Pentair IntelliChlor, Hayward AquaRite, $1,200 to $2,000 installed) have become the default for any pool where the homeowner does not want to handle chlorine. They convert salt to chlorine constantly, require less hands-on management, and produce noticeably softer-feeling water.

**Covers**. An automatic pool cover (Cover-Pools or Pool Cover Specialists, $14,000 to $22,000 installed) is the single best safety addition — it also retains heat and reduces evaporation. Solar cover reels (Solar Sun Rings, 12-mil bubble covers, $200 to $600) are the cheap season add-on.

## Real costs across the first decade

The sticker price is not what a pool costs. A realistic 10-year model for a $75,000 vinyl liner in-ground pool in the mid-Atlantic:

- Install: $75,000
- Liner replacement at year 10: $6,500
- Variable-speed pump at year 5: $1,800
- Salt system at year 5: $1,500
- Heater (a gas heater) at year 5: $3,500
- Electricity: $450 to $700 per year for 10 years = $4,500 to $7,000
- Chemicals: $400 to $700 per year for 10 years = $4,000 to $7,000
- Water refill and disposal: $300 to $500 per year = $3,000 to $5,000
- Annual service (pool service, $150 to $300 per month during season): $10,000 to $20,000 across 10 years if outsourced

A 10-year total of $112,000 to $128,000 on a $75,000 install is a realistic expectation. The same pool, owned by a family that does its own cleaning and chemistry, runs $90,000 to $100,000 across the decade.

## Size, siting, and what gets used

The most common pool regret we hear is "we made it too small." A 14×28 rectangular pool that is 3.5 to 6 feet deep holds enough water for 4 to 6 swimmers in the surface area, but in practice it feels like two lanes of a highway during a family party. A 16×32 or 18×36 with a defined shallow end (under 4 feet) and a deeper area for diving feels roomy.

Pool siting considerations: sun exposure (full sun is preferable, shade is a problem — colder water, more debris), drainage away from the house, distance from the property line (typically 8 to 10 feet setback required), distance from septic systems (typically 25 feet minimum), and tree placement (no deciduous trees within 25 feet if you can avoid it).

The second most common regret: not enough deck. Twelve feet of pool deck on at least one side is enough for chairs, a small table, and walking around. Forty-foot pools with eight-foot decks feel cramped within a year.

## Safety and code

A pool in nearly every jurisdiction in the U.S. and Canada requires a barrier — a fence at least 4 feet tall with self-closing gates, or an ASTM-compliant pool cover. Self-latching gates are mandated in most states. Some states additionally require alarms on doors facing the pool, and homes with children under 6 benefit from pool alarms ($200 to $500 installed).

Drowning is the leading cause of unintentional death for children under 5 in the U.S. Layered protection — fence, self-latching gate, pool alarm, swim lessons — is what saves lives. A pool cover alone is not enough.

## The bottom line

For a first-time pool buyer who wants year-over-year swimming without a 6-figure renovation, a semi-inground or vinyl liner in-ground pool in the 14×28 to 16×32 range is the practical default. For low-maintenance water in a warmer climate, fiberglass pays back its higher install cost in 7 to 10 years via lower chemical and resurfacing expense. Gunite is the choice when the pool is part of a designed outdoor room, not just a body of water.
