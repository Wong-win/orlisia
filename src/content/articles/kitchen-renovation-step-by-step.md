A kitchen renovation is the most expensive and most disruptive project most homeowners will undertake. The national median for a mid-range kitchen remodel in the United States crossed $80,000 in 2025 according to Remodeling Magazine, and upscale jobs routinely exceed $150,000. Half of that cost is labor, and roughly a third is cabinetry and countertops. The remainder goes to appliances, flooring, lighting, and the inevitable change orders.

The single biggest factor in whether a kitchen renovation comes in on budget and on schedule is the planning that happens before any wall is opened. We surveyed five general contractors and three kitchen designers on what determines a successful project. Their answers were unanimous: a clear brief, a fixed scope, a realistic timeline, and a contractor who has done your exact job at least a dozen times.

This guide walks through the five phases of a kitchen renovation — from the brief to the punch list — with the questions to ask and the decisions to lock down before each phase begins.

## Phase 1: Define the Brief and the Budget

Before you call a contractor or visit a showroom, write down how the kitchen is supposed to perform. Not what it should look like — what it needs to do. The brief drives every later decision, and it is the document you will measure scope changes against when a contractor proposes a $4,000 change order.

Start with the users. How many people cook at once? Does anyone bake? Do you entertain in the kitchen, or use the dining room for that? Are there mobility needs — aging parents, a wheelchair user, a tall cook who needs higher counters? Does anyone in the household have allergies, dietary patterns, or food-storage preferences that affect the layout?

Next, inventory the pain points of the current kitchen. Common complaints include: too few counters, a sink that is too small, a range that is poorly vented, insufficient task lighting, no place for the trash, no pantry, awkward traffic flow between the cook and the rest of the room. Rank these in order of severity. The top three to five pain points become the design priorities; everything else is bonus.

Then set a budget — a real one, not a wishful one. A useful rule of thumb: kitchens should run 6 to 15 percent of the home's current value for a mid-range remodel, and 15 to 30 percent for an upscale job. Within the budget, expect cabinetry to take 30 to 35 percent, countertops 10 to 15 percent, appliances 10 to 15 percent, labor 30 to 35 percent, and the rest for flooring, lighting, plumbing, electrical, permits, and contingency. A 10 to 15 percent contingency is not optional — something will surface during demolition, and the contingency is what keeps the project moving when it does.

## Phase 2: The Layout Triangle and Work Zones

The kitchen work triangle — the path between the refrigerator, the sink, and the cooktop — is the oldest design heuristic in residential kitchens. It still holds. The sum of the three legs should fall between 12 and 26 feet, with no single leg shorter than 4 feet or longer than 9 feet. The triangle prevents the cook from being more than a step or two from the three stations that handle 90 percent of kitchen work.

In larger kitchens, the work triangle has been supplemented by the work-zone model: a kitchen is divided into five zones — cooking, prep, cleanup, storage, and serving — and each zone is laid out so the tools and surfaces for that zone are close together. The prep zone, for example, should sit between the sink and the cooktop with at least 36 inches of counter on either side for landing space.

For most homes, the layout is one of six:

- **U-shape** — three walls of cabinets, ideal for one cook with frequent help
- **L-shape** — two perpendicular runs, the most flexible
- **Galley** — two parallel runs, efficient for narrow rooms
- **Single-wall** — everything on one wall, best in studios and ADUs
- **Peninsula** — an L-shape with a third leg ending in a partial wall, good for open-plan homes
- **Island** — an L- or U-shape with a freestanding island, the most popular upscale layout

Whatever the layout, walking paths through the kitchen should be at least 36 to 42 inches wide. A kitchen that funnels traffic through the work zone will frustrate every member of the household.

## Phase 3: Cabinets, Counters, and Hardware

Cabinetry is the most expensive line item and the easiest to over-spec. The first decision is stock, semi-custom, or custom. Stock cabinets (from IKEA, Home Depot's Hampton Bay, KraftMaid's value line) ship in fixed sizes and finishes; they are the cheapest and the fastest to install. Semi-custom (KraftMaid, Diamond, Decora) lets you modify dimensions by an inch or two and offers more finishes. Custom (Crystal Cabinetry, Plain & Fancy, Most Everything Custom) is built to your exact dimensions and offers unlimited finish options.

For most renovations, semi-custom is the sweet spot: more flexibility than stock, 30 to 50 percent cheaper than full custom, and quick enough to ship in 4 to 6 weeks. Custom is worth it only for unusually shaped rooms or specific aesthetic goals that stock and semi-custom cannot meet.

Box construction matters. Plywood boxes with solid wood face frames hold screws and adjust better than MDF or particleboard. Drawer boxes should be dovetailed wood or metal-sided; stapled particleboard drawer bottoms will fail. Soft-close hinges and full-extension drawer slides are now standard on most semi-custom lines and worth specifying if they are not included.

Countertops are the second-biggest line item. Quartz (engineered stone, brands like Caesarstone, Cambria, Silestone) is the most popular choice for good reason: it is durable, non-porous, stain-resistant, and consistent in color. Natural stone (granite, marble, soapstone) gives more visual variation but requires sealing and is more prone to staining and etching. Solid surface (Corian) is repairable and seamless but scratches more easily. Butcher block and other wood counters are warm and forgiving but need oiling. For most households, quartz is the practical default.

Hardware — pulls and knobs — is the cheapest line item and the easiest place to add personality. Brushed brass, polished nickel, matte black, and unlacquered brass are the most common finish families in 2026; pick one and use it consistently on cabinetry, plumbing, and lighting where possible.

## Phase 4: Appliances, Plumbing, and Lighting

Appliances should be selected before the cabinets are ordered, not after. The cabinet drawings depend on exact appliance dimensions, ventilation requirements, and electrical locations. Refrigerators come in counter-depth (24 to 25 inches deep, flush with the cabinet face) and standard-depth (30 to 36 inches deep, more capacity). Ranges come in 30-, 36-, and 48-inch widths; some are slide-in (designed to sit flush with the countertop) and some are freestanding. Cooktops and wall ovens split into separate units give more design flexibility but require careful electrical planning.

For ventilation, a properly sized range hood is the single most important appliance for indoor air quality. A hood should move at least 100 CFM per linear foot of the cooktop (300 CFM for a 30-inch range, 400 CFM for a 36-inch). Ducted hoods — vented to the outside — are dramatically more effective than recirculating hoods and worth the additional ductwork cost if at all possible.

Plumbing rough-ins should be planned at the same time as the layout. Moving a sink more than a few feet from its existing supply and drain lines is expensive; keeping the sink in roughly the same spot saves several thousand dollars. If the kitchen is moving to an island, plan the plumbing and electrical runs before drywall goes up.

Lighting deserves its own plan. A well-lit kitchen has three layers: ambient (recessed downlights or a flush mount), task (under-cabinet LED strips illuminating the full length of every upper cabinet run), and accent (pendants over the island, a fixture over the sink). Plan for a dimmer on every layer. Use 3000K or 3500K color temperature for warm-but-clean light, and CRI 90+ for accurate color rendering of food.

## Phase 5: The Build, the Punch List, and What to Watch For

Once the design is finalized, the cabinets are ordered, and the permits are pulled, the build can begin. A typical mid-range kitchen renovation takes 8 to 14 weeks of construction, with the breakdown roughly:

- Demolition: 1 to 3 days
- Rough framing, plumbing, electrical, HVAC: 1 to 2 weeks
- Inspections (rough): 3 to 7 days
- Drywall, flooring, tile: 1 to 2 weeks
- Cabinet installation: 2 to 5 days
- Countertop template and install: 1 to 2 weeks (template is often 7 to 10 days after cabinets)
- Appliance installation: 1 to 3 days
- Paint touch-up, backsplash, hardware: 1 to 2 weeks
- Final inspections and punch list: 1 week

Two schedule risks dominate. First, countertop template and fabrication: if the cabinets are not perfectly level, the countertop installer will not template, and the schedule slips a week or more. Verify cabinet level before template day. Second, appliance delivery: many appliances ship on 6- to 12-week lead times. Order them at the same time as the cabinets so they arrive when needed.

The punch list is the document you walk through with the contractor in the final week. It should include every cosmetic defect, every misaligned door, every missing pull, every drippy faucet, every outlet that does not work. Walk the kitchen in daylight and again at night. Note everything in writing; do not rely on verbal promises. Most contractors will fix punch list items within 2 weeks of project completion as part of the contract.

## The bottom line

A kitchen renovation succeeds or fails in planning. Lock the brief, budget, layout, cabinet drawings, appliance specifications, and lighting plan before any demolition. Keep the work triangle and walking paths realistic. Order cabinets and appliances on the same timeline, and never schedule the countertop template before the cabinets are level. Hold a 10 to 15 percent contingency for surprises, walk the punch list in daylight and at night, and the kitchen that opens up six months from now will be the one you planned, not the one you settled for.
