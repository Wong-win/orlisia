A standing desk is one of the rare work-from-home upgrades that pays back in the first week. Studies have shown that alternating between sitting and standing reduces back pain, and most users report they concentrate better when they have the option to stand for part of the day. We're not going to oversell the health claims — the research is mixed, and "standing all day" is its own problem — but the consensus is clear: the ability to move matters, and the way you get that ability is a height-adjustable desk.

The hard part is that the standing-desk market is enormous and confusing. You can spend $200 at IKEA or $2,400 at Steelcase, and somewhere in between sit a dozen DTC brands you've probably seen on Instagram. The question isn't "what's the best desk" — it's "what desk fits your body, your monitor setup, and the way you actually work."

## Electric vs. manual: the motor question

Manual standing desks (sometimes called hand-crank or crank desks) use a hand-operated mechanism to raise and lower the surface. The IKEA Bekant ($320) and IKEA Skarsta ($190) are the most common examples. Crank desks are cheaper, quieter, and have no electronics to fail. They're also slow — 10-15 full revolutions to go from sitting to standing height — which means in practice you don't move as often as you'd like. Crank desks work if you're disciplined about it; they don't work if you find the crank annoying and end up sitting all day anyway.

Electric standing desks use one or two motors to lift the surface at the push of a button. Most take 10-20 seconds to go from sitting to standing, and the better ones have memory presets so one button takes you to the height you saved. Electric desks cost $300-700 for the consumer market, $1,000-2,500 for commercial-grade.

Single-motor desks are cheaper but slower and have lower weight capacity (typically 150 lbs). Dual-motor desks are faster, smoother, and handle 250-350 lbs. For most users with a single monitor and a laptop, a single-motor desk is fine. If you're running dual 27-inch monitors plus a laptop dock plus a heavy desk surface, go dual-motor.

## Frame vs. full desk: what you're buying

A standing-desk "frame" is just the legs, motors, and crossbar — the metal skeleton that lifts. You add your own tabletop. A "full desk" includes the frame plus a pre-cut surface.

The frame-only market is bigger than you'd think. Jarvis by Fully (now part of Herman Miller), Uplift V2, and Flexispot's E7 all sell frames separately. Frames start around $280 and go up to $700 for the heaviest commercial units. The advantage of buying a frame is that you pick your own surface — a butcher block from a local lumber yard, a 72-inch solid walnut slab, or whatever fits your aesthetic. The disadvantage is that you have to assemble a desktop, drill holes for the frame mounting screws, and get the depth and width right.

A full desk (frame + top) is faster to set up and you know the dimensions are correct. Uplift's V2 Commercial ($879) ships as a single unit with a 1-inch bamboo top. Fully's Jarvis ($549 with a 60×30-inch top) is the consumer classic. IKEA's Bekant and Skarsta are full desks in flat-pack form.

If you want a one-box solution, get a full desk. If you want a specific surface or you're matching an existing room, get a frame.

## Height range: the spec that matters most

A standing desk's height range is the single most important spec — and the one most people get wrong. The standard seated height for typing is 28-30 inches from the floor to the desktop top. The standard standing height for a 5'10" person is about 46 inches. Adjust ±1 inch for every inch you're taller or shorter than 5'10".

Most consumer desks adjust from about 24-30 inches (seated) to 48-50 inches (standing). That covers 90% of users. The problem comes with very tall or very short users — a 6'4" person needs 50-52 inches at standing height; a 5'0" person needs a low minimum so the desk doesn't push their elbows up at seated height.

Check both numbers, not just the range. A desk that adjusts "from 28 to 50 inches" sounds great until you realize the minimum is too high for someone 5'2" to use seated. The Fully Jarvis goes from 22.6 to 48.7 inches — that's the right low minimum.

For very tall users, look at commercial frames. The Uplift V2 Commercial goes to 49 inches and the Steelcase Series 7 goes to 51 inches. The Vari Electric Standing Desk also extends to 51.5 inches and is a popular pick for users over 6'2".

## Weight capacity: more than you'd think

Consumer desks advertise 220-355 lbs capacity. That sounds like a lot until you add it up:

- 27-inch monitor: 12-15 lbs each
- Dual monitor arm: 5 lbs
- Laptop: 4-6 lbs
- Laptop dock: 3 lbs
- Speakers: 5-10 lbs
- Desk plants, books, lamp: 10-20 lbs

A two-monitor setup with a laptop dock and a couple of books is already 50-60 lbs — well within any desk's capacity. The 350 lb capacity is for setups that include a desktop PC under the desk, two large monitors, and a printer.

The weight capacity also affects stability. A 250 lb capacity desk with 60 lbs of gear is going to wobble at standing height; a 350 lb capacity desk with the same load is rock-solid. If you run dual monitors and want zero wobble, buy more capacity than you need.

## Memory presets: the feature that actually changes behavior

Memory presets are the single biggest behavioral lever on a standing desk. With one button press, the desk goes from sitting to standing height. Without presets, you have to hold the up/down button until you eyeball the right height — and most people stop using the desk after a week because the friction is too high.

Look for at least four memory presets. The Uplift V2 has four, the Fully Jarvis has four, the Flexispot E7 has three. Some cheaper desks have only one or two — avoid them.

The presets also need to be accurate. A desk where the saved height is off by half an inch from the actual ergonomic height means you end up re-adjusting every time, which defeats the purpose.

## Stability and wobble: the hidden spec

A standing desk that wobbles when you type is unusable. Stability depends on three things: the leg thickness (look for 2-3 inch steel legs, not 1.5-inch tubing), the crossbar (a stiff horizontal bar between the legs dramatically reduces front-to-back wobble), and the weight of the surface (a heavy bamboo or solid wood top wobbles less than a hollow MDF top).

Read the customer reviews for "wobble" specifically. Most complaints about $300 desks are wobble complaints. Most complaints about $600+ desks are about something else.

The T-frame desks (where the crossbar is at the back) wobble less than the C-frame (where the crossbar is at the foot) — but the C-frame gives you more legroom.

## Cable management: the spec nobody photographs

A standing desk that goes up and down needs cables that can move with it. A bundle of power cords tied to the back leg will snag at full height and pull your monitor off the desk. Look for:

- A cable tray that mounts under the desktop (Uplift and Branch both sell these for $40-60).
- A cable channel that runs along the back of the frame to keep cords away from the moving crossbar.
- Enough slack in your power cables and monitor cables to handle the full travel of the desk.

Most desks ship with a basic cable management clip or two. Buy a tray. The $50 is worth more than any other accessory.

## The brand matrix: who makes what

**Uplift** is the consumer favorite. The Uplift V2 ($599 with 1-inch bamboo top) is the gold standard: 4 memory presets, 355 lb capacity, 1.5-inch lift speed (compared to 1.0 for cheaper desks), and a wide range of tops. The V2 Commercial ($879) bumps capacity to 400 lbs and adds a 5-year warranty.

**Fully (now Herman Miller)** is the office-grade consumer pick. The Fully Jarvis ($549 with top) is one of the quietest motors on the market, goes from 22.6 to 48.7 inches, and has the cleanest app if you want to track sit/stand time. Fully was acquired by Herman Miller in 2021, and the build quality reflects it.

**Flexispot** is the budget-to-midrange workhorse. The E7 ($479 with top) is dual-motor at 355 lb capacity. The cheaper EC1 ($299) and EC4 ($399) are single-motor and wobblier but perfectly fine for lighter setups. Flexispot's customer service is hit-or-miss, but the hardware is solid for the price.

**Autonomous SmartDesk** is the DTC Instagram pick. The SmartDesk Core ($399) and Pro ($599) look great in product photos and ship fast. Build quality has improved since the early days but is still a step behind Uplift or Fully.

**Branch** is the design-forward upstart. The Branch Duo ($499) is a minimalist standing desk with rounded corners and a 4-button controller. Branch sells direct and is known for fast shipping and clean assembly.

**Vari** is the commercial-grade consumer pick. The Vari Electric Standing Desk ($649) has one of the fastest lift speeds on the market (1.5 inches per second) and a programmable controller with 4 presets. Vari's cable management is the cleanest of the consumer brands.

**IKEA Bekant and Skarsta** are the flat-pack classics. The Bekant ($320) is electric, the Skarsta ($190) is manual crank. Both use IKEA's standard tabletops, which are 1-inch thick and reasonably sturdy. The motors in the Bekant are slower than dedicated standing-desk brands, but for under $350 with a usable surface, they're hard to beat.

**Commercial grade — Steelcase, Herman Miller, HAT.** Steelcase Series 7 ($1,800+), Herman Miller Renew ($2,400+), and HAT Contract's EDP line ($1,500+) are what you find in corporate offices. They have longer warranties (12 years), better wobble performance, and proprietary cable management. Worth it if you have a budget and a 10-year horizon. Overkill for a home setup.

## How to pick the right desk for you

- **First standing desk, budget-conscious:** Flexispot EC1 ($299) or IKEA Skarsta ($190 manual).
- **Work-from-home, dual monitors:** Uplift V2 ($599) or Fully Jarvis ($549).
- **Heavy gear, large work surface:** Uplift V2 Commercial ($879) or Flexispot E7 ($479).
- **Matching a specific aesthetic:** Branch Duo ($499) or a Uplift V2 frame ($399) + custom top from a local woodworker.
- **Long-horizon home office:** Fully Jarvis ($549) or upgrade to Steelcase Series 7 ($1,800+) if budget allows.

## The bottom line

For most people working from home, a Fully Jarvis or Uplift V2 with a bamboo top is the right answer — quiet motor, accurate presets, low wobble, and a 5-10 year lifespan for under $600.
