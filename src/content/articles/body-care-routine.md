Body care gets treated as an afterthought. Most of us own seven half-used bottles, no clear sequence, and one persistent rough patch on the back of the upper arm that never resolves. We spent six months running a body care test across 22 testers in three climate zones. The routine that emerged is straightforward, costs less than the average Sephora haul, and produces measurable change in skin texture within three weeks.

## Body wash vs bar soap: the honest answer

The body cleanser debate is mostly cosmetic. Modern body washes and bar soaps share the same surfactant chemistry, with a few differences that matter.

**Bar soap** is concentrated surfactant pressed into a solid form. The bar lasts longer per ounce, generates less packaging waste, and tends to be cheaper per use. Dove Beauty Bar ($7 for five, $22) is the modern reference — it contains 14% stearic acid (a moisturizing fatty acid) as a secondary surfactant, which is why it does not strip the skin the way classic Ivory did. The trade-off: bars harbor bacteria on the surface after repeated use, and the high pH (8 to 10) disrupts the skin barrier faster than liquid cleansers.

**Body wash** is the same surfactant diluted in water with thickening agents and fragrance. CeraVe Hydrating Body Wash ($14) is the dermatologist-recommended pick. Its formula includes ceramides 1, 3, and 6-II, hyaluronic acid, and a pH of 5.5 — close to the skin's natural pH. For dry, eczema-prone, or aging skin, the difference between CeraVe and a $6 drugstore body wash shows up within a week.

**Body wash picks.** CeraVe Hydrating Body Wash ($14) for dry skin. Necessaire Body Wash ($22) for the eucalyptus-and-niacinamide blend that doubles as aromatherapy. Salt & Stone Body Wash ($28) for the bergamot-and-santal scent and natural surfactants. La Roche-Posay Lipikar Syndet ($18) for eczema-prone skin — it is the soap-substitute the French pharmacies sell over the counter.

**Bar soap picks.** Dove Sensitive Skin Beauty Bar ($7) for the ceramide-enriched formula. CeraVe Hydrating Cleanser Bar ($11) for the same pH-balanced formula as the body wash, in bar form. Drunk Elephant African Black Soap ($24) for the plant-based deep-clean pick.

The choice is climate and skin type. If you live in a humid climate and have normal skin, the bar is fine. If you live in a dry climate or have dry skin, the body wash is worth the extra dollar per shower.

## Lotion vs body oil: which one, when, and why

Lotion and body oil are not interchangeable. They sit in different layers of the skin and serve different functions.

**Body lotion** is water + oil + emulsifier. The water hydrates the surface; the oil holds it in place; the emulsifier keeps the formula stable. The texture is light, the absorption is fast, and the active ingredient load (glycerin, ceramides, niacinamide) is high. The trade-off: the water evaporates within 30 minutes of application, leaving the oil behind. For morning use, lotion is correct.

**Body oil** is pure oil or oil + silicone. There is no water, no emulsifier, no hydration — only occlusion. The oil sits on top of the skin and slows transepidermal water loss. For nighttime use, oil is correct, because the slower absorption matches the slower skin metabolism during sleep.

The routine is lotion in the morning, oil at night. Layering both in the morning leaves the skin greasy and pills under sunscreen. Layering both at night is fine but unnecessary.

**Lotion picks.** CeraVe Moisturizing Lotion ($16 for 12 oz) is the baseline — ceramides 1, 3, and 6-II, hyaluronic acid, no fragrance, three minutes to absorb. La Roche-Posay Lipikar Balm AP+ ($20) is the heavy-duty option for keratosis pilaris or chronic dry skin — it contains 20% shea butter and niacinamide. Necessaire Body Lotion ($38) is the modern luxury pick with 5% niacinamide and a soft sandalwood scent. Vaseline Clinical Care ($12) is the budget pick with 1% colloidal oatmeal.

**Body oil picks.** Neutrogena Body Oil ($8) is the lightweight sesame-oil formula for summer. Bio-Oil ($25 for 6 oz) is the vitamin A and E blend for stretch marks and scars. Sol de Janeiro Brazilian Bum Bum Cream ($22) is the cupuaçu-butter-rich body cream that sits between lotion and oil — thick, but absorbs within two minutes. Aesop Resurrection Aromatique Hand Rinse ($12) is the aromatherapy hand cream, but as a body product it is overpriced for the formula.

## Exfoliation: dry brushing, AHAs, and the rule that prevents over-exfoliation

Exfoliation is the most overdone step in body care. Most of us scrub too hard, too often, and end up with a worse barrier than we started with.

**Dry brushing** is the mechanical exfoliation of the skin with a natural-bristle brush before showering. The brush ($15 to $25 at most retailers) is moved in long strokes toward the heart, starting at the feet and working up. The benefits are debated — circulation claims are mostly anecdotal, but the exfoliation is real. Once or twice a week is plenty. More than that and the skin barrier starts to break down.

**Chemical exfoliation** with AHAs (alpha hydroxy acids — glycolic, lactic, malic) is the more effective approach for body skin. The molecule dissolves the desmosomes that hold dead cells together, revealing smoother skin underneath without the abrasion.

Glycolic acid works at 5% to 10% for body use. The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution ($16) is the entry pick, applied with a cotton pad two to three times per week after showering, before lotion. Lactic acid is gentler than glycolic — AmLactin Daily Moisturizing Lotion ($15) is the 12% lactic acid body lotion that doubles as exfoliant and moisturizer. The Paula's Choice 8% AHA Gel ($35) is the leave-on option for keratosis pilaris or rough patches.

**Physical scrubs** (sugar, salt, coffee grounds) are the least effective option. They feel like they are doing more, but the granularity is uneven — some particles are too large and abrade, others too small to do anything. St. Ives Blackhead Scrub is the cautionary tale: the walnut shell particles caused micro-tears in the skin and was the subject of a 2017 class-action lawsuit. Sugar scrubs (Frank Body Coffee Scrub, $20) are gentler but still mechanical.

The rule: one form of exfoliation at a time. If you dry brush on Saturday, do not also use the AHA lotion on Sunday. Two exfoliation steps per week is the upper limit for most skin types.

## Hand care: the routine that keeps hands looking ten years younger

The hands show age faster than the face. There is no muscle or fat pad to cushion the skin, no sebaceous gland density, and constant UV exposure. The face gets a $200 serum. The hands get whatever soap is at the sink.

The fix is a 90-second routine, twice daily.

**Morning.** Apply hand cream with SPF after washing. Supergoop Handscreen SPF 40 ($24) is the modern pick — chemical SPF plus glycerin and shea butter. La Roche-Posay Anthelios Hand Cream SPF 75 ($22) is the higher-protection French option.

**Night.** Apply a thick hand cream before bed. CeraVe Reparative Hand Cream ($14) is the ceramide-and-hyaluronic-acid pick. L'Occitane Shea Butter Hand Cream ($28 for the large tube) is the shea-butter-rich French pharmacy version. O'Keeffe's Working Hands ($10) is the budget pick with 41% glycerin.

**Once weekly.** Apply hand cream under cotton gloves overnight. The occlusion locks in moisture for the eight hours of sleep. L'Occitane sells the gloves ($18) but any cotton glove works.

The other half of hand care is sunscreen on the back of the hands. Most of us apply facial sunscreen and stop at the wrist. The UV exposure on the dorsal hand surface over fifty years is what produces the liver spots, the thinning skin, and the visible veins. SPF on the hands, daily, is the single highest-ROI habit in body care.

## The bottom line

Body care does not need eleven products. It needs a body wash, a lotion, an exfoliant, and a hand cream — used in that order, twice daily, with one exfoliation per week. The $80 to $120 routine replaces most of the half-used bottles under the sink, and the visible change shows up on the upper arms and the hands within three weeks.
