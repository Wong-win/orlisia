A portable power station is a battery, an inverter, a charge controller, and a handful of outlets packed into a plastic case with a handle. That is the whole product. The hard part is matching its capacity and output to the things you want to power, whether that is a phone on a weekend camping trip or a refrigerator during a three-day blackout.

We have hauled power stations to mountaintops, run a sump pump through a 14-hour outage, and charged e-bikes from the back of a truck. This is the guide we now use to size a station before buying.

## Capacity in watt-hours, not amp-hours

The number that matters is watt-hours, written Wh. A 1,000 Wh station can deliver 1,000 watts for one hour, or 100 watts for 10 hours, or 10 watts for 100 hours. Everything else — volts, amp-hours, mAh — converts into watt-hours with multiplication.

A smartphone charges from empty to full in roughly 15 Wh. A laptop in roughly 60 Wh. A 50W portable fridge uses about 600 Wh in a day. A 1,500W induction cooktop uses about 750 Wh in 30 minutes. A 1,500W space heater uses about 1,500 Wh in one hour. Knowing the watt-hours of the things you want to power lets you pick a station that can run them.

As a rough sizing rule: 100 to 300 Wh for phone-and-tablet camping trips; 500 to 1,000 Wh for car camping with lights, a fridge, and laptops; 1,000 to 2,000 Wh for overlanding and short blackouts; 2,000 Wh and up for home backup, full-size refrigerators, and off-grid cabins.

## Output and ports, the second spec that matters

A 2,000 Wh station is useless if its inverter only puts out 200 W. The inverter rating (continuous AC output) tells you what the station can run. Peak output (sometimes 2x continuous) tells you what it can briefly start, like a refrigerator compressor.

The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 delivers 1,500 W continuous and 3,000 W peak. That covers most kitchen appliances, hairdryers, and induction cooktops. The EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max delivers 2,400 W continuous and 4,800 W peak, enough to run a 1,500 W air conditioner for short bursts. The Bluetti AC200L delivers 2,400 W continuous and 3,600 W peak. The Anker SOLIX F1500F delivers 1,800 W continuous and 3,600 W peak. The Goal Zero Yeti 1500X delivers 1,500 W continuous and 3,000 W peak.

Ports matter as much as wattage. Most stations ship with two to four AC outlets, two to four USB-A ports, one or two USB-C PD ports (60 to 100 W each), a 12V car outlet, and XT60 or Anderson Powerpole DC inputs for solar. The number of USB-C PD ports is the single most underrated spec. Newer laptops, drones, and camera batteries charge over USB-C PD, and a 100 W PD port replaces a chunky laptop power adapter.

## Battery chemistry: LiFePO4 has won

Older portable power stations used lithium-ion NMC chemistry, the same family as electric car batteries. NMC packs more watt-hours per kilogram but degrades faster and is more thermally sensitive. A 2026 NMC station typically rates 500 to 800 full charge cycles before capacity drops to 80%.

Lithium iron phosphate, written LiFePO4 or LFP, is now the default in most premium stations. LiFePO4 packs slightly less energy per kilogram but rates 3,000 to 3,500 full charge cycles and is far less prone to thermal runaway. The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2, EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max, Bluetti AC200L, and Anker SOLIX F1500F all use LiFePO4. Goal Zero still ships some NMC models, and we avoid them.

In practice, a LiFePO4 station used once a week for five years will still hold about 90% of its original capacity. An NMC station will be at 50% in the same period.

## Solar input and the full off-grid loop

A portable power station becomes genuinely useful when it can recharge from the sun. Most stations accept 100 to 500 W of solar input through an MPPT controller (the smarter, more efficient charge controller that squeezes more power out of low-light conditions).

The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 accepts up to 200 W of solar input and recharges fully in about 4.5 hours of direct sun. The EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max accepts up to 1,000 W of solar input and recharges fully in about 2.3 hours. The Bluetti AC200L accepts up to 1,200 W and recharges in about 1.7 hours. The Anker SOLIX F1500F accepts up to 600 W and recharges in about 2.5 hours.

Two factors govern how much solar you actually harvest: panel wattage and direct sun hours. A 200 W panel in full sun produces about 180 W of real input (controllers lose 10 to 15%). A day with 6 peak sun hours gives you roughly 6 x 180 = 1,080 Wh into the station. That is enough to recharge a 1,000 Wh station and power a laptop for an afternoon. It is not enough to run an air conditioner around the clock.

If you camp with a fridge (60 W average draw) and a couple of lights (20 W total), a 500 Wh station with a 200 W solar panel keeps you off-grid indefinitely, assuming 6 hours of sun. Anything more power-hungry requires either more panels or more battery.

## Use cases that decide what to buy

Camping and overlanding: A 500 to 1,000 Wh station at $499 to $999 is the sweet spot. Look for a station under 25 pounds if you are hiking it from the car to the site. The Jackery Explorer 500 at $499 and EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro at $599 are both 16 to 18 pounds. Pair with a 100 W folding panel.

Backup power for a city apartment: A 1,000 to 2,000 Wh station covers a fridge (briefly), a phone, a laptop, a router, and a few lights for a day. The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 at $799 and EcoFlow DELTA 2 at $999 are the right size. Plug the fridge into the station, charge from the wall normally, and the station takes over when the grid drops.

Home backup for a house: Anything under 5,000 Wh is a band-aid. A whole-home backup system from EcoFlow, Bluetti, or a hardwired Tesla Powerwall at $9,000 to $15,000 is a different category and outside the scope of a portable power station. For a portable stopgap, the EcoFlow DELTA Pro at $2,999 with a 3,600 Wh capacity can run a refrigerator for 24 hours.

Off-grid cabin or work site: A 2,000 Wh station with 800 to 1,200 W of solar input gives you near-independence. The Bluetti AC200L at $1,999 paired with four 200 W panels covers almost any realistic load short of heating or air conditioning.

## Weight, form factor, and portability

A 1,000 Wh station typically weighs between 23 and 31 pounds. A 2,000 Wh station typically weighs 50 to 62 pounds. The weight matters because the difference between carrying a station 30 feet from the car and 300 feet to a campsite is the difference between a useful tool and a regret. The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 at 23.8 pounds is the lightest 1,000 Wh class we have tested. The EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max at 50 pounds is the heaviest of its class, though that weight buys you faster charging and higher output.

Form factor also matters. Stations with a top handle and a flat top are easier to stack in a car trunk. Stations with side handles and round shapes roll around. The Bluetti AC200L has a flat top that doubles as a wireless charging pad, a small but useful feature. The Anker SOLIX F1500F has a built-in light strip that doubles as a flashlight, which is genuinely useful during a power outage.

For car camping, weight is mostly about convenience. For hiking or boating, weight is the deciding factor. A 500 Wh class station at 12 to 16 pounds — the EcoFlow RIVER 2 Pro at 16.2 pounds, the Jackery Explorer 500 at 13.3 pounds — is the right ceiling for anything you have to carry more than a few minutes.

## Noise, heat, and inverter quirks

The inverter inside a power station makes a faint high-pitched whine under heavy load, similar to an old CRT television. Most stations stay quiet under 1,000 W. Above 1,500 W, the cooling fans kick in and run at 35 to 50 dB. EcoFlow stations have the quietest fans we have measured. Bluetti fans are louder but rarely need to spin up because the unit runs cool. Jackery fans sit in the middle.

Heat is a real consideration in summer. Most stations are rated to 104°F operating temperature. Above that, the battery management system throttles input and output. Leave the station in the shade or under a tarp during summer camping. Do not leave it in a closed car trunk in August.

Inverter quirks matter for sensitive electronics. Pure sine wave inverters (the kind in every premium station we have mentioned) power laptops, phone chargers, and CPAP machines without issue. Modified sine wave inverters, which appear in some budget stations, can cause buzzing on some audio equipment and can shorten the life of motor-driven appliances. Stick to pure sine wave.

## How we recommend choosing

Start with the watt-hours you need. Add up the devices you want to power, the hours you want to power them, and the buffer you want for bad weather. Multiply by 1.5x to be safe. Then check the inverter rating for the highest-draw device you want to run. Then pick the battery chemistry (LiFePO4 unless you have a budget reason not to). Finally, pick the solar input you can pair with it.

If you want the most balanced all-rounder, the EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max at $1,499 is hard to beat. If you want the lightest 1,000 Wh class, the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 at $799 wins. If you want the most solar input and fastest recharge, the Bluetti AC200L at $1,999 leads. If you want a budget option that still uses LiFePO4, the Anker SOLIX F1500F at $1,299 sits in the middle. Goal Zero remains the most field-proven brand for serious expeditions, but you pay a premium for that pedigree.

## The bottom line

A portable power station is a battery with an inverter, and the right size is the one that covers your worst-case day with a 50% buffer. We would pick the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 for car camping, the EcoFlow DELTA 2 Max for home backup, the Bluetti AC200L for off-grid solar loops, the Anker SOLIX F1500F for budget LiFePO4, and the Goal Zero Yeti for expeditions where service matters more than price.
