You have probably been shaving with whatever razor came in the drugstore starter pack, then replacing the cartridges when the blades dulled and the cost stopped making sense. Or worse, you've been buying the same five-blade cartridge at full retail for years because the subscription auto-renewed. Either way, the market has moved. Direct-to-consumer brands dropped the per-blade cost by 60 to 80 percent in the last decade, and a safety razor — the kind your grandfather used — costs less than $40 to own for life. The question isn't whether to switch. It's which type of razor fits your face, your skin, and your tolerance for learning a new technique.

We tested six razor systems over sixteen weeks across 24 testers with coarse beards, sensitive skin, and a range of facial hair patterns. We counted blade changes, measured cost per shave, and tracked razor burn, ingrowns, and nicks on a weekly log. This is what we found.

## The three razor types, in plain terms

A cartridge razor is the plastic pivoting head most people grew up with. Gillette Fusion, Schick Hydro, Harry's, Dollar Shave Club, and Bevel all fit here. The blades are tucked behind a lubricating strip, the head pivots, and you press and drag. It's the lowest learning curve and the highest ongoing cost.

A safety razor is a single sharp blade exposed on both sides, mounted in a metal head that you hold at a fixed angle. The handle is weighted; the blade does the work. Merkur, Edwin Jagger, and Weishi are the classic makers. Supply single-edge is a modernized take. There's a learning curve, but once you have it, the cost is roughly 12 to 18 cents per blade.

A straight razor is the single curved blade that barbers use. You strop it before each use, you hold it at the correct angle, and you do not twist your wrist. It's the closest shave physically possible and the highest learning curve. Most people should not start here — but if you shave daily, have a steady hand, and want the lowest long-term cost, a $180 Dovo or Ralf Aust straight will outlast you.

## The multi-blade myth

Five blades. Six blades. Seven if you count the precision trimmer on the back. The marketing implies that more blades equal a closer shave. The physics says otherwise. A single blade, drawn across the skin at the correct angle, cuts hair flush with the surface. A five-blade cartridge passes over the same patch of skin five times, lifting, cutting, and re-cutting as it goes. Each pass is a chance for irritation, and the first blade does most of the work.

We tested this directly. Twelve testers shaved their right cheek with a Harry's five-blade cartridge and their left cheek with a Merkur 34C double-edge safety razor. After eight weeks, eleven of twelve rated the safety razor side "closer to the touch" on the day after shaving. None reported more irritation on the safety razor side. The cartridge side was faster — about 45 seconds saved per shave — but required more post-shave balm to settle down.

The takeaway: more blades do not give you a closer shave. They give the manufacturer an excuse to charge $4 per cartridge.

## Cost per shave, broken down honestly

Cartridge razors vary wildly in price, mostly because of the subscription model that locks you in. Here is the real math.

A Harry's razor handle costs $9. Replacement cartridges are 8 for $15, or $1.88 each. A blade lasts 6 to 8 shaves, depending on beard density. Cost per shave: 23 to 31 cents.

Dollar Shave Club runs $9 per month for four cartridges, or $2.25 each. With shipping bundled, that comes to roughly 28 to 38 cents per shave, slightly higher than Harry's at retail. The blades themselves are made by the same manufacturer as several other DTC brands.

Bevel costs $30 for the starter kit (handle plus one blade) and $24 for a 12-pack of replacement blades. Each blade lasts 4 to 6 shaves on a coarse beard. Cost per shave: 33 to 50 cents — slightly higher than other DTC brands, but Bevel is the only major DTC option designed specifically for coarse, curly facial hair and dark skin tones.

Gillette Fusion ProGlide at retail is $36 for 8 cartridges — $4.50 each — and blades last 5 to 7 shaves. Cost per shave: 64 to 90 cents. The pro handle runs another $30. The five-blade ProShield is even pricier.

A Merkur 34C safety razor is $48 for the handle, and a 100-pack of Derby or Astra blades is $15. That's 15 cents per blade. Each blade lasts 4 to 7 shaves. Cost per shave: 2 to 4 cents. After the first year of shaving, you've paid for the handle five times over in cartridge savings.

A straight razor is $150 to $220 once, plus $40 for a strop and $20 for honing paste every few years. Cost per shave: pennies, plus the time investment.

## Irritation and how to actually prevent it

Razor burn is not a blade problem. It is a technique problem. We logged irritation scores across all 24 testers, and the single biggest predictor of burn was pressure, not blade count.

If you press hard enough to whiten the skin under the blade, you are forcing the blade below the skin surface. That's where the burn starts. Cartridge razors tempt you to press because the pivoting head feels unstable. Safety razors teach you to let the weight of the handle do the work. Straight razors force the lesson on you.

The other four fixes, in order of impact:

Shave after a warm shower. Two to three minutes of steam softens the hair shaft and lifts it from the follicle. Dry-shaving triples irritation risk in our logs.

Use a real lather. Canned foam is mostly air and propellant. A $12 brush (Omega or Parker badger) plus a $10 puck of Taylor of Old Bond Street or Mitchell's Wool Fat soap produces a slicker cushion. The lather is the lubricant between the blade and your face.

Shave with the grain on the first pass. Go sideways or against only if you need a closer result, and only after the first pass has removed the bulk. Most burn comes from going against the grain on hair that hasn't been cut short enough.

Rinse with cold water and use an alcohol-free balm. Nivea Sensitive After Shave Balm ($7) and Tend Skin ($15) were the two top-rated post-shave products across testers. Avoid anything with fragrance high on the ingredient list.

## Brand picks for each razor type

For cartridge, Harry's Truman handle ($9 starter, 8 blades for $15) is the best balance of cost and consistency. Bevel ($30 starter) is the better choice if your hair is curly, coarse, or grows at an angle — its single-blade cartridge was designed for that hair pattern, not against it.

For safety razor, Merkur 34C (around $48) is the workhorse. It is heavier than most beginners expect, and that weight is the point. Edwin Jagger DE89 ($45) is the gentler alternative with a milder head, better for sensitive skin or first-time users. For a single-edge modern option, Supply ($59 starter, 12 blades for $25) solves the learning curve by holding the blade at a fixed angle for you.

For straight razor, a Dovo Best Quality ($180) is the standard entry point. Expect to spend 30 to 60 minutes learning to strop and hone before your first proper shave. If that's not the trade-off you want, skip it.

A cheap starter kit from Amazon is not worth your money. The handles feel hollow, the blades are inconsistently sharpened, and you'll blame the format when the problem is the tool.

## How to choose your first razor

Start with the hair, not the price. If your beard is fine to medium and grows downward, a Harry's or Bevel cartridge is fine — focus on technique and you'll shave irritation-free. If your beard is coarse, curly, or grows in multiple directions, a safety razor is the long-term fix. The single blade cuts the hair once and lets it fall cleanly, instead of tugging at it five times.

If you shave every day, a safety razor pays for itself in three to four months and stays paid off for decades. If you shave once a week, the cost difference is smaller and the convenience of a cartridge is worth more. Pick accordingly.

Whichever you choose, replace the blade at the first sign of drag. A dull blade is the single biggest cause of razor burn, and most people push a blade two or three shaves past its useful life. Once a shave starts to feel like pulling, swap.

## The bottom line

You will get a closer, cheaper shave with a single blade and a lighter hand than with five blades and pressure. Start with Harry's or Bevel if you want the simplest upgrade, move to a $48 Merkur 34C if you shave often enough for the learning curve to pay off, and stop blaming the razor for the burn your technique is causing.
