Most homes end up with mediocre Wi-Fi because the equipment was chosen for the wrong reason. People buy the cheapest router their ISP offers, find a dead spot in the bedroom, drop a range extender onto it, and live with a network that's faster in some rooms than others. Sometimes that works. More often, it doesn't, and the fix is either a better single router or an actual mesh system — not the two things confused together.

The decision is simpler than the marketing. Below we walk through when each option is right, what "mesh" actually does versus what people think it does, and which systems deliver on coverage without becoming a privacy footgun.

## When a Single Router Is Enough

A single router covers a surprisingly large area. Modern Wi-Fi 6 and Wi-Fi 6E routers from Asus, Netgear, TP-Link, and Linksys deliver usable throughput (over 100 Mbps to a phone) over roughly 1,500-2,500 square feet in a typical wood-frame, drywall-interior home. Concrete, brick, or steel-stud walls cut that significantly — 800-1,200 square feet is more realistic in an apartment block.

If your home is under 1,500 square feet and you can place the router centrally, a single Wi-Fi 6 router is the right answer. The Asus RT-AX86U at $250, TP-Link Archer AX90 at $200, and Netgear Nighthawk RAX50 at $180 are competent choices, and a $400 Wi-Fi 7 router like the Asus RT-BE96U is overkill for most households. Save the mesh budget.

If you have a single-story home under 2,000 square feet with drywall walls, try the single router first. You can always add a mesh node later, and almost every modern system supports mixing standalone routers with mesh satellites.

## When Mesh Actually Wins

Mesh becomes the right call when any of these conditions apply.

The home is larger than 2,000 square feet. One radio at one location cannot cover that much area to a usable signal strength, regardless of how powerful it is. The physics won't move.

The home has more than one floor. Wi-Fi degrades sharply through floors. Every floor in a wood-frame house attenuates the signal by 10-15 dB. Concrete slabs are worse. Adding a mesh node on each floor is usually the only way to get full coverage.

The home has thick walls, plaster, brick, or steel framing. Older homes with plaster-and-lath walls, post-and-beam construction, or modern ICF (insulated concrete forms) construction block Wi-Fi enough that a single router covers maybe half the footprint.

The home is irregular — long, narrow, with wings or L-shapes. A router in the corner of an L-shaped house will always leave the far end of the L weak. A mesh node placed at the bend fixes it.

If none of these apply — single story, wood frame, under 1,800 square feet, central placement possible — a single router is the correct answer. Do not buy mesh you don't need.

## What Mesh Actually Does

A mesh system uses multiple wireless nodes that communicate with each other and present a single network name (SSID) to your devices. As you walk around the house, your phone and laptop automatically roam between nodes based on signal strength.

That's the marketing pitch. The implementation details matter.

A true tri-band mesh system dedicates one of its three radio bands (usually a 5 GHz or 6 GHz channel) to the backhaul — the connection between nodes. The other two bands serve your devices. This backhaul is what separates real mesh from a glorified range extender. A netgear Nighthawk range extender at $60 rebroadcasts on the same radio it listens on, which cuts throughput in half. A true mesh system with a dedicated backhaul radio does not.

The practical effect: a tri-band mesh system delivers roughly 60-75% of its rated top speed between nodes, while a range extender delivers roughly 25-40%. Over enough floor area, that's the difference between usable and unusable.

Dual-band mesh systems without a dedicated backhaul are fine for internet up to about 300 Mbps but bottleneck badly on gigabit connections. If your ISP gives you 500 Mbps or more, look for tri-band systems.

## Wi-Fi 6, Wi-Fi 6E, and Wi-Fi 7 — What They Mean

The naming has gotten harder, not easier. The practical differences are clear.

Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) operates on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands. It improves throughput over Wi-Fi 5 by 30-50% in dense environments. Real-world peak throughput to a single device is around 1.2 Gbps in ideal conditions, which is to say faster than most ISPs deliver. Wi-Fi 6 is the right baseline in 2026.

Wi-Fi 6E adds a third band at 6 GHz. This band has up to 1,200 MHz of clean spectrum in most countries — much wider than the 5 GHz band — and it's free from the radar and microwave-oven noise that plagues older bands. The catch: range on 6 GHz is noticeably shorter than 5 GHz because of higher propagation loss. Wi-Fi 6E matters for offices, high-density apartments, and homes where you need to serve many devices in close range. It does not help coverage at distance.

Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) is the current flagship. Practical improvements over Wi-Fi 6E include wider 320 MHz channels, multi-link operation (a device connects on 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz simultaneously), and 4K-QAM modulation for higher peak throughput. Real-world improvements over a well-tuned Wi-Fi 6E network are 20-40% in throughput and significantly better latency under load. Wi-Fi 7 mesh systems cost $500-$900 for a three-pack; whether they're worth it depends on whether your devices even support Wi-Fi 7 yet. Many phones from 2024 and 2025 support it; older devices don't.

The recommendation: if you're buying mesh in 2026, get Wi-Fi 6E minimum. Wi-Fi 7 is a smart forward purchase if you live in a house that will last ten years and you can absorb the extra $200 over a Wi-Fi 6E system.

## The Top Mesh Brands Compared

Four brands dominate mesh for good reason. The differences are in coverage, software quality, and how much they lock you in.

Amazon Eero is the easiest to set up and run. The Eero Pro 6E three-pack at $549 covers 6,000 square feet with a triband backhaul. The Eero Max 7 at $1,799 for a three-pack is Wi-Fi 7 with the longest published range in Eero's lineup. Eero's app is genuinely simple, and the units just work. The downsides: Eero requires a cloud account, Eero Plus is a paid subscription ($99/year) for advanced features, and Eero devices have an Amazon-managed hardware firewall that filters some traffic by default. Privacy-conscious buyers should know that Eero collects network metadata.

Netgear Orbi is the coverage leader. The Orbi 970 Series (Wi-Fi 7) at $1,999 for a three-pack covers over 10,000 square feet with dedicated 5 GHz backhaul. The Orbi 860 Series at $899 covers up to 8,000 square feet in a triband configuration. Orbi is the right pick for large homes and small offices. Setup is harder than Eero; the Orbi app regularly draws complaints about reliability.

TP-Link Deco is the value pick. The Deco XE200 (Wi-Fi 6E) three-pack costs $499 with a dedicated 6 GHz backhaul and 7,200 sq ft of rated coverage. The Deco BE85 (Wi-Fi 7) three-pack at $899 delivers similar coverage to Orbi for half the price. Deco is widely recommended because the underlying hardware is genuinely competitive — TP-Link owns the same supply chain — and the software has matured. Power-user features (VLAN tagging, VPN server, custom DNS) are available in the web interface for users who want them.

Asus ZenWiFi is the enthusiast pick. The ZenWiFi AX (XT8) at $650 for a two-pack is triband with strong range, and Asus's AiMesh software allows adding almost any Asus router as a node. The ZenWiFi ET8 (Wi-Fi 6E) at $850 extends that to the 6 GHz band. The ZenWiFi BQ16 (Wi-Fi 7) at $1,200 for two is the flagship for users who want Wi-Fi 7 and full router-level config. Asus is the right choice for buyers who want the mesh experience with full router features: VPN server, custom firewall rules, per-device QoS, guest VLANs, and no cloud account required.

## Installation and Positioning

A mesh system only helps if positioned correctly. Two rules cover most placements.

Rule one: place the primary router where your current router is. The primary node needs an Ethernet connection to your modem. Yes, Eero, Orbi, and Deco support wireless backhaul between any two nodes, but Ethernet is faster and more reliable. If you can run a single Ethernet cable from the modem location to a central point in the home, do that and put the primary node there.

Rule two: place each satellite halfway between the primary and the area you need covered. A satellite ten feet from the primary doesn't help; a satellite halfway to the dead zone does. Each node should be visible from the primary with at most one wall in between, and the satellite should similarly cover the area with one wall or less between it and the devices it serves.

Most mesh systems include a setup app that walks through placement. Follow those instructions; they include a signal test between nodes that tells you whether each satellite is in a good spot.

## Common Mesh Mistakes

Mixing brands. A Deco node will not pair with an Eero system. Pick one brand and stick with it.

Using the old router as the gateway. Your mesh system wants to be the only router on the network. Put your ISP-provided gateway into bridge mode if your ISP allows it, then connect the mesh primary to the bridged gateway via Ethernet.

Hiding nodes in cabinets. Furniture, especially solid wood or with metal hardware, cuts signal. Nodes belong in open space, ideally at least three feet off the floor.

Buying tri-band but using single-band settings. If your mesh offers a "dedicated backhaul" mode in its settings, use it. The default sometimes routes traffic across both 5 GHz bands, which is fine for most users but loses throughput in heavy-use homes.

## The bottom line

Pick mesh if your home is over 2,000 square feet, more than one story, or has thick walls — otherwise a single $200 router is enough. For most homes that need mesh, the TP-Link Deco XE200 at $499 three-pack or the Eero Pro 6E at $549 deliver the best balance of coverage, performance, and software quality. Spend more on Wi-Fi 7 only if you intend to keep the system for five years and you have devices that actually support Wi-Fi 7.
