Container gardening looks simple from the deck of a garden center: a row of pretty pots, a bunch of flowering plants, soil in a bag, and the assumption that it will all work out. The reality is that the failure modes are specific and avoidable. A pot too small for the plant. A pot with no drainage. A fast-draining terracotta with a thirsty tomato. A heavy ceramic pot on a deck not engineered for the weight. Once you understand pots as a system — material, size, drainage, soil, plant — the rest becomes straightforward.

What follows is the working decision framework: which material makes sense for which plant, what size pot to buy, how to manage drainage without flooding the deck, and the plants that actually thrive in containers.

## Pot materials: what each one does

Every container material has tradeoffs. Pick by what you need most.

**Terracotta** is the unglazed clay pot. It is porous, which means it breathes — moisture and air move through the walls. That is genuinely good for plants that hate wet feet (rosemary, lavender, citrus, succulents, herbs, Mediterranean shrubs). The downside: it dries out fast. In summer heat in zone 7+, a small terracotta can dry out in a single afternoon. The salt-and-mineral patina that develops on the outside over years is the look most gardeners want and others do not. A 12-inch terracotta pot runs $15 to $50; 18-inch pieces run $40 to $120.

**Glazed ceramic** is the colorful, sealed pot you see in front of every restaurant patio. The glaze seals the clay so it does not breathe; moisture stays in the soil longer, which is good for moisture-loving plants (fuchsias, ferns, caladiums) and risky for drought-tolerant plants. Glazed pots are heavy (a 20-inch glazed ceramic weighs 40 to 80 pounds empty) and crack in hard freezes. The colorful Mexican pottery, Italian Vietri, and Indonesian terracotta are mostly this category. $40 to $250 for most sizes.

**Fiberglass, resin, and plastic** are the modern lightweight pots. They look like stone or ceramic, weigh a third as much, and survive a decade outdoors. Theumat Plainview (Resin) and theVeradek (fiberglass) brands dominate the higher end. The cheap plastic pots from the box stores crack after 3 years in sun; the better fiberglass (Crescent Garden, Campania, Lite Stone) lasts 10 to 15 years and looks like real stone at half the weight. Excellent for balconies and rooftop decks where weight is a constraint. $60 to $300 depending on size.

**Fabric grow bags** (Root Pouch, Smart Pots, the 5-gallon and 10-gallon sizes at Amazon and hardware stores, $7 to $20 each) are the volume-pick for vegetables, herbs, and annuals. They air-prune roots, which produces dense root balls, they fold flat to store in winter, they last 3 to 5 seasons, and they cost almost nothing. They are not pretty, so they belong behind the tomatoes and the tomatoes do the aesthetic work.

**Metal** pots (galvanized steel, zinc, copper, copper-painted steel) are a strong category. Galvanized stock tanks in the 3-foot and 4-foot sizes (Tractor Supply, $80 to $200) are hugely popular for vegetable gardens and decorative annuals. They heat up in direct sun, which can cook roots of sensitive plants; lining the inside with burlap or foam insulation and using light-colored pots helps. Real copper develops a beautiful green patina over 5 to 10 years. $40 to $300 depending on size and material.

**Wood** boxes (cedar, redwood, or pressure-treated pine, $60 to $200 in standard sizes) are the right answer for raised beds rather than patio pots. Cedar and redwood last 10 to 15 years untreated. The half-bourbon-barrel planters are still widely sold and last about 10 years before the bands need tightening and the oak begins to rot.

**Stone and concrete** (cast stone, hypertufa, fiber-stone) are gorgeous, expensive ($200 to $800 for most garden-center sizes), and extremely heavy — a 24-inch cast stone pot can weigh 200 pounds empty. They are permanent installations and need to be set on a surface that can take the load. Many manufacturers offer stone-looking pots in fiberglass at 30 percent of the weight.

## What grows in what

Not every plant belongs in every pot. The rough rule: the plant's root depth determines pot depth, and the plant's water needs determine pot material.

**Vegetables**. Tomatoes need a 5-gallon or larger pot, ideally a 10-gallon fabric grow bag or a 14-inch plastic or fiberglass pot. They are heavy feeders and need water daily in summer. Peppers want a 3-gallon to 5-gallon pot (10 to 12 inches across) — peppers actually like a constrained root system that pushes energy into fruit instead of foliage. Lettuce, herbs, and salad greens do fine in 6-inch to 10-inch pots. Cucumbers and squash want 10 gallons or larger and a sturdy trellis. Carrots and beets need depth (12 inches for full-size carrots; 6 inches for round varieties).

**Herbs**. The Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage, lavender) want terracotta, want to dry out between waterings, and want full sun. Italian basil is the opposite — moisture-loving, fast-growing, heavy feeder. Mint needs its own pot because it is invasive; a single mint in a 12-inch pot will fill it in a season and try to escape.

**Flowering annuals**. Petunias, calibrachoa, sweet potato vine, million bells (Calibrachoa), geraniums, marigolds — all do fine in 10 to 14-inch plastic or glazed pots. The "thriller, filler, spiller" recipe (an upright plant in the middle, mounders around it, vines cascading over the edge) works in any 14-inch+ pot.

**Perennials**. Anything you would plant in the ground, you can plant in a pot for the first few seasons. The constraint is cold hardiness — the roots are less insulated in a pot than in the ground, so a zone 6 perennial (USDA hardiness rating) in a pot is really a zone 7 plant. Plan to overwinter in a sheltered spot, an unheated garage, or to treat it as an annual.

**Small trees and topiary**. Citrus, fig, Japanese maple, and small ornamental trees are the high-end container category. They need 20 to 30 inches of pot diameter and high-quality potting soil (Fox Farm, Coast of Maine Stonington Blend, or Espoma Organic Potting Mix at $20 to $50 per 2 cubic foot bag). Most are happy in the same pot for 3 to 5 years before going up a size.

**Houseplants in summer**. Tropical foliage — elephant ear (Colocasia), banana (Ensete, Musa), coleus, philodendron, caladium — thrives in containers outside in summer. They want moisture, partial shade, and rich soil. Most get dug and overwintered indoors at the first frost.

## Sizing: how big, why it matters

Most container plants die because the container is too small. A small container holds less soil, less water, and less margin for error.

The rule of thumb: the smaller the mature plant, the smaller the pot; but the larger the plant, the larger the pot relative to its size. Tomatoes are big plants and want proportionately bigger pots.

Real numbers:

- 6-inch pot: small succulents, single herb cuttings, small annuals like pansies
- 8 to 10 inch pot: lettuces, herbs, single petunia, small pepper plant
- 12 to 14 inch pot: large herbs (basil, dill, parsley), single geranium, paired annual planting, blueberry bush
- 16 to 18 inch pot: tomato, large pepper, small fig, raspberry, small citrus
- 20 to 24 inch pot: full-size tomato, blueberry, small tree, large herb collection
- 24+ inch pot: ornamental tree, large vegetable planting, large shrub, large annual arrangement

Tall pots have the same root volume as wide pots at any given depth, so a 24-inch wide pot with 12-inch depth and a 16-inch wide pot with 18-inch depth grow similarly. Drainage matters at every size.

## Drainage: the often-skipped, always-important step

Every container, with very few exceptions, needs a drainage hole. Standing water at the bottom of a pot rots roots fast.

Workarounds for pots without holes: drill a hole (terracotta, ceramic, and plastic all drill cleanly with a masonry bit), put the plant in a smaller plastic pot and drop it into the decorative outer pot with rocks between them, or treat the pot as a cover and use it only for plants that like standing water (papyrus, pickerel weed, lotus — none of which the typical gardener grows).

When you water, water until it drains from the bottom. A common mistake is "little and often" watering that wets the surface and never the root zone. For a 14-inch pot on a 90°F day in July, that may mean watering twice a day.

Elevating pots 1 inch off the deck (pot feet, $3 to $10 for a 4-pack; broken pot shards also work) lets water drain freely rather than pooling under the pot.

## Self-watering systems and how they actually work

A self-watering container has a reservoir in the bottom (typically 1 to 3 gallons depending on pot size), a wicking chamber that draws water up into the soil, and a fill tube or hole at the side. Examples we trust:

- **Lechuza** (the German brand, $90 to $400 for most sizes) — premium self-watering planters with integrated water level indicators
- **Crescent Garden** (self-watering TruDrop line, $80 to $300)
- **EarthBox** ($60 to $80 with cover and stakes) — the volume-pick for vegetable gardeners who want weeks of unattended watering
- **Bloem** and **HC Companies** sell budget-tier self-watering planters at $30 to $80
- **Self-watering grow bags** (AutoPot, Earth Box junior) for vegetables, $20 to $40 per bag plus reservoir

Self-watering pays off when the plants are water-hungry (tomatoes, peppers, leafy greens) and the gardener is away during the day or week. For Mediterranean herbs and succulents, self-watering is the wrong choice — they prefer dry-down cycles.

In an 18-inch self-watering planter, the reservoir holds 2 to 4 quarts. That is enough to water a tomato for 2 to 3 days in summer heat, longer for a less-demanding plant. Plan to top off every 3 to 5 days.

## Soil: don't use garden soil

The single most common container gardening mistake is filling pots with garden soil or topsoil. Garden soil is heavy, has living organisms that decay quickly in a confined pot, and lacks the air pockets containers need.

The right soil for containers is **potting mix** — not garden soil, not topsoil, not compost alone. Potting mixes are blended to drain well, retain moisture, and resist compaction. The big three in any garden center:

- **Fox Farm Ocean Forest** ($25 to $40 per 1.5 cubic foot) — the premium pick with composted forest humus, perlite, and earthworm castings
- **Coast of Maine Stonington Blend** ($25 to $35 per 1.5 cubic foot) — lobster meal, kelp, and composted cow manure, designed for vegetables and herbs
- **Espoma Organic Potting Mix** ($10 to $20 per 2 cubic foot) — the value pick, peat-based, good enough for most annuals and vegetables

For raised beds (which are containers at scale), use a blend of 60 percent topsoil, 30 percent compost, and 10 percent perlite or coarse sand. Pure potting mix is too expensive for large volumes and too free-draining for deep beds.

## The bottom line

Pick pots in the right size for the mature plant, in the right material for its water needs (terracotta for dry-loving, glazed or plastic for moisture-loving), with drainage holes, filled with potting mix not garden soil. The plants that fail you in pots almost always fail you because of one of those four things — not because you bought the wrong variety.
