You walk into the average living room and the same pattern repeats almost every time. The sofa sits flush against the longest wall. A single chair tucks into an empty corner. The TV gets bolted opposite the sofa. The room technically has furniture in it, but it does not function the way a real living room should. People pass through instead of gathering in it.

Good layout is not about picking nicer pieces. It is about deciding what the room has to do and arranging everything else in service of that. Below is the sequence we use when we plan a space, from the focal point all the way down to the rug size.

## Pick the focal point first, then orient everything else

Every living room needs one anchor the eye lands on the moment you walk in. Sometimes that is the fireplace. Sometimes it is a big window with a view. Sometimes it is an oversized piece of art, a media wall, or an architectural feature like an exposed brick chimney. Whatever the anchor is, the primary seating has to point at it.

A common mistake we see is letting a TV become the focal point by default. Flat screens on the opposite wall turn the entire room into a viewing gallery. That is fine for a dedicated media room, but in a living room, you usually want to choose. If your fireplace and your TV fight for the same wall, look at installing a mantel-mounted TV (MantelMount, Peerless-AV, and Superior makes hinges that swing the screen down to eye level). If that is not in the budget, angle the seating in a U-shape so half the room faces the fireplace and the other half angles toward the TV.

When the focal point is a window, leave at least 12 inches between the back of the sofa and the glass line. Sofas placed flush under windows block the lower sash and trap cold air in winter.

## The 30-inch traffic-flow rule

Walkways inside the seating arrangement should be at least 30 inches wide. The path from one major opening (door, archway, hallway mouth) to another needs to be 36 to 42 inches, especially if two people will pass each other or if you regularly carry a tray through.

We sketch the layout on graph paper before we move anything. One square equals 6 inches works well. Place the openings first, then the focal point wall, then the traffic lanes as dotted lines. Furniture that crosses a dotted line is the first thing to move. If a 96-inch Crate & Barrel sectional keeps pushing a coffee table into the walkway, dropping to an 84-inch West Elm sectional often opens the room.

In studios and open floor plans, run a long, low bookcase perpendicular to the wall to act as a divider. CB2's Metal Locker Storage piece is 33 inches deep and 80 inches tall, which is enough to visually separate the living area from the entry without blocking light from a nearby window.

## Float furniture off the Walls

Pushing every piece against the wall is a habit from the 1990s that has not earned its place in 2026. Pulling the sofa 8 to 12 inches into the room creates breathing room behind it for a console table, a floor lamp, or a slim bench. A West Elm Parsons console at 48 inches wide and 18 inches deep fits perfectly behind most sofas and gives you a drop zone for keys.

In rooms under 14 by 16 feet, even 6 inches is enough to break the "waiting room" feel. The space behind the sofa doesn't need to be functional - it needs to be visual relief. If you have baseboard heating along that wall, skip the console and put a single arc floor lamp like the CB2 Editor behind the left end of the sofa. The pool of warm light is what reads as furniture.

## Conversation Zones Versus TV Zones

Most family living rooms do two jobs at once. The trick is to design two zones - one for talking, one for watching - even if they overlap.

Build the conversation zone around the sofa facing two chairs across a coffee table. Keep chairs within 8 feet of the nearest sofa seat so people do not have to raise their voice. The TV zone is whatever faces the screen directly. A Pottery Barn Big Sur Square Arm sofa at 96 inches can comfortably face two swivel chairs from the same line, so a guest can watch with you or turn 45 degrees away to read.

If the room is small, consider putting a console table behind the sofa and turning the front seating into a reading nook. A single leather club chair plus a floor lamp and side table is enough. A West Elm Slope Leather Chair at $1,499 is a comfortable reading chair that does not bulk up the room.

## Scale and Proportion Math

Three numbers govern whether a room reads as balanced:

- **Sofa length** should be roughly two-thirds of the wall it sits against. A 96-inch sofa on a 14-foot (168-inch) wall feels too small; a 96-inch sofa on a 12-foot wall fits but feels cramped. Aim for the sofa to fill 60 to 75 percent of that wall.
- **Artwork over the sofa** should be two-thirds the width of the sofa and hung 6 to 12 inches above the top of the back. A 64-inch-wide canvas above an 84-inch sofa typically works.
- **Coffee table length** should be about two-thirds the length of the sofa and sit at the same height as the seat, give or take an inch. A 54-inch oval coffee table is right for an 84-inch sofa; a 56-inch rectangle works for a 96-inch sofa.

If the ceiling is 9 feet or lower, switch to low-profile furniture. Backs of 28 inches or under keep the room feeling open. Pottery Barn's Cameron Roll Arm and Crate & Barrel's Lounge II both sit in the 30- to 32-inch range.

## Layout Patterns by Room Shape

Different room shapes call for different arrangements. Here is what consistently works in each.

**Long, narrow rooms (under 12 feet wide):** Split the room lengthwise. Two facing loveseats, each 64 to 72 inches, with a console behind one of them. Keep the depth of the seating zone to 11 feet and let the back 18 to 24 inches hold a desk or a display shelf. IKEA's FÄRLÖV and KIVIK sofas both come in 72-inch widths and under 33-inch depths that fit narrow footprints.

**Square rooms (14 by 14 or 15 by 15):** Treat the room symmetrically. Four chairs around a square coffee table, or a sofa facing two chairs across a long table. Avoid L-shaped sectionals - they eat the floor and break symmetry. A CB2 Lounge Chair at $999 and two of them facing a sofa work beautifully.

**L-shaped or open concept rooms:** Anchor the living corner with a rug large enough to define it (8 by 10 minimum), then let the other leg of the L serve a separate purpose - dining, reading, or play. Crate & Barrel's Marne rug at 9 by 12 ($799) is a common anchor. Keep at least the front legs of every major piece of furniture on the rug.

**Rooms with awkward openings (arches, half-walls):** Place the sofa on the wall opposite the opening so it faces into the room. Float a chair at a 90-degree angle to create a corner. The opening itself becomes the "fourth wall."

## Rug Size and Coffee Table Sizing

A rug too small is the most common layout mistake in any living room. For seating fully on the rug, you need an 8 by 10 minimum. For the front legs only rule, a 5 by 8 will hold a sofa and one chair. Anything smaller is a doormat.

As a quick check: the rug should extend at least 6 inches beyond each side of the coffee table, and the front legs of the sofa should sit on it. If the rug is too short for the sofa, layer a 4 by 6 wool rug on top of a larger jute or sisal to fake scale (West Elm, Crate & Barrel, and Dash & Albert all carry both at $129 to $399 per piece).

Coffee table height should be within 2 inches of the sofa seat height, and the table top should sit 14 to 18 inches above the seat. The clearance between sofa front and table edge should be at least 18 inches for walking past, ideally 24 inches for putting your feet up.

## Storage, Lighting, and the Final Layer

Once the big pieces are placed, the layout is finished with a few quieter moves. A floor lamp in the corner behind a chair eliminates the cave effect that floating sofas sometimes create. A 24- to 30-inch side table next to every seat - end table or C-table - gives people a place for a drink that is not the coffee table.

A media console should be at least as wide as the TV but no wider than two-thirds the wall. A 65-inch TV pairs with a 60- to 72-inch console. Pottery Barn's Toulouse and West Elm's Mid-Century Media Console both hit those widths at $1,099 and $849 respectively.

The final move is to stand in the doorway and squint. The shapes of furniture should read as a clean arrangement - sofa, table, chairs - without anybody having to think about it. If something visually shouts, edit it out or move it to a different room.

## The bottom line

A good living room layout starts with one focal point, runs on traffic lanes that are at least 30 inches wide, and pulls seating off the walls by 8 to 12 inches. Get those three things right and the rest of the room falls into place.
