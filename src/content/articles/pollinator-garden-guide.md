The phrase "pollinator garden" covers a wide range of ambitions. At one end it is a single 4×8 raised bed of milkweed, coneflower, and bee balm that brings 20 species of bees into a backyard. At the other end it is a designed, multi-season native landscape with over 50 species, layered for bloom from early April through October. The first one takes a Saturday. The second one takes a few years and a budget in the $1,500 to $4,000 range for a half-acre.

What follows is what to plant, where to plant it, when to plant it, and how to make sure the garden actually attracts what it is supposed to attract — without spraying anything that kills the pollinators you are trying to help.

## What "pollinator" actually means in your yard

Most of what you hear about pollinator decline — colony collapse disorder, monarch butterfly counts — relates to three pollinator groups that respond differently to habitat. The plants that help each group are not the same.

**Honeybees and native bees** (there are 4,000 species in North America, of which only one is the European honeybee) need nectar and pollen throughout the growing season. A garden that blooms only in June is useful for three weeks; a garden that blooms from April through October supports brood-rearing and reproduction.

**Butterflies** need nectar for adults and host plants for caterpillars. Monarch caterpillars eat only milkweed (Asclepias). Black swallowtail caterpillars eat only plants in the parsley family (parsley, dill, fennel, rue). Tiger swallowtails depend on cherry, willow, and tulip poplar trees. Planting milkweed brings monarchs; planting the wrong host plants in the wrong zone does nothing.

**Hummingbirds** are nectar specialists. They feed on tubular flowers with high sugar concentrations: bee balm, salvia, cardinal flower, trumpet honeysuckle, and red buckeye. They migrate into the northern U.S. and southern Canada from Mexico each spring — about 8 species in the U.S. east, fewer in the west — and they need refueling stops along the way.

A pollinator garden that hits one or two of these groups is fine; a garden that hits all three throughout the season is what most people describe when they talk about the idea.

## Native plants versus ornamental cultivars

This is the single most important plant selection in any pollinator garden. Native plants (those that evolved with your region's pollinators) support 10 to 50 times more caterpillar and bee biomass than ornamental cultivars of the same genus. The native bee species that depend on native asters, for instance, cannot digest nonnative asters from Asia.

Where the conversation gets blurry is in the ornamental cultivar market. **Proven Winners** (the brand), **Monrovia**, **Star Roses and Plants**, and **Great Garden Plants** sell extensively bred cultivars of native species — compact coneflowers, reblooming milkweeds, "powder blue" and "pow wow" bee balms, dwarf Joe Pye weed. These are still native at the species level (Echinacea purpurea is still Echinacea purpurea) and most still feed bees.

What does not feed bees is a double-flowered cultivar (the "pom-pom" type). A double coneflower has no accessible pollen or nectar because the reproductive parts have been bred into petals. Same for most double-flowered daisies, double bee balm, and double marigolds. If the flower looks like a fully stuffed satin pillow, it is probably useless to a bee.

The brand-name native plant sources we trust: **Monrovia** (premium containerized natives from independent garden centers), **Proven Winners** (mass-market retail, lower price), **Native Roots Garden Shop** and other regional native plant nurseries, **Prairie Moon Nursery** (midwest and eastern natives by mail order), and **High Country Gardens** (western native specialists, also by mail).

## Region-by-region plant picks

The right plants depend on where you live. What works in Phoenix (desert willow, fairy duster) feeds nothing in Vermont. Here are the working picks for the major U.S. regions.

**Northeast and Upper Midwest (USDA zones 3 to 6)**. For bees and general pollinators: bee balm (Monarda didyma), New England aster (Symphyotrichum novae-angliae), wild bergamot (Monarda fistulosa), purple coneflower (Echinacea purpurea), black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia hirta), goldenrod (Solidago). For monarchs: common milkweed (Asclepias syriaca) and butterfly weed (Asclepias tuberosa). For hummingbirds: cardinal flower (Lobelia cardinalis), wild columbine (Aquilegia canadensis), and spotted bee balm.

**Southeast and Mid-Atlantic (zones 6 to 9)**. For bees: sweet pepperbush (Clethra alnifolia), Joe Pye weed (Eutrochium purpureum), mountain mint (Pycnanthemum muticum), coastal plain Joe Pye (Eutrochium dubium). For monarchs: swamp milkweed (Asclepias incarnata) and butterfly weed. For hummingbirds: red buckeye (Aesculus pavia), trumpet vine (Campsis radicans, which can be aggressive), salvia.

**Plains and Mountain West (zones 4 to 7)**. For bees: prairie clover (Dalea), purple prairie clover (Dalea purpurea), beebalm (Monarda fistulosa), Rocky Mountain bee plant (Cleome serrulata), goldenrod. For monarchs: showy milkweed (Asclepias speciosa). For hummingbirds: scarlet gilia (Ipomopsis aggregata), hummingbird trumpet (Zauschneria).

**Pacific Northwest (zones 6 to 9)**. For bees: Douglas aster, blue camas, lupine, kinnikinnick. For monarchs: showy milkweed in dry sites. For hummingbirds: red-flowering currant (Ribes sanguineum), orange honeysuckle (Lonicera ciliosa), columbine.

**California, Southwest, and dry West (zones 7 to 10)**. For bees: California buckwheat (Eriogonum fasciculatum), Cleveland sage (Salvia clevelandii), lavender (in many microclimates), brittlebush (Encelia farinosa). For monarchs: desert milkweed (Asclepias erosa) and showy milkweed. For hummingbirds: desert willow (Chilopsis linearis), fairy duster (Calliandra eriophylla), hummingbird sage.

**Texas, Oklahoma, and the South-Central (zones 6 to 9)**. For bees: blackfoot daisy (Melampodium leucanthum), mealy blue sage (Salvia farinacea), Mexican hat (Ratibida columnifera), Texas bluebonnet (Lupinus texensis). For monarchs: antelope horns milkweed (Asclepias asperula) and green milkweed (Asclepias viridis). For hummingbirds: standing cypress (Ipomopsis rubra), autumn sage (Salvia greggii).

## Milkweed specifically, because monarchs are the headline species

The monarch butterfly population has declined by roughly 90 percent over the last 25 years, and the central cause is the loss of milkweed in the Midwest, where monarchs bred each summer. The native milkweeds you can plant, by region:

- **Common milkweed** (Asclepias syriaca) — USDA zones 3 to 9, sun, wet-to-medium soil, eastern U.S. Spreads aggressively, so give it room or use a rhizome barrier.
- **Swamp milkweed** (Asclepias incarnata) — zones 3 to 9, moist soil, the easiest milkweed in most home gardens. The "Ice Ballet" and "Soulmate" cultivars from Proven Winners are reliable.
- **Butterfly weed** (Asclepias tuberosa) — zones 3 to 9, dry soil, full sun, orange flowers. The monarch caterpillar host plant that does not spread invasively. Native cultivars include "Hello Yellow."
- **Showy milkweed** (Asclepias speciosa) — zones 3 to 9, western U.S.
- **Antelope horns and green milkweed** — southern Plains, zones 5 to 9.

A common mistake: planting tropical milkweed (Asclepias curassavica). Tropical milkweed is sold widely at big-box stores because it blooms nonstop in pots. The problem is that it does not die back in winter the way native milkweeds do, which disrupts monarch migration by hosting them year-round. It also carries higher loads of a parasite (Ophryocystis elektroscirrha, or OE) that has been linked to monarch mortality. Stick to native milkweeds.

## Sun, soil, water — and the USDA zone question

Most pollinator-friendly plants need at least 6 hours of full sun. The shade tolerance ranges are narrow: foxglove (Digitalis), turtlehead (Chelone), and cardinal flower tolerate part shade; asters and goldenrods can handle afternoon shade but bloom less.

Soil type matters more than most garden guides admit. The native plant palette for heavy clay (most of the eastern U.S.) is different from the palette for sandy, dry soils (Plains, parts of the South). Pick plants that match your actual soil, not the idealized loam the plant tag assumes you have. A "clay-busting" 2-inch compost layer (Coast of Maine Quoddy Blend, $15 per cubic foot) raises most beds into a workable soil for natives.

USDA hardiness zones limit what survives your winters. Zone 6 (parts of Tennessee, Missouri, the mid-Atlantic) is the line where many native perennials start to struggle. Anything north of zone 4 (Minnesota, Maine, the Dakotas) requires careful plant selection — the pollinator palette narrows.

Watering is the biggest maintenance reality. Native plants, established, typically get by on rainfall east of the Mississippi and need supplemental water their first year. West of the 100th meridian, supplemental water is mandatory in summer for most plants.

## When to plant and how to plant

**Spring planting** (April–May in most regions) is the right answer for first-year survival. Plants installed in fall (September–October) catch fall moisture, develop roots through winter, and out-perform spring-planted stock in their first full summer. The exception is warm-season annuals (zinnia, cosmos, Mexican sunflower), which go in after last frost.

**Spacing**: natives spread to fill gaps. Plant at the spacing the tag suggests, not at the spacing the pot is. A 1-gallon plant that goes in at 18 inches on center will look sparse at install and full within 2 years.

**Soil prep**: less than you think. Most natives dislike heavily amended soil and respond with floppy, leggy growth when given rich fertilizer. A light 1-inch top-dressing of compost is plenty.

**Mulch**: shredded hardwood mulch, 2 inches, keeping it off the plant stems. Or better — leave the soil bare between plants and accept that weeds will colonize. Leaf mulch is the native-friendly choice in the East.

## The no-pesticide reality

A pollinator garden that gets sprayed defeats itself. The most common pesticide mistakes:

- **Neonicotinoid-treated nursery plants**: Proven Winners, Monrovia, and most retail nurseries have phased out neonics since 2018, but some small growers still treat their stock. The label rarely says so. Buying from a nursery that certifies neonic-free is the safer bet.
- **Mosquito sprays**: the standard yard mosquito fogger is broad-spectrum pyrethroid and kills bees and butterflies along with the mosquitoes. Avoid.
- **Systemic rose and shrub insecticides** (Bayer Advanced All-in-One and similar): these are applied to soil and taken up into the plant, contaminating nectar and pollen. Do not use on anything that blooms.
- **Even organic pesticides**: pyrethrin, spinosad, neem oil — all kill bees on contact. Treat only at dusk, when bees are not foraging, and only on the pest-affected plant, never as a blanket application.

For caterpillar pest pressure (especially on milkweed), hand-picking or accepting some loss is the pollinator-friendly answer. The same is true for aphids. If you spray for them, you remove the aphid-farming ant populations and the lacewings and ladybugs that depend on them.

## The bottom line

Buy plants that are native to your county, bloom across multiple seasons, include at least one milkweed if monarchs are a priority, and never spray the bed. A starter 4×8 bed of bee balm, coneflower, aster, and goldenrod will host dozens of bee species, dozens of butterfly species, and probably hummingbirds — for around $150 in plants and a Saturday of planting.
