A winter coat is the most expensive thing most people will put on their body in any given year. A $450 Canada Goose Expedition, a $1,400 Max Mara 101801, a $79 Uniqlo Ultra Light Down — each one is marketed as the answer, and none of them is. The answer depends on three things: where you live, how you move through it, and what your body looks like in silhouette.

We have spent more years than we would like to admit testing coats in real winters. The guide below cuts through the marketing, names the three categories that actually matter, gives you the numbers (fill power, wool weight, parka temperature ratings), and matches each to body type and budget. Nothing here is paid promotion. The brands are the brands we would buy or recommend to a friend.

## The three categories that actually matter

Almost every winter coat you will consider falls into one of three buckets: wool topcoats, down jackets, and parkas. Each solves a different problem, and pretending one can replace another is how people end up freezing in a beautiful wool coat or sweating in a parka at 35°F.

**Wool topcoats** are for cities, commutes, and dress. Think wool, cashmere, alpaca, or blends — woven fabric, not stuffed. They look polished with a suit or trousers. They do not keep you warm below about 25°F without heavy layering underneath. A $220 Aritzia Wilfred Cocoon or a $695 Toteme T-Lock Coat both fall here.

**Down jackets** are for cold but dry. Down is the fluffy under-feather of geese or ducks. It traps warm air and weighs almost nothing. A 600-fill down jacket is fine for 25–40°F; an 800-fill jacket can handle sub-zero. Down fails the moment it gets wet, which is where parkas come in. The $99 Uniqlo Ultra Light Down and the $279 Patagonia Down Sweater live in this category.

**Parkas** are for cold, wet, windy. A parka is a longer, often thigh- or knee-length jacket with a hood, usually insulated (with down or synthetic fill), and built to handle sleet, snow, and wind. The hood is the point. The $450 Canada Goose Expedition and the $220 Patagonia Tres 3-in-1 are parkas.

The mistake people make is buying the most expensive option in the wrong category. A $2,000 wool coat is useless in a Chicago February. A $400 down jacket soaked through is dangerous. Pick the category first, then the brand.

## Understanding fill power, the number that actually matters

If you buy down, fill power is the number to watch. It measures the loft of the down — how much space one ounce of down occupies in cubic inches. Higher fill power means more warmth for less weight.

- **500–600 fill**: budget down. Heavy, less warm per ounce. Common in entry-level jackets. Fine for moderate cold and short commutes.
- **600–700 fill**: the sweet spot. Light enough for everyday wear, warm enough for most winters. Patagonia's Down Sweater is 700-fill.
- **700–800 fill**: premium. Lighter, warmer, more compressible. You see this on $300+ jackets like the Arc'teryx Cerium.
- **800+ fill**: expedition. The best down, the lowest weight. Used in serious mountain gear and high-end jackets.

Fill power is not the same as fill weight. A jacket stuffed with 4 ounces of 800-fill down is warmer than 6 ounces of 600-fill. We see people confuse this constantly. A heavy down jacket is not automatically a warmer one — what matters is the loft-to-weight ratio of what is inside.

For most people in most cities, 650-fill is the minimum worth buying. Below that, you end up wearing a thick coat that does not keep you as warm as a lighter, higher-fill one.

## Wool, demystified: weight, weave, and warmth

Wool topcoats are sold by weight (in grams or ounces per linear yard) and by weave. Heavier is warmer but drapes worse. The sweet spot for most climates is 700–900 grams.

- **600–700 grams**: lightweight wool. Spring/fall or mild winter. Drapes beautifully. A $245 COS wool coat lives here.
- **800–1,000 grams**: the classic winter weight. Warm enough for 20–40°F with a sweater underneath. Most Aritzia, Toteme, and Theory coats land in this range.
- **1,000+ grams**: heavy melton or boiled wool. Substantial, warm, but stiff. This is the $1,400+ Max Mara territory.

Weave matters too. Worsted wool (smooth, tightly twisted) is dressier and more wind-resistant. Woolen or tweed (looser, more textured) is warmer and more casual. Cashmere blends soften the hand and add warmth without much weight, but they pill and need more care. Pure cashmere topcoats are lovely and not particularly durable — most are dry-clean-only and last five years of regular wear before looking tired.

A 90% wool / 10% cashmere blend is the most practical ratio. The wool holds the shape, the cashmere softens the touch. Anything under 70% wool and you are paying for marketing.

## Matching length to body type

Coat length is not a style preference — it is a geometry problem. The wrong length shortens you, adds visual weight where you do not want it, or makes a taller frame look like it is swimming in fabric.

- **Under 5'4"**: hip-length or higher. A knee-length coat on a short frame cuts the leg in half and looks borrowed. The Patagonia Down Sweater hits at the hip and works well. Avoid parkas that drop below the knee unless you are actually climbing something.
- **5'4" to 5'8"**: hip to mid-thigh. The most forgiving range. Most Aritzia, Madewell, and Everlane coats work here. Knee-length parkas (Canada Goose Mystique) work too.
- **5'8" to 6'0"**: mid-thigh to just below the knee. Long topcoats (Toteme, Max Mara) finally make sense. The extra length does not overwhelm your proportions.
- **Over 6'0"**: anything goes, but watch sleeve length. Most off-the-rack coats have 24–25" sleeves, which read cropped on a tall frame. Plan to alter.

Petite and plus lines exist for a reason. Aritzia makes a Wilfred line that runs slightly shorter. Toteme's fit is long by default. If you are between sizes, size up and have the sleeves shortened — shoulders are the one part you cannot easily let out.

## Care and longevity

A $450 coat that lasts 10 years is cheaper per year than a $150 coat that lasts three. Treat it accordingly.

Wool: brush weekly with a soft horsehair brush, hang on a broad wooden hanger, dry clean once a season (not monthly — over-dry-cleaning breaks down wool fibers). Spot-clean between visits.

Down: wash once a season in a front-loading machine on gentle, with a down-specific detergent like Nikwax Down Wash. Tumble dry on low with three clean tennis balls to break up clumps. Never store compressed.

Parkas: follow the same down rules. Re-treat the DWR (durable water repellent) finish every year or two with a spray-on product like Nikwax TX.Direct. The waterproofing wears off faster than the insulation fails.

Storage in summer: never in a plastic garment bag (traps moisture, breeds mildew). Use breathable cotton. Mothproof with cedar rings or lavender sachets — both work for a single season.

## The bottom line

Buy the category first, not the brand. Wool is for dress and city life, down is for cold and dry, parkas are for cold and wet. On down, do not pay for less than 650-fill. On wool, look for 80%+ wool and 700–900 gram weight. Match length to your height, not to the trend. A $200 Patagonia will outlast a $1,000 coat you bought for the label.
