The right TV depends on three things: how far you sit from the screen, what you actually watch, and how much ambient light your room has during the day. Spend $2,000 on a flagship OLED for a bright sun-flooded family room and you'll be squinting through reflections all weekend. Spend $400 on a budget LED for a dedicated home theater and you'll wonder why the blacks look gray. Match the panel to the room first, then worry about the spec sheet.

The category is also more crowded than it was five years ago. LG and Samsung still own the high end, but TCL and Hisense now ship genuinely competitive Mini-LED sets at half the price Sony charges for the equivalent size. Knowing which trade-offs matter — and which don't — saves you $500-1,000 without giving up the picture quality you actually use.

## Panel types, in plain language

Every modern TV is one of four panel types. They trade off black level, brightness, viewing angle, and price.

**OLED** (LG, Samsung, Sony, Panasonic) is self-emitting — every pixel makes its own light and can turn completely off. That's why OLED delivers "perfect" blacks with no halo or glow around bright objects. It's also why OLED sets are thin, light, and expensive. The 2024 LG C4 55" runs $1,299; the Samsung S90D 55" is $1,399; the flagship Sony Bravia 9 65" is $2,799. OLED is the pick for dim rooms, dark movies, and anyone who watches a lot of TV at night. The catch is brightness: peak HDR on most OLEDs tops out around 1,000-1,500 nits, which is plenty for indoor viewing but gets washed out by direct afternoon sun.

**QLED** (Samsung's branding, also used by TCL and Hisense) is a traditional LED panel with a quantum-dot layer that improves color. It can't match OLED's black level, but it gets brighter — Samsung's QN90D 65" hits 2,000+ nits, which is visible even in a sunny living room. Prices run from $700 (Hisense U7N 55") to $2,500 (Samsung QN90D 75"). QLED is the right pick for bright rooms, big rooms, and most living-room buyers who can't control the light.

**Mini-LED** is a subcategory of LED/LCD that uses thousands of tiny LEDs behind the panel instead of dozens. The smaller LEDs let the TV dim zones more precisely, which closes the gap to OLED on black level. Hisense's U8N, TCL's QM8, and Samsung's QN85D all use Mini-LED. A 65" Hisense U8N runs around $1,000; a 65" TCL QM8 is $900. Mini-LED is the value play for buyers who want near-OLED blacks without the OLED invoice.

**Standard LED** is everything else. Edge-lit LED sets are cheap ($300-500 for a 55"), look fine in a guest bedroom or kitchen, and start to wash out in any room with windows. The 2024 Hisense A6, TCL S4, and Vizio V-Series are all standard LED at budget prices. Buy these for secondary rooms and kids' playrooms, not for the main display.

## Size by viewing distance: the 1.5x rule

A 65" TV in a 10-foot room is too big for text-heavy work but perfect for movies. A 55" in the same room looks small once you upgrade to 4K content. The rule of thumb we use is the 1.5x multiplier: take your viewing distance in inches, multiply by 0.667, and that's your ideal screen size.

Eight feet (96 inches) viewing distance works out to a 60-65" screen. Ten feet (120 inches) lands at 75-85". Six feet (72 inches) sits at 48-50" — a good range for bedrooms and dens. Sitting closer than 1.5x the screen height still works, but you'll notice pixel structure on cheaper panels.

If you sit 7-9 feet from the screen and want one safe pick for a living room, the 65" size is the single most common today. The LG C4 65" is $1,999, the Samsung QN90D 65" is $1,499, the Hisense U8N 65" is $1,000, and the TCL QM8 65" is $900. Step up to 75" only if your room is large enough to swallow it.

## Refresh rate and motion handling

Refresh rate is how many times per second the panel redraws the image. 60Hz is the standard; 120Hz is the premium spec. For movies and most streaming content, 60Hz is fine. For sports, gaming, and any source that involves fast motion, 120Hz reduces blur noticeably.

A 120Hz panel also unlocks VRR (variable refresh rate) — the TV matches its refresh to the frame rate coming from the source, eliminating judder and screen tearing. VRR is now standard on PS5, Xbox Series X, and most gaming PCs over HDMI 2.1. Look for it if you game.

Most "120Hz" panels from budget brands are actually 60Hz panels with motion-interpolation software that fakes higher rates. The faked rate looks smooth but adds artifacts (the "soap opera effect") and input lag. A real 120Hz panel says so explicitly in the spec sheet, and it costs $200-400 more than the 60Hz equivalent.

If you watch a lot of broadcast sports or play competitive games, 120Hz is worth paying for. If you mostly watch Netflix and YouTube, save the money.

## HDR: HDR10, Dolby Vision, and HLG explained

HDR (high dynamic range) is the bigger story than 4K, and most TVs shipped in the last five years support it. There are three formats in active use:

- **HDR10.** The open standard every HDR TV supports. Static metadata — the TV gets one brightness/color range for the whole show and applies it everywhere. It's the floor.
- **Dolby Vision.** Dynamic metadata — the brightness and color are tuned scene by scene, or even frame by frame. Supported by most streaming services (Netflix, Disney+, Max, Apple TV+) and most 4K Blu-rays. Requires a license from Dolby, so some TVs skip it.
- **HLG (Hybrid Log-Gamma).** The broadcast-TV format. Live sports and over-the-air 4K broadcasts use it. Cheap to implement; rarely a deciding factor.

LG, Sony, Panasonic, Hisense, and TCL support both HDR10 and Dolby Vision. Samsung deliberately dropped Dolby Vision support starting in 2022, sticking with HDR10+ — Samsung's alternative dynamic-metadata format, backed by Panasonic and Philips. If you watch Netflix and want the format with the broadest content support, Dolby Vision is the safer bet. HDR10+ is fine if you're committed to Samsung.

Brightness determines how much HDR actually pops. An OLED at 1,000 nits produces a clean, sharp HDR image; a budget LED at 300 nits shows HDR with the highlights clipped and the colors flat. If HDR matters to you, aim for at least 700 nits peak brightness and 500+ for the sustained full-screen level. Anything below that and HDR is mostly a marketing label.

## Smart platforms: Google TV, Roku, Fire TV, Tizen, WebOS

Every new TV is also a streaming box. The smart platform determines what apps you have, how snappy the menus feel, and how often you have to talk to a remote.

**Google TV** (Sony, Hisense, TCL) is the most app-rich and the best at personalized recommendations. It pulls content from every service you subscribe to into one watchlist. Sony uses it across the lineup.

**Roku TV** (Roku's own branded sets, plus Hisense and TCL entry levels) is the simplest and most consistent. The remote has dedicated buttons for Netflix, Disney+, Apple TV+, and Hulu. Setup takes 90 seconds. Roku TVs are the pick for guest rooms, parents, and anyone who hates menus.

**Fire TV** (Amazon-branded Omni sets, plus Insignia and Toshiba) leans hard into Amazon content and ads but supports every major app. If you're already in the Alexa ecosystem, the integration is seamless.

**Tizen** (Samsung) and **WebOS** (LG) are the platforms built into the two biggest TV brands. Both are good — LG's WebOS has the Magic Remote with point-and-click, Samsung's Tizen has better Samsung-device integration. Either works.

Apple TV 4K ($130) or Roku Ultra ($100) external boxes fix most platform complaints if you don't love what your TV ships with. Worth it if you're already inside one of those ecosystems.

## The brand matrix: who makes what

**LG** owns the OLED category. The C4 ($1,299 55", $1,999 65") is the year's best midrange OLED; the G4 ($2,499 55", $3,299 65") is brighter and slimmer. LG's WebOS is one of the better smart platforms and the lineup has Dolby Vision and HDR10.

**Samsung** splits its lineup. The QN90D ($1,499 65", $2,499 75") is the bright-room Mini-LED flagship. The S90D ($1,399 55", $1,899 65") and S95D ($2,599 55", $3,499 65") are OLEDs that compete head-to-head with LG. Note: no Dolby Vision on any 2022+ Samsung — HDR10+ only.

**Sony** is the picture-processing specialist. The Bravia 7 ($1,899 65", $2,799 75") and Bravia 9 ($2,799 65", $3,999 75") Mini-LED sets use Google's smart platform and have the best motion handling in the category. Sony's XR processor is the gold standard for upscaling lower-resolution content.

**TCL** is the value leader. The Q6 ($450 55") covers the budget. The QM8 ($900 65", $1,300 75") is the Mini-LED pick — it competes with sets twice its price. TCL also builds the panels for Roku TVs.

**Hisense** is the second value player. The U7N ($700 55", $1,000 65") is the midrange sweet spot. The U8N ($1,000 65", $1,400 75") is the Mini-LED dark-horse winner — frequently recommended over the Samsung QN85D at the same size and a much lower price. The U9N is the flagship at $2,200 for 75".

**Vizio** is the U.S. budget pick. The V-Series ($300 50", $400 55") is the cheapest 4K HDR set worth buying. The M-Series QLED ($500 55", $700 65") adds quantum dots for better color. Vizio's smart platform has been inconsistent, so factor in a $100 streaming stick if you buy one.

**Roku TV** (the standalone brand) makes TVs in 24" through 75" sizes with the simplest interface on the market. The Plus Series ($400 55", $600 65") is the QLED step up. Roku TVs are the pick when a parent or grandparent will be using the remote.

## How to pick the right TV for you

- **Bright living room, mixed use, big screen:** Samsung QN90D 65" ($1,499) or Hisense U8N 65" ($1,000).
- **Dim theater room, movies and prestige TV:** LG C4 55" ($1,299) or Sony Bravia 8 55" ($1,499). OLED.
- **Bedroom or guest room, on a budget:** Hisense A6 55" ($280) or TCL S4 55" ($250).
- **Bright room, big budget, want it all:** LG G4 65" ($3,299) or Sony Bravia 9 65" ($2,799).
- **Secondary set, kids' playroom:** Vizio V-Series 43" ($220), mount on the wall, done.

## The bottom line

Match the panel to the room: OLED for dim and dark spaces, Mini-LED or QLED for bright living rooms, standard LED for everything secondary. Buy the largest screen you can afford at the 1.5x viewing-distance rule, get a real 120Hz panel if you game or watch sports, and don't pay extra for a smart platform you don't love — a $100 streaming stick fixes any complaint.
