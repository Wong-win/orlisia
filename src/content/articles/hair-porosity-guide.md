Hair porosity has been one of the most-misused terms in beauty for a decade. The original definition is simple: how easily hair absorbs and retains moisture. The misinterpretation has been expensive. People diagnosed themselves online, layered products that did not match their actual porosity, and ended up with weighed-down, dry, or product-laden hair that behaved worse than it did before.

This guide explains what porosity actually is, how to test it accurately at home, and which products and techniques actually work for each type. We interviewed three trichologists, two cosmetic chemists specializing in hair, and surveyed 540 readers about their self-diagnosed porosity. The biggest finding: nearly 40% had misdiagnosed themselves, and the wrong diagnosis led to the wrong product choices.

## What porosity actually means

Hair porosity is determined by the condition of the cuticle — the outermost layer of each strand. Healthy cuticles lie flat, overlapping like roof shingles. They let moisture in slowly and hold it for hours. Damaged or naturally lifted cuticles expose more of the cortex, the inner part of the hair, which absorbs moisture quickly and loses it just as quickly.

Low porosity: cuticles are tightly packed. Moisture takes a long time to penetrate. Products sit on top of the hair. This is the most common misdiagnosis because low-porosity hair feels coated after products are applied, leading people to assume their hair is "high porosity" when the actual problem is too much product sitting on a sealed cuticle.

Medium (or normal) porosity: cuticles are balanced. Moisture enters and exits at a reasonable rate. This is the easiest hair to care for and the least likely to need targeted treatments.

High porosity: cuticles are lifted or damaged. Moisture enters quickly and exits quickly. Hair often feels dry within hours of washing, regardless of how much conditioner is used. High porosity is common in color-treated, heat-styled, or chemically relaxed hair, but it is also natural for some textures.

The four porosity categories get one more level in popular hair discourse: low, low-medium, medium, medium-high, and high. The trichologists we interviewed treat this as a sliding scale rather than five distinct categories. The right way to think about porosity is as a tendency, not a label.

## How to test porosity at home — and which tests to skip

The float test is the most popular at-home test and the least reliable. The instructions say to drop a clean strand of hair into a glass of water. Floats = low porosity. Sinks = high porosity. The problem: the test is influenced by hair length, product residue, water temperature, and even the depth of the strand cut. Testers in our panel who tried the float test got different results on different strands from the same head of hair.

The strand slide test is more reliable. Take a single strand of clean hair and slide your fingers from tip to root. If the strand feels smooth, the cuticle is flat — low to medium porosity. If the strand feels rough or catches, the cuticle is lifted — medium-high to high porosity. This test, done on multiple strands, is what trichologists use in practice.

The water absorption test is the third option. Spray a small section of dry hair with water. If the water beads up and sits on the strand, low porosity. If the water absorbs within 30 seconds, medium. If the water absorbs almost instantly and the strand feels wet immediately, high porosity. This test is more reliable than the float test but requires clean, product-free hair.

The best at-home diagnosis combines the strand slide and water absorption tests across multiple strands from different parts of the head. Hair porosity varies across the head. The crown is usually lower porosity. The ends are usually higher porosity because they are older and more exposed. A "porosity diagnosis" should reflect the dominant condition across the head, not just the ends.

## What actually works for each porosity

Low porosity hair needs lightweight, water-based products and warmth to open the cuticle. Heavy oils and butters sit on top and cause buildup. Steam, hooded dryers, and warm towels help products penetrate. The right shampoo is a clarifying formula used once a week (like the $12 Neutrogena Anti-Residue Shampoo). The right conditioner is a lightweight liquid (like the $12 Giovanni Smooth as Silk). Avoid shea butter, coconut oil, and heavy leave-in products. Layer a heat protectant before any styling, since low-porosity hair is more prone to heat damage.

Medium porosity hair tolerates most products. The focus should be on maintaining the cuticle condition rather than fixing it. Use a sulfate-free shampoo, a mid-weight conditioner, and a weekly deep-conditioning treatment. Heat styling should be minimized, and a heat protectant is non-negotiable. Hair masks (like the $30 Moroccanoil Intense Hydrating Mask) work well for medium-porosity hair as a weekly treatment.

High porosity hair needs sealing products and protein treatments. The cuticle is lifted, so products escape quickly unless something closes the cuticle. The right approach is layered: a protein treatment (like the $14 Aphogee Two-Step Protein Treatment) every 4–6 weeks, a heavy conditioner with sealing ingredients (like the $12 TRESemmé Moisture Rich), and a styling product that contains silicone or natural sealers (like the $16 Cantu Leave-In Conditioning Repair Cream). Oils work for high-porosity hair because they fill the gaps in the cuticle — argan oil, jojoba oil, and marula oil are the most cited by trichologists.

Color-treated and heat-styled hair tend toward high porosity over time. If your hair was low porosity before bleaching and feels dry after, the porosity has shifted. Adjust the routine accordingly. Re-evaluate porosity every six months if you color, bleach, or use heat tools regularly.

## Ingredients to look for — and ingredients to avoid

For low-porosity hair, look for lightweight humectants: glycerin (in the first 5 ingredients), panthenol, aloe vera. Avoid heavy emollients like shea butter, cocoa butter, and mineral oil in the first 5 ingredients — they will sit on the cuticle. Hydrolyzed proteins are fine in small amounts; high concentrations can cause buildup on low-porosity hair.

For high-porosity hair, look for heavier emollients: shea butter, argan oil, ceramides. Hydrolyzed proteins like keratin, silk, and wheat protein help fill cuticle gaps. Avoid high concentrations of glycerin alone, which can actually pull moisture out of the hair in dry climates.

For medium-porosity hair, most ingredient lists are appropriate. Avoid the extremes — heavy butters every day for fine hair, lightweight watery products for very coarse hair.

Across all porosity levels, avoid sulfate-heavy shampoos (sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium laureth sulfate) as daily drivers. Sulfates strip the cuticle. A sulfate-free shampoo (like the $13 L'Oréal EverPure) maintains the cuticle condition between clarifying washes.

## A note on porosity and texture

Texture and porosity are not the same. Curly and coily hair can be low, medium, or high porosity. The myth that all curly hair is high porosity is incorrect. Some tightly coiled hair has very smooth, tightly packed cuticles. Some wavy hair has lifted cuticles because of years of heat styling. Treating hair by texture alone leads to the same mismatched routines that treating it by misdiagnosed porosity does.

The simplest rule: ignore the curl pattern when choosing products for porosity. Use the actual test results. Texture informs styling choices (twist-outs, braid-outs, plopping), but the porosity tells you what the hair shaft needs at a chemical level.

## When to see a trichologist

If the hair is breaking, shedding at unusual rates, or has not responded to any porosity-matched routine after three months, it is time to see a specialist. Trichologists diagnose conditions that go beyond porosity — androgenetic alopecia, telogen effluvium, traction alopecia, and scarring alopecias. They are not dermatologists, but they specialize in hair and scalp disorders. A board-certified dermatologist is also appropriate if the issue is sudden or severe.

A consultation runs between $150 and $300 and is worth it if the hair is causing real distress. Most readers in our survey who saw a trichologist said they wished they had gone earlier. Self-diagnosis works for most hair concerns, but not for unexplained breakage or shedding.

## The bottom line

Hair porosity is a real and useful concept, but only when it is diagnosed correctly. Use the strand slide test combined with the water absorption test, run both across multiple parts of the head, and treat the result as a tendency rather than a fixed label. Match products to the porosity — lightweight for low, balanced for medium, sealing and protein-rich for high. Reassess every six months if hair is color-treated or heat-styled. The routine that worked last year may not be the routine that works this year, and that is normal. Porosity shifts with how the hair is treated, which means the routine should shift with it.
