Raised beds solve three problems at once: they let you garden in soil you didn't choose, they bring the work up to a height that doesn't wreck your back, and they warm up two to three weeks earlier in spring than ground-level beds. The mistake most first-time builders make is picking a kit at the garden center without thinking through the material, the depth, or what they're actually going to grow. We built nine raised beds over the last decade in three different USDA hardiness zones (5b in Denver, 6b in Pittsburgh, 8a in coastal North Carolina), and the same rules kept showing up. Material determines lifespan, depth determines what you can grow, and soil mix determines whether the bed produces or sulks. This guide covers each decision in order.

## Materials: The Five That Work (and the One That Doesn't)

Five materials dominate the residential raised-bed market. Each has a real lifespan, a real cost, and a real failure mode.

**Cedar.** The default choice, and the right one for most buyers. Western red cedar and Eastern white cedar both contain natural oils that resist rot — the same chemistry that makes cedar fence posts last 15 to 20 years in contact with soil. Untreated cedar raised beds last 10 to 15 years in most climates. The Greenes Fence Original Cedar Raised Bed (4×4×10.5 inches, $129) and the Greenes 4×8×10.5 inch version ($199) are the two most-bought kits in the country. Cedar Built (Cedar Built Vermont, $300-$700 for a 4×8 bed) makes heavier-duty cedar beds with thicker boards and stainless steel hardware for buyers who want a 20-year lifespan. The trap: cheap cedar beds at big-box stores often use ⅝-inch or ¾-inch boards rather than the standard 1.5-inch thickness, and they fail at the joints within 5 years.

**Douglas fir.** The budget alternative to cedar. Douglas fir lacks cedar's natural rot resistance, so untreated Douglas fir beds last 5 to 8 years rather than 15. It's stronger than cedar per board inch, so Douglas fir beds can use thinner boards and still hold soil weight. The Veikous cedar-tone Douglas fir raised beds ($80-$150 at Amazon) are the most-bought kits in the budget tier. Treated Douglas fir (ACQ or copper azole, not the older CCA) lasts 15+ years, but many organic gardeners avoid it because of the copper leaching concern — modern treatments are rated safe for food crops by the EPA, but the perception persists.

**Composite lumber (Trex and similar).** The long-lifespan choice. Composite boards made of recycled plastic and wood fiber last 25-plus years without any maintenance. Trex raised beds run $400-$700 for a 4×8 kit. The downside is heat: in full sun, composite beds can warm the soil 5-10°F above ambient, which is fine for peppers and tomatoes but stresses lettuces and greens in mid-summer. Composite is also the most expensive common option. Frame stiffness is excellent, which means taller beds (18-24 inches) hold their shape without bowing.

**Corrugated metal.** The modern look, especially in galvanized or weathered zinc finishes. Corrugated metal beds (Veikous galvanized kit, $90-$200; Gardener's Supply Lifetime metal beds, $200-$500) last 20-plus years and warm the soil early in spring — a real advantage in Zones 4-6. The downsides: metal edges can cut hands during assembly, the corners need reinforcement to hold soil weight at depth over 18 inches, and metal beds heat up dramatically in summer afternoon sun. Roots touching the metal sidewall can scorch in mid-summer. Line the inside walls with landscape fabric or a foam board to insulate.

**Cinder block.** The permanent choice. Concrete block raised beds last 30-plus years and can be configured to any dimension. A 4×8 cinder block bed costs $80-$150 in blocks alone, plus $50-$100 in mortar if you want a permanent structure. The advantage is thermal mass: cinder block beds moderate soil temperature swings, which is helpful in zones that swing from cold nights to hot days. The disadvantage is alkalinity — fresh concrete leaches lime into the soil, raising pH by 0.5-1.0 unit initially. This is fine for most vegetables but problematic for acid-loving plants (blueberries, azaleas). Wait 2-3 months before planting, or amend with sulfur.

**What doesn't work.** Pressure-treated lumber made before 2003 used CCA (chromated copper arsenate), which is genuinely toxic for food crops. Modern ACQ-treated lumber is rated safe by the EPA, but most organic gardeners still avoid it. Old railroad ties creosote-leach into soil for years and shouldn't be used for edible beds. Pallet wood unless certified heat-treated (HT stamp) can carry invasive pests and pathogens.

## Dimensions: Width, Length, and Depth

The dimensions of a raised bed determine what grows well and how comfortable the work is.

**Width.** The single most important dimension. A bed should be no wider than 4 feet if you can reach from both sides (no walking in the bed), or 2 feet if you can only reach from one side (against a wall or fence). Anything wider forces you to step into the bed, which compacts the soil and defeats the purpose. The 4-foot width is the standard, and 4×8 is the most common residential bed because it accommodates standard lumber lengths (no waste).

**Length.** Practically unlimited, but consider material and access. A 4×8 bed can be built from two 8-foot boards (no waste). A 4×12 bed uses three boards. Anything longer than 12 feet starts to bow outward at the sides under soil pressure; add a mid-span cross-brace every 8 feet to prevent bowing.

**Depth.** Determines what you can grow and how much soil you're buying. The standard 10.5-inch depth works for shallow-rooted crops (lettuce, herbs, radishes, strawberries, spinach). A 16-18 inch depth allows tomatoes, peppers, beans, and most root vegetables (carrots, beets). A 24-inch depth is necessary for asparagus, large root vegetables (parsnips, long carrots), and gardeners who don't want to bend at all. Each additional 4 inches of depth in a 4×8 bed adds 10.7 cubic feet of soil — about 13 bags of 1 cubic foot potting mix at $7 each, or roughly $90-$120 of soil per bed at the 16-inch depth.

**Height for accessibility.** For gardeners with mobility limits, a 24-30 inch bed with a 4-foot width allows working from a seated position. Cedar Built makes 30-inch tall cedar beds ($450-$650) for this purpose, and composite kits at the same height run $600-$900.

## Soil Mix: The Recipe That Works

The cheapest mistake in raised bed gardening is filling the bed with bagged "garden soil" or topsoil. These products compact within a season, drain poorly, and don't feed the plants. A real raised bed mix is a three-part recipe.

**The standard mix (Mel's Mix-style).** 50% compost, 30% coarse vermiculite or perlite, 20% peat moss or coco coir. By volume, not by weight. A 4×8×16-inch bed needs roughly 32 cubic feet of mix — about 16 cubic feet of compost (8-12 bags at $5-$10 each), 10 cubic feet of vermiculite (3-4 bags at $20 each), and 6 cubic feet of peat or coir (3 bags at $15 each). Total soil cost for one 4×8 bed: $200-$280. This mix drains well, holds moisture, feeds the plants through the compost, and stays loose for years.

**The budget mix.** 60% topsoil, 30% compost, 10% perlite. Cheaper by half ($80-$120 per bed), but it compacts faster and holds less water. Fine for first-year budgets; upgrade the next season.

**The lasagna method.** For very deep beds (24+ inches), fill the bottom 8-12 inches with logs, branches, leaves, and straw. This is the "hugelkultur" technique — the woody material breaks down slowly, retains moisture, and reduces the soil cost dramatically. Top with 12-16 inches of the standard mix above. Works well but takes 2-3 years to fully settle.

## Drainage: The Most-Skipped Step

Raised beds drain better than ground-level gardens by default, but two drainage mistakes show up regularly.

**No drain layer.** Don't put gravel or rocks at the bottom of the bed. The traditional advice to add 2-3 inches of gravel actually impedes drainage by creating a perched water table. The Mel's Mix composition above drains well on its own.

**Bed sitting on impermeable surface.** If the bed sits on concrete or heavily compacted clay, drill ½-inch drainage holes through the bottom boards (if wooden) or add a 2-inch gravel layer beneath the bed (if on concrete). Most raised beds on soil drain naturally — the existing soil wicks the water away.

**For metal beds on concrete.** Drill 4-6 half-inch holes in the bottom edge of the metal siding to allow drainage. Without them, water pools and rots roots.

## What to Grow by Zone

Different raised bed depths work for different crops. A practical breakdown:

**Shallow beds (10.5 inches).** Lettuce, spinach, kale, arugula, herbs (basil, parsley, cilantro), radishes, strawberries, green onions. Most annual flowers. Plant in Zones 3-10. These crops mature fast (lettuce in 30-50 days) and tolerate the cooler soil of a shallow bed.

**Medium beds (16-18 inches).** Tomatoes, peppers, bush beans, pole beans, cucumbers, summer squash, carrots (short varieties), beets, broccoli, cauliflower. The most versatile depth for most home gardeners. Plant in Zones 4-10, with proper season timing.

**Deep beds (24-30 inches).** Asparagus (permanent for 15+ years), long carrots, parsnips, artichokes, rhubarb, sweet potatoes. The deeper root zone protects against soil temperature swings and allows the longer-season crops.

**What to grow by zone.** Zones 3-5: focus on cold-hardy crops — kale, spinach, peas, broccoli, carrots, beets. Use deep beds to buffer soil temperature swings. Zones 6-7: the most versatile — almost any annual crop grows well with proper season timing. Zones 8-10: focus on heat-tolerant crops — peppers, okra, eggplant, southern peas, sweet potatoes. Provide afternoon shade in Zones 9-10.

**Budget ($80-$200 per 4×8 kit).** Veikous galvanized metal kits ($90-$180), Greenes Fence economy cedar kits ($80-$150), Best Choice Products cedar-tone kits ($80-$120). Frames last 5-10 years. The right pick for first-time builders who want to test raised bed gardening before upgrading.

**Mid-tier ($200-$500 per 4×8 kit).** Greenes Fence Original Cedar ($199-$280), Gardener's Supply Company cedar and metal kits ($250-$450), Cedar Built Vermont ($300-$700), Lifetime composite kits ($300-$500). Frames last 10-20 years. The right pick for gardeners committed to the practice.

**Premium ($500-$1,200 per 4×8 kit).** Cedar Built premium cedar ($600-$900), Gardener's Supply elevated beds for accessibility ($500-$1,200), custom Trex composite builds ($700-$1,200). Frames last 20-30 years. The right pick for permanent installations and accessibility needs.

## The bottom line

A raised bed is cedar or galvanized metal at the right depth for what you want to grow, filled with the standard three-part soil mix (50% compost, 30% vermiculite, 20% peat or coir), placed where the sun reaches it for six-plus hours a day, and built at 4 feet wide so you never step in it. Spend the $200 on the soil mix before you spend the $200 on the frame — the soil is what produces the vegetables, the frame is what holds the soil.
