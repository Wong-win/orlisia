Raised beds get sold on the frame, and the growing happens somewhere else entirely. The frame is the cheap part. Depth decides whether you get a productive bed or a trough of clay; the fill decides whether it survives year three. Here is the order: depth, soil volume, frame cost, soil mix, drainage, and planting.

## The depth decision

Depth is the hardest decision to change, because cost scales with it: every extra inch across a 4 by 8 frame is 2.7 cubic feet of soil.

### 6 inches: salad and herbs

**Six inches** holds 16 cubic feet in a 4 by 8 frame. It works for salad and herb beds, strawberry beds, and patio beds; it does not work for tomatoes, peppers, squash, asparagus, or parsnips. A 6-inch bed in zone 7 needs water every second day in July.

### 12 inches: the default that feeds a household

**Twelve inches** is 32 cubic feet in a 4 by 8 frame, and it handles nearly everything a household eats except asparagus and parsnips. It works for the crops that fill a weekly grocery run — tomatoes, beans, cucumbers, brassicas, roots, and greens. It does not work for a tomato without a stake, parsnips, or potatoes you hill 4 inches over.

### 18 inches: root crops and no bending

**Eighteen inches** is 48 cubic feet — the set-it-and-forget-it depth for tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, and staked cucumbers, and the minimum for parsnips and sweet potatoes. It works for deep-rooted crops, trellised melons and zucchini, and anyone who cannot bend. It does not work in tight gardens: it holds 50 percent more soil in the same footprint.

### The root-depth table

| Crop | Rooting depth | Minimum bed depth |
|---|---|---|
| Radish, lettuce, spinach, arugula | 6 to 8 in | 6 in |
| Cucumber, kale, potatoes, chard | 12 to 14 in | 12 in |
| Tomato, trellised cucumber | 18 to 24 in | 12 in staked, 18 in unstoned |
| Parsnips, long carrots, sweet potato | 18 to 24 in | 18 in |

**Minimum bed depth is not root depth.** A tomato's roots reach 24 inches, but 90 percent of the active mass sits in the top 12.

## The soil volume math

Soil is sold by the cubic foot and bought by the bag. **One cubic foot is 28.3 litres.** A 4 by 8 bed at 12 inches holds 32 cubic feet, or 906 litres.

| Bed footprint | 6 in deep | 12 in deep | 18 in deep |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 by 4 ft | 8 cu ft / 226 L | 16 cu ft / 453 L | 24 cu ft / 679 L |
| 4 by 8 ft | 16 cu ft / 453 L | 32 cu ft / 906 L | 48 cu ft / 1,358 L |

### The worked example: a 4 by 8 bed at 12 inches

**Bags.** Two-cubic-foot bags are the standard format. **Miracle-Gro Nature's Blend** and **Kalgro** run $8 to $12 per bag, **Espoma Organic Potting Mix** $12 to $18. Thirty-two cubic feet is 16 bags — $160 at $10 a bag.

**Bulk.** A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so 32 cubic feet is about 1.2 yards. Screened topsoil runs $28 to $45 per yard, compost $40 to $70. Mixed on site, it costs **$60 to $110**.

**The trap**: bagged "garden soil" is mostly compost and peat at $30 to $40 per 3-cubic-foot bag, so 32 cubic feet runs $350 to $430 and shrinks by half.

## Materials and cost

### Cedar

**Cedar** is the default. Western red and Northern white cedar carry rot-resistant oils, so a bed lasts 12 to 15 years. For a 4 by 8 kit: **Greenes Fence Original Cedar** at **$199 to $279**, **Olympic Technology** at **$189 to $240**, and **Cedar Built Vermont** at **$450 to $750**.

**Where it works**: any climate, and any bed you might rebuild.

**Where it does not work**: as a 30-year build — cedar cups if the bed dries out.

### Galvanized steel

**Galvanized steel** is corrugated zinc-coated steel, the same material as roofing and corral panels. It lasts 20 to 30 years. For a 4 by 8 kit: **Gardener's Supply Lifetime** at **$300 to $400**, **VegBed** at **$340 to $460**, and **Neudorf** at **$520 to $700**.

**Where it works**: zones 3 to 6 in spring, where steel warms the soil 2 to 4 weeks earlier than cedar.

**Where it does not work**: unlined in zones 8 to 10 — a dark steel bed in a Phoenix summer pushes soil past 130°F. Line the interior with cardboard.

### Composite

**Composite** lumber, recycled plastic and wood fiber, is the 25-year answer. A 4 by 8 kit runs **$700 to $1,050** from **Trex** and **$280 to $380** for the **Lifetime Composite** bed.

**Where it works**: beds you build once, and accessible beds.

**Where it does not work**: a tight budget — composite runs 5 to 10°F above ambient soil temperature, which stresses lettuce in July.

### Corner kits and hardware

Beds fail at the corners, not the boards. Predrilled cedar corner post kits run **$25 to $45 per set of four**, and stainless L-brackets **$6 to $14 each**. Anchor every corner with rebar at **$3 to $8 per stake**.

## The soil mix

### The 5:3:2 recipe

**5 parts compost, 3 parts screened topsoil, 2 parts soilless amendment**, by volume:

- **5 parts compost** for nutrition and biology — screened compost from **Worm Castings**, **Black Gold**, or a municipal source.
- **3 parts screened topsoil** for the mineral fraction and the weight that keeps the mix from collapsing.
- **2 parts soilless amendment** — perlite for drainage, vermiculite for moisture, coco coir for water holding.

For a 4 by 8 bed at 12 inches: 16 cubic feet of compost (0.6 yard, $25 to $45), 10 of topsoil (0.37 yard, $12 to $20), 6 of amendment ($30 to $45). **Total: $70 to $110 in bulk.**

### Top-dressing an existing bed

If the bed is already full, **top-dress 6 to 8 inches of the 5:3:2 mix over it** — roots move up within a season, at a third of the cost of a full replacement.

### What not to use

- **Fresh manure.** High in ammonium and full of weed seed. Compost it 6 months or more.
- **Construction fill or site topsoil.** It may be 30 percent debris and can carry lead.
- **Fresh sawdust or bark fines mixed in.** They tie up nitrogen. Use as mulch only.
- **Play sand.** No structure — it forms a cement-like slab.
- **Gravel at the bottom.** It creates a perched water table at the root line.
- **Bagged potting mix as the whole bed.** It costs $220 to $320 and compacts fast.

## Drainage and liners

**If the bed sits on soil, it needs no liner** — the ground underneath is your drainage. On concrete, pavers, or compacted clay you need a plan, because water that cannot leave collects at the bottom and the lowest 6 inches go anaerobic.

### Landscape fabric vs cardboard vs nothing

- **Nothing (on soil)**: best and free — the soil profile stays connected to the ground.
- **Cardboard** (2 to 4 layers of corrugated): best on a hard surface. It smothers weeds for 12 to 18 months, then breaks down.
- **Landscape fabric** (4 to 5 oz non-woven, **DeWitt Pro 5**): a **sidewall liner only**, never the bottom, where it creates a perched water table.
- **Never plastic sheeting.** It traps water and cooks the bed in summer.

### Drainage holes

On a hard surface, drill **four to six half-inch holes** through the bottom boards, 12 to 18 inches apart and angled downhill. On a metal bed, drill the bottom edge of the sidewall instead. Put pea gravel *underneath* the bed — never inside it — so the holes do not clog.

### Compaction over the years

A newly filled bed **settles 3 to 6 inches in its first year**: a 24-inch bed drops to 18, a 12-inch bed to 8. Fill a 12-inch bed as though it were 16 inches, or top-dress with compost every spring.

## What to plant, and when

![A gardener in gloves using a red trowel to plant a seedling in soil outdoors](https://images.pexels.com/photos/27176060/pexels-photo-27176060.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1400)

Raised beds run 2 to 3 weeks ahead of ground-level beds in spring because the side walls absorb the day's heat overnight.

### Cool-season crops: your first and last planting

**Cool-season crops** grow between 40°F and 70°F, and they take the space twice — spring and fall — when nothing else will.

- **Spring**: leaf lettuce (30 to 45 days), spinach, arugula, radish, sugar snap peas (60 to 70), and cilantro.
- **Fall**: kale (55 to 80), Swiss chard, spinach, arugula, radicchio, and a second broccoli round in zones 6 and warmer.

Transplant brassicas into a 6 to 8 inch bed, direct-sow into a 12-inch bed.

### Warm-season crops: the long middle of the year

**Warm-season crops** need soil above 60°F to germinate and 70°F to set fruit, so plant them after the last frost.

- **Tomatoes**: 18 to 24 inches of rooting depth, 5 to 6 feet staked, 18 to 24 inches apart. In a 12-inch bed, run two rows 24 inches apart.
- **Peppers**: 14 to 18 inches of depth, and the crop most improved by a light-colored mulch.
- **Cucumbers, zucchini, and beans**: 12 to 18 inches plus a 6-foot trellis. One 4 by 8 trellis produces 40 to 60 pounds a season.

### Succession planting

**Succession planting** is how a 4 by 8 bed produces for 120 days instead of 40. For a 12-inch bed in zone 6:

1. **Early April**: lettuce and radish. Harvest late May.
2. **Late May**: cucumber transplants along the north side, trellised. Harvest July to September.
3. **July**: bush beans, then fall spinach and arugula in the vacated rows. Harvest September and October.
4. **All season**: one tomato, one pepper, one basil, each in the same square foot.

## What it costs per season

For a 4 by 8 foot bed, mid-2026 U.S. pricing:

| Item | Cedar | Galvanized steel | Composite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | $199 to $279 | $300 to $400 | $280 to $1,050 |
| Seeds, hardware, drip line | $115 to $195 | $115 to $195 | $115 to $195 |
| **Year one total** | **$385 to $585** | **$485 to $705** | **$465 to $1,355** |

From year two, one bed costs **$55 to $95 a year**: seeds $25 to $40, compost $20 to $35, drip fittings $10 to $20.

### What you get back

University extension guidance puts the conservative floor at roughly **$0.60 per square foot per season** for a beginner's garden, which on 32 square feet is about $19. With succession planting and high-value crops, a well-run 4 by 8 bed produces **$80 to $150** a season — paying the bed back in **three to four seasons**.

Budget **1 to 2 gallons a day per 4 by 8 bed** in peak summer, and **4 to 6 hours a week in season** for the work.

## The mistakes

### Under-buying the soil

A bed filled with half native clay and half bagged mix compacts within a season and produces a 6-inch root system in a 12-inch box. Buy the full volume.

### The wrong light

A bed needs **6 hours of direct sun minimum**, and 8 to 10 for tomatoes and peppers. Watch the site across a week in July.

### Expecting the bed not to settle

Beds drop 3 to 6 inches in year one. Never top-dress a 12-inch bed and you will garden in 8 inches of settled soil by year two.

### Overwatering and bare soil

Raised beds dry out faster than ground beds, and an unmulched surface loses moisture and forms a crust that resists germination. Check 3 inches down before you water, and lay 2 inches of straw.

### Anchoring the frame

An 18-inch bed with wet soil weighs about 1,000 pounds and will shift and dump the soil on a slope. Stake the corners.

## The bottom line

Decide the depth first, because it sets the soil bill and the crop list: 6 inches for salad and herbs, 12 inches for the crops a household eats, 18 inches for tomatoes, roots, and anyone who cannot bend. Buy the full 32 cubic feet for a 4 by 8 bed at 12 inches and mix it yourself at 5 parts compost, 3 parts topsoil, 2 parts perlite or coir for $70 to $110. Cedar at $199 to $279 is the right frame for most people; galvanized steel at $300 to $400 buys 25 years. Line the sides, drill drainage holes on concrete, and top-dress with compost every spring.
