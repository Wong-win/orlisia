Every laundry room carries the same burden: it has to disappear when not in use and perform the moment you open the door. A good makeover doesn't turn the room into a magazine spread; it makes the unglamorous work faster, quieter, and easier on your back.

We've worked through enough laundry rooms to know the patterns that work and the ones that don't. The walls can be as plain as you want, but the appliances, the counter, the sink, the light, and the airflow have to be right. Below is the framework we use when planning a laundry room from scratch or rethinking an existing one.

## Start with the layout, not the appliances

The order of decisions matters more than the brand choices. Lock the layout before you order machines.

**Side-by-side front loaders** (LG WM6500, Samsung WF53, Maytag MHW) take about 27 inches wide each, plus 1 to 2 inches of clearance on each side and 1 inch at the back. Plan 60 to 64 inches for a flush built-in.

**Stacked front loaders** (LG WashCombo, Samsung Bespoke AI, Electrolux) cut the footprint to roughly 27 by 34 inches but reduce drying capacity by about 30%. The trade-off is worth it only if the laundry room is under 30 square feet or you need a closet-door appliance.

**Side-by-side top loaders** (Speed Queen, Maytag Commercial) are taller and deeper — about 30 by 32 inches — but they finish a normal load faster than most front loaders and need no bending to load. For households that wash six or more loads a week, a top-loader pair is the practical pick.

The room needs 36 inches of clear floor in front of the machines. Anything less, and you'll hate doing laundry forever.

## Three layouts that actually work

**The wall layout (5x6 feet minimum).** Both machines on one wall, counter above at 36 inches with cabinetry, drying rack on the opposite wall. This is the easiest layout to build and works for nearly every room.

**The L layout (6x7 feet or more).** Washer on one leg, dryer on the other, with a corner counter or sink that bridges them. Better workflow than a single-wall setup because you can move wet clothes in a straight line from washer to dryer to folding surface.

**The closet layout (under 5x3 feet).** Stack only — no counter, no sink. Use a curtain or sliding barn door (Crestview Doors hardware runs $110 to $220 per door) to hide the pair. This is the right call only when the laundry is a dedicated closet for a one- or two-person household.

If you have plumbing in an outside wall, keep the machines on that wall — moving supply and drain lines more than 4 feet costs $400 to $900 in a renovation and quickly erases the budget for nicer finishes.

## The counter and folding station

A laundry counter earns its space in the first week. The standard depth is 25 to 28 inches — enough to fold a queen sheet laid flat — and the standard height is 36 inches. If you're 5-foot-4 or under, drop the height to 34 inches.

**Quartz** (Cambria, Caesarstone, MSI) is the right pick for almost everyone. Expect $60 to $90 per square foot installed for a mid-grade pattern. Specific styles we like: Cambria Brittanicca Warm, Caesarstone Cloudburst, and MSI Calacatta Idillio. Quartz wipes clean, takes a hot iron set down for a few seconds, and shows nothing.

**Butcher block** (John Boos, IKEA Numerär) is cheaper — about $40 to $60 per square foot installed with the wood — but it needs sealing every 12 to 18 months if the iron lives on it. Most butcher block counters end up with steam damage within two years unless you commit to upkeep.

**Solid surface** (Corian, Meganite) at $50 to $80 per square foot is the hidden middle pick. It seams invisibly and is easy to repair if burned.

Above the counter, plan a 12-inch-deep upper cabinet at 84 inches above the floor — high enough for a typical hanger but reachable on a step stool. Add a hanging rod underneath for 4 to 6 hangers of "dry clean only" items. Most laundry rooms skip this and end up draping shirts over doorframes.

## Sink choices: utility vs. prep vs. omit

A laundry sink pays back if you ever do any of these: hand-wash delicates, soak muddy rugs, fill watering cans, bathe small dogs, or rinse paint brushes.

![modern laundry sink with tiled surround](https://images.pexels.com/photos/4700611/pexels-photo-4700611.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

**Utility sinks ($80 to $250).** Mustee 18F Utilatub and Sterling 9959 are the workhorses. A 24-inch-deep basin handles a mop bucket. Pair with a wall-mount gooseneck faucet (Delta 2131 at $110) for clearance.

**Prep-style sinks ($300 to $900).** A 24- to 30-inch under-mount or drop-in stainless or composite sink gives you a usable sink without dominating the room. Kraus KHU100-23 at $260 and Elkay ELU2520 at $340 are quality mid-range picks.

**Skip the sink.** If the laundry room is 5x6 feet or smaller and the kitchen sink is 15 feet away, drop the sink and put that 24 inches to better use. Add a wall-mounted utility faucet over the machines ($75 to $150) so you can still fill a mop bucket.

Venting matters more than you'd think. A laundry room needs either an exhaust fan at 80 to 110 CFM (Panasonic FV-0511VKS2 at $90 is excellent) or a window that opens. Without it, moisture creeps into adjacent rooms and feeds mold.

## Drying solutions beyond the dryer

A clothes dryer handles most loads, but it doesn't handle everything. Plan for at least one secondary drying method.

**Wall-mounted drying rack.** A pull-out rack from Pottery Barn ($179 to $279) extends 18 to 24 inches and pulls flat when not in use. The Home-Enova Bamboo Folding Rack ($65) is the budget option that hides in a closet.

**Over-the-door rack.** Whitmor's 39-inch over-the-door rack is $30 and handles woolens and knits that can't go in the dryer. Holds about 6 to 8 pounds.

**Retractable clothesline.** A single retractable line (Leifheit Teleclip at $50 or a generic Umbra version at $25) lets you air-dry inside out of sight when not in use. Pulls 8 to 12 feet of line.

**Heated drying rack.** A wall-mounted electric rack like the Stelmax 8-bar at $260 cuts drying time for woolens in half and uses about 220 watts. Worth it in humid climates.

If you're drying bulky items, plan a 24x24 floor footprint near the dryer for a freestanding folding rack — the $60 to $100 options from Seville Classics and Whitmor all work.

## Lighting: bright, even, and on a dimmer

A laundry room needs more light than you think. Aim for 50 to 70 foot-candles at counter height — that's about 4,000 to 5,500 lumens for a 6x8 room.

**Recessed lighting.** Four 6-inch LED cans at 900 lumens each, evenly spaced 4 feet from each wall and 4 feet apart. Halo and Lithonia both make reliable $25 to $40 LED modules. Pick 4000K for a daylight feel.

**Under-cabinet strips.** A 12-inch strip under the upper cabinet eliminates shadows on the counter. Lit Switch at $22 per 12-inch strip or a Diode LED tape at $18 per foot both work well.

**Pendant over the sink.** Optional but useful for folding detail work. We like the Schoolhouse Electric 6-inch pendant ($189) for traditional rooms and the Big Boy pendant from West Elm ($249) for modern ones.

All laundry-room lights should be on a dimmer — it's the cheapest ergonomic upgrade in the room. Lutron Caseta dimmers at $60 handle LED cleanly. Plan one switch for the main lights and a separate switch for the under-cabinet strips.

## Brand picks for the appliances

We don't recommend buying all one brand; the right answer depends on your priorities.

**LG:** Best all-around efficiency. The WM6700HWA front-loader pairs with the DLE7150W dryer. The new WashCombo All-In-One at $1,799 replaces both in one unit. Quiet, reliable, strong warranty.

**Samsung:** Best smart features. The WF53BB8700AT front loader and DVE53BB8700T electric dryer talk to each other and pick optimal cycles. The AI Smart Dial is genuinely useful after a month of use.

**Maytag:** Best for the basics. The MHW6630HC front loader at $1,099 is what we suggest for households that just want clean clothes with no learning curve. Pair with the MED6630HC dryer at $1,049.

**Speed Queen:** Best for the long haul. The TR7 top-loader and DR7 dryer are commercial-grade and rated for 25+ years. You'll pay $1,500 to $2,200 each. Worth it for very large households.

**Electrolux:** Best for delicates. The ELFW7637AW front loader has an actual hand-wash cycle that's gentler than competitors' equivalents. Pairs with the ELFE7637AT dryer.

**Whirlpool:** Best budget line. The WFW6620HC at $949 is the cheapest front-loader we still recommend.

Skip matching pedestals at $200 to $300 each unless you actually need the height to load comfortably. Most people don't.

## Storage and the small extras

Plan these from the start or you end up with a junk drawer on the counter.

A **detergent and stain remover cabinet** at 12 to 18 inches wide, with a child lock if needed. IKEA's Segrist cabinet at $69 works; for a built-in look, a pull-out like the Rev-A-Shelf 4PSP at $108 holds three laundry baskets side by side.

A **sorting station** with three labeled bins (whites, darks, delicates) keeps the floor clean. Sterilite's 18-gallon bins at $13 each are the budget pick; Curver's foldable bins at $28 each look better.

A **charging and steam station** above the counter holds the iron at $80 to $250 and an ironing board at $40 to $110. The Brabantia Steam Iron at $179 pairs with a Brabantia ironing board at $109.

A **lost sock bin.** The single best improvement we ever made to our own laundry room — a small wicker basket where unmatched socks accumulate. Sounds ridiculous. Saves five minutes a week for years.

## The bottom line

A successful laundry room is built around the workflow: drop, sort, wash, dry, fold, put away — in that order, with each step on its own surface. Choose appliances based on how much you wash, not on feature lists, and spend the rest of the budget on the counter, the sink, and the light. Those three pay you back every single week.
