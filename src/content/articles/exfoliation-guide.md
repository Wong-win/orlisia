Every routine eventually hits the same wall. Cleanser, serum, moisturizer, SPF, repeat — and yet the texture never really changes. There is still a small bump under the eye that never quite flattens. There is still a dullness that no amount of hydration seems to fix. That wall is the wall exfoliation exists to break through, but only if you pick the right tool.

Most of us reach for whatever scrub is sitting in the shower, scrub too hard, overdo it, and then spend the next two weeks nursing a raw, stinging face. That is not the failure of exfoliation. That is the failure of choosing exfoliation without understanding the four ways skin actually sheds.

This is the part of skincare where the difference between a $9 acid and a $90 acid is often the packaging and the marketing rather than the molecule, but only because you already understand what each acid does. Get this right and every serum you apply afterwards works harder.

## How the skin actually sheds, and why it stops

Your skin replaces itself roughly every 28 days. New cells form at the base, push upward over a month, and flake off invisibly when they reach the surface. This process slows dramatically after age 30, and slows further with sun damage, dehydration, and chronic inflammation. Pores also become more sluggish: sebum mixes with dead skin, oxidizes, and forms the micro-clogs that show up as blackheads, milia, and the bumpy texture we all pretend not to notice in the mirror.

Exfoliation speeds up that turnover artificially. It removes dead cells before they pile up so the surface looks smoother and more even-toned. The right method resurfaces without disrupting the lipid barrier, which is the thin, slightly acidic film of oil and water that keeps your skin waterproof and comfortable.

## Physical exfoliation, the right way and the wrong way

Physical exfoliation uses friction. It is the category that covers everything from a $4 St. Ives Apricot Scrub to a $45 Dermalogica Daily Microfoliant powder to a konjac sponge and a washcloth. Each works by manually lifting dead skin from the surface.

The wrong way is the one most of us default to. St. Ives Apricot Scrub uses crushed walnut shells with jagged edges that micro-tear the skin. Even at its small size, it leaves micro-cuts you cannot see but can feel. If your face stings after washing, you are not exfoliating. You are injuring your barrier.

The right way is small particle size, light pressure, and short contact time. The Dermalogica Daily Microfoliant uses rice bran and papain enzyme that dissolves with water. You rinse after 60 seconds and the skin feels smooth, never raw. The $32 Dr. Dennis Gross Alpha Beta Peel Pads are technically chemical exfoliation with a physical swipe, but the acid does the work and the pad texture just removes what's loosened.

Konjac sponges are an underrated middle ground. Used damp with a gentle cleanser, they lift the surface layer without a single grain. They cost around $10, last two to three months, and are safe for daily use on most skin types including sensitive and rosacea-prone skin.

If you want physical exfoliation to mean anything, choose a product with round, smooth, or dissolving particles and apply it for less than a minute. Anything gritty that requires pressure is too coarse.

## Chemical exfoliation, broken down by acid

Chemical exfoliation uses acids to dissolve the bonds between dead cells. Each acid works at a different depth, has a different molecular size, and behaves differently depending on pH. This is where most of the confusion lives, so we go acid by acid.

**Alpha hydroxy acids (AHAs)** are water-soluble and work on the surface of the skin. They brighten, smooth texture, and fade pigmentation from sun damage and old acne marks.

Glycolic acid has the smallest molecular size of any AHA, which means it penetrates the fastest and often the deepest. At 5% to 10% in leave-on products, it is effective for most skin types. The $9 The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution is one of the best beginner options on the market. Anything over 30% is a professional peel only and lives in the $90 to $250 range at a dermatologist's office.

Lactic acid is a larger molecule, so it works more slowly and is generally better tolerated. The $42 Paula's Choice 5% AHA is lactic-based and a far gentler daily option. Lactic acid also pulls double duty as a humectant, drawing water into the skin as it exfoliates.

Mandelic acid has the largest molecular size of any AHA, which makes it the slowest and the most tolerable on darker and reactive skin tones. It also has antibacterial properties that make it particularly useful for acne-prone skin. The Allies of Skin 1% Mandelic Acid Cream is around $80, but The Ordinary's Mandelic Acid 10% at $9 gives a similar result.

**Beta hydroxy acids (BHAs)** are oil-soluble, which means they get inside the pore and dissolve the plug of sebum and dead skin that forms a blackhead or a closed comedone. Salicylic acid is the only BHA used in mainstream skincare. At 1% to 2% in leave-on products, it is a daily-use ingredient for oily, combination, and acne-prone skin.

The $34 Paula's Choice 2% BHA Liquid Exfoliant is the most recommended BHA product in dermatology. It has a pH between 3.2 and 3.8, which is what you want for actual efficacy. The Sephora Collection Waterproof Eye Makeup Remover and most "BHA toners" under $15 are often too gentle to actually do meaningful work. Check the percentage.

**Polyhydroxy acids (PHAs)** are the newest category and the gentlest. Gluconolactone and lactobionic acid have larger molecules than AHAs and BHAs, so they sit on the surface and do not penetrate. They are excellent for sensitive skin, rosacea, and anyone whose barrier is currently recovering from over-exfoliation. The $35 CeraVe SA Lotion for Rough and Bumpy Skin uses salicylic acid but pairs it with ceramides that keep the barrier intact. The $50 Drunk Elephant T.L.C. Framboos Glycolic Resurfacing Night Serum combines AHA, BHA, and PHA at relatively low percentages for combination skin that needs everything in moderation.

## What percentage and what pH actually matter

Two numbers on the label determine whether an acid product works. Percentage tells you how much of the acid is in the formula. pH tells you how active the acid is. Most effective leave-on exfoliants sit between pH 3 and pH 4.

For beginners, the rule we follow is straightforward. Start at the low end of each acid range: 5% AHA, 1% BHA, or 5% PHA. Use it three times a week for two weeks. If your skin tolerates it well, increase to every other day. If it still feels fine after a month, daily use is reasonable.

If the label does not list a percentage, you are taking a leap. Brands like Drunk Elephant, Paula's Choice, The Ordinary, and CeraVe always disclose percentages. Brands that hide behind phrases like "AHA complex" usually mean a low dose designed to never irritate anyone.

## When to exfoliate, when to back off

You exfoliate in the evening. This is non-negotiable. AHAs and BHAs both increase photosensitivity in the hours after use. Daytime exfoliation raises your UV sensitivity to a meaningful degree even on cloudy days.

You do not exfoliate on the same night you use retinol. The two combined are too much. The standard beginner schedule looks like this:

- **Sunday:** AHA (5-8% lactic or mandelic)
- **Tuesday:** BHA (1-2% salicylic) only on areas that need it
- **Thursday:** PHA or gentle physical exfoliation (konjac sponge, fine rice powder)
- **Saturday:** Recovery night with a ceramide-rich moisturizer

Nights without exfoliation are nights for hydration, retinol, peptides, and barrier-supporting ingredients like ceramides, squalane, and panthenol. The Ordinary's "Buffet" serum and La Roche-Posay's $42 Cicaplast Baume B5 are both excellent barrier-repair products for the nights off.

The moment you see redness that does not fade within an hour, stinging on application, or a tightness that does not go away with moisturizer, you stop every active for at least five days. The skin is telling you the barrier is compromised. More acid will only deepen the wound.

## Exfoliation myths that keep costing us money

**"The more it tingles, the better it works."** Tingling is irritation. Some tingling on first use can be normal with high-strength AHAs and stops within the first two weeks of use as the skin acclimates. Persistent stinging is a sign to dilute, reduce frequency, or switch acid types.

**"Scrubs tighten pores."** Nothing tightens pores. Pores do not have muscles to tighten them. What scrubs do is clear the debris that makes pores look stretched. They appear smaller because they are empty.

**"Sensitive skin cannot exfoliate."** Sensitive skin needs to exfoliate more carefully, not less. Polyhydroxy acids, mandelic acid, and ultra-gentler enzyme masks like the $48 Dermalogica Gentle Cream Exfoliant are appropriate for reactive, rosacea-prone, and post-procedure skin.

**"Acne skin needs to be scrubbed clean."** Active acne is inflammation. Mechanical scrub pressure pushes bacteria deeper and worsens the lesion. Use salicylic acid leave-on products instead and let the chemistry do the work.

The $90 Dr. Dennis Gross Alpha Beta Peel Pads are a genuine splurge worth defending if you can afford them. Pre-soaked in a stable AHA/BHA blend with a low pH around 3.5, they are portion-controlled and travel-friendly. The $32 Drunk Elephant T.L.C. The $9 The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution, Mandelic Acid 10%, and Lactic Acid 10% are absurdly well-formulated for the price. So is the $7 The Ordinary Salicylic Acid 2% Masque for weekly pore decongestion.

CeraVe is the most reliable drugstore pick. The $16 CeraVe SA Cleanser, $13 CeraVe Hydrating Cleanser, and $18 CeraVe Moisturizing Cream cover the cleansing and barrier-repair half of the routine for under $50 combined.

If you have a little more to spend, the $42 La Roche-Posay Anthelios UVMune 400 SPF 50+ is the daily sunscreen that everyone in dermatology recommends, because exfoliating without daily SPF is creating new damage to replace the damage you just removed.

## The bottom line

Choose one acid family that targets your actual concern, start at the low end of the dose range, use it three nights a week, and never on the same night as retinol. Pair every exfoliation with daily SPF, and back off the moment the barrier says stop.
