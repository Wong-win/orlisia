Mechanical keyboards have moved from a niche enthusiast category to a mainstream purchase, and the buying experience has only gotten more confusing. Walk into a keyboard subreddit and you'll find a vocabulary that looks designed to exclude newcomers: linear, tactile, clicky, hot-swap, gasket-mount, lubed, filmed, PBT, POM, ABS. Strip those terms away and the actual decision is simpler than the discourse suggests.

The keyboard you pick comes down to four things: switch type, physical layout, keycap quality, and whether you want to tinker. The brand you buy it from matters less than the configuration you choose once you understand those four.

## Switch Types: Linear, Tactile, and Clicky

Every mechanical keyboard key sits above a switch — a small mechanism that registers a press. The switch determines how the keyboard feels and sounds. There are three families, and your preference between them tends to lock in within a week of use.

Linear switches move straight down with no bump or click. They feel smooth, register a press with relatively light force (typically 45g actuation weight), and are the choice of most gamers because of the short, predictable travel. Cherry MX Red, Gateron Yellow, and Kailh Box Red are all common linears. If you type fast and don't want any tactile distraction, linear is the default.

Tactile switches add a small bump partway through the press — a physical signal that the key has registered. They cost nothing in speed but make typing more confident, especially if you touch-type and want feedback that a keystroke has landed without bottoming out. Cherry MX Brown, Gateron Brown, Kailh Box White, and the popular Holy Panda are tactiles. Most office and programming users end up here.

Clicky switches add an audible click on top of the tactile bump. They are loud — typically 60-70 dB at a normal typing distance, comparable to a loud conversation. Cherry MX Blue, Kailh Box White, and most buckling-spring switches (IBM Model M-style) are clicky. Clicky keyboards were once the standard office keyboard, and they remain excellent for people who never share an office with anyone else.

The fourth option, increasingly popular, is a silent switch — typically a tactile or linear switch with dampeners pre-installed. Silent linears from the Cherry MX Silent Red and Gateron Silent Yellow lines, and silent tactiles like the Boba U4T, are now widely available. These give you most of the switch personality without the noise, which matters if your keyboard is in a shared room or on a recorded call.

## Form Factor: 60%, 65%, TKL, and Full Size

Layout refers to how many keys are on the board, which dictates desk footprint and which function keys (and how many) survive. The four most common layouts each have a real use case.

A 60% keyboard drops the function row, arrow keys, and the navigation cluster. It's roughly 60% of a full-size keyboard's width — about 11.5 inches for a typical 60% versus 17.5 for a full-size. You access missing keys through a Fn-layer, holding a modifier and pressing another key. The compromise is real: hitting arrow keys for spreadsheet navigation gets annoying, and the lack of a dedicated Delete key trips people up. But the saved desk space is significant, and the 60% is the favorite layout of programmers and writers who value a clean workspace.

A 65% keeps the arrow keys and a small column of navigation keys but still drops the function row. This is the most popular enthusiast layout for a reason: it's a 60% for the bulk of the time, with arrows always reachable. Most 65% boards sell for $100-$200.

A TKL (tenkeyless) keeps the function row, navigation cluster, and arrows, but drops the number pad. The footprint is roughly 14 inches wide. If you do any serious navigation in spreadsheets, code, or DAWs, TKL is the smallest layout that doesn't force compromises. It's also the layout of most prebuilt office keyboards from Logitech and Razer.

Full-size keeps everything — the number pad, the four-block navigation cluster, the function row. Most office keyboards are full-size. You should only buy full-size if you actually use the numpad regularly; for accounting, CAD, or anything that uses a numeric keypad, full-size is the right answer.

## Hot-Swap vs Soldered PCBs

This is the single most consequential decision for buyers who might want to change their switches later.

A hot-swap PCB has sockets for each switch. To change a switch, you pull it out with a keycap puller (or the included switch puller) and push in a new one. No soldering. This is the modern mainstream standard: Keychron, GMMK, Drop, Akko, and most Glorious boards ship with hot-swap PCBs in 2026.

A soldered PCB requires the switches to be soldered onto the board. Changing switches means desoldering each switch with a soldering iron. Soldered boards used to be the enthusiast default for electrical reasons, but the latest hot-swap sockets from Kailh and Gateron are reliable enough that the only real advantage of soldered is fitting tighter switch layouts (ortholinear boards, split keyboards, and ergo boards often still solder).

If you're buying your first mechanical keyboard, get a hot-swap board. The cost difference is negligible, and the option to swap switches without any tools is the single feature most first-time buyers wish they'd had from day one.

## Keycap Materials: ABS, PBT, and Profile

Keycaps are consumable, swappable, and the single most visible part of the keyboard. The two materials matter more than the print method, and the profile determines how the keys feel under your fingers.

ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene) is the default plastic. It's cheaper, takes dye well, and produces bright legends. The trade-off is that ABS develops shine within months of regular use, especially on the home row. The shine is permanent — the plastic physically smooths under finger friction.

PBT (polybutylene terephthalate) is more expensive and slightly harder to mold, but it doesn't shine. PBT legends are typically either doubleshot or dye-sublimated. Most quality aftermarket keycap sets — GMK, KAT, KBDfans, and Drop's signature sets — are PBT. If you intend to keep a keyboard for more than a year, PBT is worth the upcharge, which is usually $40-$90 over a base ABS set.

Profile refers to the height and shape of the caps. The three most common are OEM (medium height, slightly sculpted — the default on most boards), Cherry (slightly lower than OEM, sculpted — the most popular enthusiast profile), and SA (tall, spherical — what most people picture when they think of a typewriter). SA keys are noticeably louder and slightly slower to actuate. For most buyers, OEM or Cherry is the right default.

## Connectivity: Wired, Bluetooth, and 2.4 GHz

Three connection options exist. Wired is the lowest latency option (under 1 ms), the most reliable, and the only choice that works with mechanical keyboards for serious gaming. Bluetooth adds wireless freedom but adds 8-15 ms of latency and limits you to one device at a time on most boards. 2.4 GHz wireless (using a USB dongle) sits in the middle: under 5 ms latency, dedicated wireless link, and typically one device at a time.

If you want one board for a desktop setup, wired is fine. If you want to share the board between a laptop and desktop, look for Bluetooth plus 2.4 GHz plus wired — Keychron, Logitech, and the better Asus boards all support all three. A board that supports only Bluetooth is wrong for gaming; a board with only 2.4 GHz is wrong for travel.

## Brand Matrix: Who Makes What Worth Buying

The mechanical keyboard market is more crowded than almost any consumer electronics category. The five brands worth taking seriously cover nearly every budget.

Keychron is the strongest default for buyers in the $80-$200 range. The Q1, Q2, and Q3 are gasket-mount, hot-swap, with aluminum cases and QMK/VIA firmware for key remapping. The V-series (V1, V2, V3, V8) at the $80-$120 level is plastic-cased but still hot-swap and well-built. Keychron's biggest gap is sound — the plastic V-series is noticeably more hollow than the metal Q-series.

Logitech dominates the office keyboard market for good reason. The MX Mechanical at $150-$180 is a low-profile mechanical with Kailh Choc switches, Bluetooth plus the Logi Bolt dongle, and the Logi Options+ software stack. It's the right keyboard for typing in a Mac-heavy office where you want a unified Logitech peripheral ecosystem. It is hot-swappable only in newer revisions.

Razer owns the gaming keyboard segment. The Huntsman Mini at $130 and the Huntsman V3 Pro at $180-$220 use Razer's optical-mechanical switches with light-based actuation (faster than metal-contact switches in theory, with similar real-world feel). The BlackWidow V4 at $170 covers full-size. If you want RGB above all else, Razer's Chroma is the brightest, most programmable lighting ecosystem.

GMMK (Glorious) is the modular specialists. The GMMK Pro at $170-$200 is an aluminum 65% with hot-swap sockets, gasket-mount, and a polished case that runs heavy. The original GMMK at $90 is a hot-swap 60% with a plastic case and lots of room to mod. Glorious is the right pick for tinkerers who plan to swap switches and keycaps often.

Drop makes curated boards under the CTRL, ALT, and SHIFT lines — all hot-swap, all with machined aluminum cases, all in the $140-$220 range. Drop also operates the largest aftermarket keycap store in the US, so buying a Drop board plus a Drop keycap set in one order is convenient. The product lines have moved slower than Keychron's in the last two years, but the build quality is consistent.

## Practical Recommendations by Budget

For $50-$90, the entry tier: Akko 3068B (65%, hot-swap, Bluetooth), Royal Kludge RK68 (65%, hot-swap, RGB), and Keychron V1 (60%, hot-swap, plastic case) are all reasonable first boards. Pick whichever layout you like — they all use similar Gateron or Akko switches and will perform similarly.

For $90-$180, the mid tier: Keychron Q1 or Q2 (65%/75%, aluminum gasket-mount, hot-swap), GMMK Pro (75% with rotary encoder), and Drop CTRL (TKL, hot-swap, USB-C) are the strongest picks. At this price, you'll feel the difference in build quality immediately — the aluminum case dampens sound, the gasket mount softens the bottom-out, and the software lets you remap anything.

For $180-$350, the enthusiast tier: Keychron Q Pro variants with brass plates, Mode Designs Sonnet and Envoy, Tofu65 with premium cases, and Studio Smith — at this level the differences are in switch customization, gasket-mount tuning, and case finish. The improvements are real but subtle.

## The bottom line

Pick your switch type first — linear for gaming, tactile for typing, clicky only if you live alone — then pick the smallest layout you can comfortably work with (65% and TKL are the most popular for good reason), buy hot-swap so you can change your mind on switches later, and spend the difference on PBT keycaps rather than RGB. A $120 Keychron V2 with Brown switches and a $90 PBT keycap set will outlast three laptops.
