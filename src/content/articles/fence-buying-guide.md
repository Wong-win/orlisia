Choosing a fence sounds simple until you stand in the lumber aisle at Home Depot with five different materials, a salesperson who recommends whatever is in stock, and no idea which one will still look acceptable in ten years. We have pulled together the real numbers — installed cost per linear foot, life span, maintenance calendars, permit thresholds, and the brands contractors actually order — so the decision comes down to climate, budget, and how much weekend time you want to give up.

## The four questions that drive every fence decision

Before we get into materials, the decision tree is short.

1. **Why are you fencing?** Privacy, pet containment, pool code, curb appeal, or marking a property line? Each leads to a different height and style.
2. **What is the fence's purpose in five years?** A picket fence that looks charming with a dog may be insufficient when the dog becomes a hundred-pound adolescent.
3. **What is your climate?** Coastal salt, desert sun, freeze-thaw cycles, and high-wind zones each penalize a different material.
4. **How much maintenance will you actually do?** Be honest. The cedar fence you never seal rots in eight years. The vinyl fence that never needs sealing looks the same in year fifteen.

A 150-linear-foot fence around a typical backyard costs between $2,400 and $15,000 installed in most U.S. markets. Where in that range you land depends almost entirely on material, with labor accounting for roughly 40 to 60 percent of the total.

## Wood fences: cedar, pine, redwood, and the truth about pressure-treated

Wood is what most homeowners picture. It is also the most variable category in performance and lifespan.

**Western red cedar** is the long-running favorite for fence boards. It is naturally rot-resistant (the heartwood contains thujaplicins that resist decay), light, and stable. A 6-foot-tall cedar privacy fence in a stockade or board-on-board style runs $25 to $40 per linear foot installed. The boards alone cost $2.50 to $4.50 per linear foot at retail; the rest is labor, posts, and concrete. Cedar weathers to a silver-gray if left unfinished, which many homeowners now prefer, but the dark-haired clean look requires a stain or sealer ($35 to $55 per gallon of oil-based, applied every 2 to 3 years from brands like Penofin, Behr, or Olympic).

**Pressure-treated Southern yellow pine** is the budget option. PT pine fence boards run $1.50 to $3 per linear foot at Home Depot or Lowe's; an installed PT pine privacy fence runs $18 to $30 per linear foot. The catch is the maintenance: PT pine absorbs moisture, twists, and checks within five years if not sealed. Plan on a sealing every 2 years for the first decade, or accept the weathered look.

**Redwood** (old-growth heartwood from California) is the premium wood option, with natural tannins that resist rot for 25 to 35 years. It runs $6 to $10 per linear foot for boards alone, which translates to $40 to $60 per linear foot installed. Construction-grade redwood today is mostly second-growth, with lower tannin content and shorter lifespan. If you want the real longevity of redwood, specify "heartwood, clear all heart" or "construction heart" grades, not the lighter grades.

The honest maintenance calendar for wood fences: clean annually, reseal every 2 to 3 years, replace individual boards as they split ($8 to $20 per board installed). A cedar privacy fence that is well-maintained gives 20 to 25 years. A PT pine fence that is neglected gives 8 to 12.

## Vinyl (PVC) fences: the zero-maintenance trade-off

Vinyl fencing has eaten a serious share of the wood market over the last 20 years. It is what we recommend to homeowners who will not seal anything.

**Solid PVC panels** (sometimes called "virgin vinyl") from manufacturers like ActiveYards, CertainTeed Bufftech, and Homeland Vinyl run $30 to $55 per linear foot installed for a 6-foot privacy fence. The boards will not rot, will not fade significantly (most have a UV inhibitor warrantied for 20+ years), and never need to be sealed. Pressure-washing once a year is the only maintenance.

**Recycled plastic-core panels** (a wood-flour core wrapped in PVC) cost $25 to $40 per linear foot installed and look identical but expand and contract more, leading to occasional post-popping in freeze-thaw climates.

There are three honest downsides to vinyl. First, it gets brittle in cold weather — a direct hit from a lawnmower-thrown rock at 10°F can shatter a panel, and warranty claims exclude impact damage. Second, wind rating matters: a solid privacy panel is a sail, and most residential vinyl fences are rated to 80 to 110 mph; in hurricanes zones (Florida, the Carolinas, the Gulf Coast) you want an aluminum-reinforced bottom rail and concrete-set posts. Third, vinyl fades to a slightly chalky look after 10 to 15 years. It never rots, but it does not look factory-new forever.

## Aluminum and wrought iron: low-maintenance, high-cost-per-foot

**Aluminum fencing** is what most people picture when they think "wrought iron fence." Modern aluminum from makers like Master Halco, Ameristar, and Spec Rail is powder-coated, will not rust, and is rated for 30+ years with virtually no maintenance. Because aluminum fences are almost always open-picket (4-foot-tall pool code, 3-rail landscape style), they are not privacy fences. Pricing runs $30 to $50 per linear foot installed for a 4-foot residential aluminum fence.

**Wrought iron** (actual forged, not cast aluminum) is rare, expensive, and used mostly in historic districts or upscale urban homes where the look must be authentic. Installed costs run $60 to $120 per linear foot. Wrought iron needs rust treatment and repainting every 12 to 20 years. It is the highest-maintenance and highest-cost fence per linear foot, chosen because nothing else looks like it.

**Steel fences** are the cheaper cousin of wrought iron: galvanized then powder-coated, with a similar look at $40 to $70 per linear foot installed. ActiveYards, Jerith, and Delgard are the main brands.

## Chain link: the practical, ugly, affordable workhorse

Chain link is what you install when function is the only goal: dog runs, pool enclosures (with privacy slats), commercial perimeters, and rental properties. Galvanized chain link runs $10 to $20 per linear foot installed for a 4-foot fence, $15 to $25 per linear foot for 6-foot. Vinyl-coated (black or green) chain link runs $15 to $30 per linear foot.

It is not pretty, but it works for 25+ years with no maintenance. Privacy slats ($1.50 to $3 per linear foot of slats) reduce visibility to about 90 percent. For a rental property or a backyard that will not be landscaped, chain link is rarely the wrong answer.

## Composite fencing: the modern hybrid worth knowing about

Composite fences (Trex, SimTek, and MoistureShield Fencing) are molded panels made from recycled wood fiber and HDPE plastic, factory-colored to look like wood or stone. They are heavy (a 6-foot composite panel can weigh 60 pounds), so installation is more involved, but the result is essentially a 25-to-30-year zero-maintenance fence.

Costs run $45 to $70 per linear foot installed for a 6-foot composite privacy fence, putting them at the high end of the market. For a homeowner who wants the look of wood with zero maintenance and has the budget, Trex Seclusions or SimTek Ecostone panels are the right answer. For everyone else, vinyl at half the installed cost gets 80 percent of the benefit.

## Privacy, picket, and split-rail: matching style to purpose

Material is one axis. Style is the other.

**Privacy fences** are 6-foot-tall solid panels with no gaps. They require more wind resistance in your material choice — vinyl solid privacy needs aluminum reinforcement; wood works but heavier. They also often trigger permit and HOA review.

**Picket fences** are 3 to 4 feet tall, evenly spaced vertical boards, traditionally white-painted cedar or pine. They are decorative markers, not privacy. Costs run $20 to $35 per linear foot installed.

**Split-rail fences** (zigzag of two or three horizontal rails, often used for rural properties, horse fencing) are 3-rail wood or vinyl at $15 to $28 per linear foot installed. Not for close urban lots, but the right call for a few acres.

**Post-and-rail** is the upgraded split-rail look, with vertical posts and horizontal rails — a 3-rail runs $18 to $30 per linear foot installed and is the right fence for a country-style property with no privacy requirement.

## Permits, codes, and the utilities question

Almost every U.S. municipality requires a permit for fences over 4 or 6 feet (varies by jurisdiction), for pool fences (typically 4-foot minimum with self-closing gates, ASTM F2286 compliant), and for any fence within an easement. The permit is $50 to $400 depending on the city.

Call 811 before digging. The 811 national "call before you dig" line (or the online equivalent) is free and legally required in every state. Hitting a buried gas, water, or fiber line while digging post holes is both dangerous and a repair you will pay for out of pocket. Most utility location happens within 2 to 3 business days, and it is good for 14 days.

HOA approval, where it applies, is a separate and sometimes longer process. The HOA may specify material, color, and height with rules tighter than the municipal code.

## Cost summary per linear foot (installed, 6-foot privacy, mid-2026 U.S. pricing)

- **PT pine privacy**: $18 to $30 per linear ft
- **Cedar privacy**: $25 to $40 per linear ft
- **Redwood privacy (heartwood)**: $40 to $60 per linear ft
- **Vinyl privacy**: $30 to $55 per linear ft
- **Aluminum (4-foot, open picket)**: $30 to $50 per linear ft
- **Composite (Trex, SimTek)**: $45 to $70 per linear ft
- **Chain link 6-foot**: $15 to $25 per linear ft
- **Wrought iron**: $60 to $120 per linear ft

For a 150-linear-foot fence — typical for a single-family backyard — the installed total lands between $2,700 (chain link) and $10,500 (composite or wrought iron).

## Brand picks by category

- **Wood**: cedar from Real Cedar–certified suppliers; PT pine from local lumber yards with "ground contact" rated boards.
- **Vinyl**: ActiveYards, Bufftech (CertainTeed), Homeland Vinyl.
- **Aluminum/steel**: Master Halco, Ameristar, Jerith, Spec Rail.
- **Composite**: Trex Seclusions, SimTek (now part of MoistureShield), Fiberon.
- **Chain link**: Master Halco, Eastern Wholesale Fence.
- **Hardware and gate operators**: Locinox, Nationwide Industries, LiftMaster (for automatic gates).

## The bottom line

For a typical suburban backyard where privacy is the goal and zero maintenance matters, vinyl or composite is the right answer and will pay for itself over 25 years versus cedar if you skip more than one round of sealing. For a property where the fence is decorative and the lot is open, aluminum from Master Halco or Jerith looks correct for 30 years. For a tight budget, pressure-treated pine is fine — seal it on schedule or accept the silver patina.
