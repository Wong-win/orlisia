Five patio surfaces dominate the residential market, and the right choice has less to do with style than with climate, soil, and how long you plan to live with it. Walk into any landscape architect's office and the same five materials come up — poured concrete, concrete pavers, natural stone, brick, and the "softer" options (gravel, decomposed granite, and tile). They're rarely chosen for looks alone. The decision is almost always driven by climate (freeze-thaw exposure), soil (clay vs. sand), maintenance expectations, and budget. What follows is the practical comparison — what each surface is, what it costs installed, what it asks of you, and where it tends to fail. We've drawn on site visits, conversations with masons and hardscape contractors in five regions, and current 2026 material pricing to put the numbers in context.

## Concrete Pavers, the Workhorse

The single most-installed patio surface in North America. Concrete pavers are 2.5-to-3-inch thick individual units, manufactured in pressed or wet-cast form, laid on a compacted base of crushed stone and jointing sand. The leading brands — Belgard, Unilock, Techo-Bloc, and Pavestone — offer hundreds of styles, from $4-to-$8 utility pavers to $14-to-$22 premium "wet-cast" styles that mimic natural stone. Installed cost for a typical residential patio runs $18 to $32 per square foot in most regions, with $22 being the mode.

Why paver patios keep winning: they're repairable. A cracked paver replaces in 10 minutes. Settled pavers lift and re-level. Snow shovels, salt, and freeze-thaw cycles that destroy poured concrete barely touch a properly installed paver field. The base matters more than the paver: a 4-to-6-inch compacted Class II road base topped with 1 inch of bedding sand is the spec most masons use. Skimping on base depth is the single most common cause of paver failures.

Where they fall short: pavers are harder to clean than poured concrete (the joints collect organic matter), and the jointing sand needs to be replenished every 5 to 10 years. Polymeric sand (Techniseal or Sakrete, $25 to $40 per 50-pound bag) hardens in the joints and slows the wash-out but doesn't eliminate it. Expect to re-sand a paver patio every decade. They also don't read as natural stone no matter how good the manufacturer gets. The wet-cast Belgard "Ardesie" and Unilock "Natural Stone" lines come closest, and even then a trained eye tells the difference.

## Natural Stone, the Premium Choice

When "patio" really means "outdoor room," natural stone is the surface most homeowners describe as their target. The most common options are bluestone (the sandy blue-gray flagstone of the Northeast), limestone (more uniform, lighter in color, the modern darling of the high-end landscape press), travertine (cream-colored, mostly Southwest and Florida), and granite or basalt (denser, harder to cut, more expensive). Sandstone, slate, and quartzite show up regionally.

Installed cost runs $30 to $60 per square foot in most markets, with limestone and bluestone at the lower end and imported travertine, granite, or specialty stones at the higher end. The material itself is often only $8 to $18 per square foot; the labor and base prep drives the rest. A 350-square-foot patio in limestone realistically runs $11,000 to $18,000 installed, which is a lot — but stone patios installed with proper base routinely last 50-plus years with minimal maintenance.

The honest failure mode for natural stone is flagging (rocks rocking underfoot) and staining from leaves, water, and rust. A dry-laid patio re-levels easily; a mortared one does not. Buy stone rated for exterior freeze-thaw use (your mason will know which quarries), and avoid marble and limestone variants that fracture under hard freeze conditions. Polymeric sand or a stone sealer ($50 to $90 per gallon, applied every 3 to 5 years) slows staining significantly.

For the climate question: bluestone and limestone both stand up to Northeast freeze-thaw; travertine and granite are the right calls for hot, dry climates; concrete pavers or tile beat stone in extreme freeze-thaw regions like Minnesota or Quebec because they handle heave better.

## Brick and Clay, the Quiet Classic

Brick patios aren't as popular as they were 30 years ago, but they're still one of the longest-lasting patio surfaces when properly installed, and they remain the right choice for Cape Cod, Mid-Atlantic, and Midwestern colonials. Real clay brick (not concrete brick) is fired at high temperatures, which gives it a 50+ year color-fastness that concrete pavers can't match. The leading manufacturers — Glen-Gery, Whitacre-Greer, and Pine Hall — produce brick in the $3 to $7 per piece range, with old-style reclaimed brick at a premium.

Installed cost runs $20 to $35 per square foot in most markets. A 300-square-foot brick patio realistically runs $7,000 to $10,000 installed. The base spec is the same as for pavers — 4 to 6 inches of compacted road base, 1 inch of bedding sand, jointing sand or mortar on top. A dry-laid brick patio re-levels like a paver patio; a mortared one is a permanent installation that needs to be built right the first time.

Failure modes for brick are different from stone or concrete. Brick can spall (flake) at the surface, especially with de-icing salts. Avoid rock salt on brick — use calcium magnesium acetate (CMA, $20 per 25 pounds) or sand for traction. Like pavers, brick weedeater damage to the joints is common; replenish jointing sand annually in active seasons. A well-built brick patio in a mild climate routinely goes 25 years before any maintenance; in a hard-freeze climate, expect to reset a few bricks every decade.

A common upgrade is the "mortar-set" brick patio laid on a 4-inch concrete slab. This costs $35 to $50 per square foot and lasts 50+ years, but it's a permanent installation rather than a paver field — most homeowners can't lift and re-set a $15,000 brick patio the way they can a paver patio. The choice between the two is really a choice about whether you want a 25-year patio you can renovate or a 50-year patio you can't.

## Gravel, Tile, and the Permeable Options

The "soft" patio surfaces — gravel, decomposed granite (DG), and pea stone — are the right answer for many climate and drainage realities, and they're the most common choice for native-plant and xeriscape gardens. A 4-to-6-inch compacted depth of 3/4-inch crushed stone, topped with a finer DG for walkability, runs $6 to $12 per square foot installed — dramatically cheaper than any hardscape. Gravel and DG handle freeze-thaw without moving (much); they drain instead of shedding water onto neighbors; and they invite renovation.

Pea stone (smooth, rounded, smaller) is more comfortable underfoot than crushed stone but moves more. A 2-to-3-inch decomposed granite topping over crushed stone base is the practical sweet spot for patios. Stabilized DG (mixed with a binding agent like Quartzloc or Orgill's DG-Plus) holds shape better and is the choice for paths and chairs-with-legs. Maintenance is mostly edging with a metal or stone border, plus occasional raking and replenishment every 5 to 10 years ($300 to $500 in stone depending on size).

Porcelain and ceramic tile are the latest category to mature for outdoor use. 2-cm-thick porcelain pavers (from brands like Marazzi, Tile Tech, or country-of-origin Italian or Spanish makers) handle freeze-thaw better than the older 1-cm tiles that came to market a decade ago. Installed cost: $25 to $45 per square foot. The benefit: a consistent look without the color variation of stone, easier to clean than stone, often rated for high-traffic commercial use. The drawback: tile needs a perfect base, and a single cracked tile is harder to replace than a paver. The 2-cm exterior-rated porcelain tile is the right answer for modern minimalist landscapes and pool decks, and it can be installed on a pedestal system over a waterproof membrane for rooftop patios.

For permeable surfaces specifically — important in regions with stormwater rules or impervious-coverage limits — consider permeable pavers from Uni Eco-Stone or Belgard Aqua-Brick. They cost 20 to 30 percent more than standard pavers but satisfy most municipal permeable-surface requirements and let the yard drain in place rather than running water off onto neighbors.

## Cost, Installation, and the Surface That Holds Up

The cheapest patio is not the cheapest surface — it's the one with the right base. A paver, brick, or stone surface with a 4-to-6-inch properly compacted road base installs at $20 to $60 per square foot, with $25 being a realistic residential average. The life expectancy by material:

- Concrete pavers (good base, periodic re-sanding): 25–40 years
- Natural stone (limestone, bluestone, granite, properly installed): 50+ years
- Brick (clay, properly installed): 50+ years
- Gravel and DG: 10+ years before significant replenishment, indefinite with upkeep
- Porcelain tile (exterior 2-cm, properly installed): 30+ years
- Poured concrete: 15–25 years before cracks develop

For most homeowners, the practical answer is a concrete paver patio with a proper base, in a style and color that complements the house. It costs less than stone, lasts 30+ years, and can be re-leveled or expanded by a mason in 2026 if the next owner has different ideas. Stone and brick are the right calls when the home style and budget justify the upgrade. Gravel and DG are the right calls for drainage, slope, or a more natural garden feel. Tile is the right call for modern architecture and pool decks. Pour concrete is rarely the right call — it's expensive per square foot once the form work, rebar, and finishing are accounted for, and it cracks within 15 to 25 years in most climates.

The install makes or breaks the patio. Hire on the base prep and the manufacturer certification, not on the brand of paver. ICPI (Interlocking Concrete Pavement Institute) certified installers, NCMA certified stone masons, and Tile Council of North America certified tile installers are the credentials to ask for. A poorly installed $8 paver outperforms a perfectly installed $20 paver only when the spec of each is correct. Get three bids, ask for references within the last 18 months, and walk those references before signing.

## The bottom line

Most patios should be concrete pavers on a 4-to-6-inch road base — $22 per square foot installed and the right answer in almost any climate. Natural stone and clay brick are the right calls for traditional architecture and long horizons, both lasting 50-plus years with minimal care. Gravel, decomposed granite, and porcelain tile round out the practical options for drainage, slope, or modern design. The base matters more than the surface: budget $5 to $8 per square foot for the base work alone, hire an installer certified by the trade association for that material, and any of these surfaces will outlast the furniture set on top.
