Most outdoor heaters are sold by the BTU number, and most buyers overshoot. A 41,000-BTU propane patio heater in a 6-foot-diameter seating area drives everyone out with the radiant heat on one side of the body and the chill on the other. The right heater is sized to the space it actually heats, mounted at the height it actually runs at, and fueled by what is actually cheapest at your house. We break down the five types, the real BTU and watt output, the safety clearance numbers from the manufacturers themselves, and the running cost per hour so you can pick the right one without buying a heater and a return trip together.

## The five types and what they actually heat

The category looks crowded. There are really five technologies:

1. **Propane patio heaters** (tall standing mushroom-style, table-top, and wall-mounted)
2. **Natural gas patio heaters** (tall standing or wall-mounted, plumbed to a gas line)
3. **Electric infrared heaters** (wall- or ceiling-mounted, hardwired)
4. **Fire pits** (wood-burning, propane, or natural gas)
5. **Chimineas** (wood-burning clay or cast-iron)

Each has a different heating physics, which is why a "41,000 BTU" label does not mean the same thing twice.

## Propane patio heaters: the workhorse that almost everyone owns

The most familiar outdoor heater is the standing propane "mushroom" — a 7-to-8-foot-tall aluminum pole with a reflector at the top and a 20-pound propane tank hidden in the base. They produce 35,000 to 48,000 BTU per hour and heat a circular area about 8 to 12 feet across in still air.

**Heat coverage**: effective in still air to a 6-to-10-foot radius; in any wind above 8 mph the heat plume is pushed sideways and the effective area drops by half. They are radiant heaters — the heat travels in a straight line from the dome — so they work best when the seating is arranged around the heater, not in a long line next to it.

**Fuel cost**: a standard 20-pound propane tank holds about 4.7 gallons and runs a 40,000-BTU heater for 8 to 10 hours. Propane prices in 2026 run $3.20 to $4.80 per gallon, so the operating cost is roughly $1.50 to $2.40 per hour at full output. Lower the output (most have a variable knob) and you stretch to 14 to 16 hours per tank.

**Brand picks**: AZ Patio Heaters (the value leader, $159 to $279 for a 41,000-BTU standing model), Lava Heat (mid-range, $279 to $499), Fire Sense (mid-to-premium), Thermo Tiki, and Hiland. Commercial-grade mushroom heaters from Sunbelt and Muskoka run $600 to $1,200.

**Clearance**: 24 inches from the dome to any combustible on the sides, 36 inches overhead. Read the label; manufacturers vary.

**The hidden cost**: propane tanks run empty at the worst moments. A spare tank ($25 to $35 with a refill or exchange) solves that. If you are going to use one weekly, consider a permanent gas line conversion kit ($80 to $140) and have a gas fitter run a propane manifold.

## Natural gas patio heaters: clean, quiet, and never run out

Natural gas heaters are the same physical heater as a propane model, but plumbed to your home's gas line. They produce 30,000 to 50,000 BTU per hour with no tank to refill.

**Fuel cost**: natural gas prices in 2026 average $1.40 to $1.80 per therm (with significant regional variation — California runs $2.20 to $3.00, Texas $0.90 to $1.30). A 40,000-BTU natural gas heater running at full output uses about 0.4 therm per hour. Operating cost: $0.55 to $1.20 per hour at 2026 prices.

That is roughly half the operating cost of propane, and the convenience factor is decisive for an outdoor kitchen, a covered patio you use 100 nights a year, or a restaurant patio.

**Brand picks**: Bromic (Australian, the dominant premium brand), Infratech (the U.S. premium brand), and Sunpak (a good mid-range). Bromic's Platinum Smart-Heat series (natural gas, ceiling- or wall-mounted, 21,000 to 38,000 BTU) starts around $1,800 installed for residential. Infratech's WD series wall-mount natural gas heaters run $1,200 to $1,800 installed.

**Installation note**: any natural gas heater installation requires a permit and a licensed gas fitter; the unit itself runs $1,000 to $2,800, and the gas line installation runs $400 to $1,200 depending on distance from the existing gas meter.

## Electric infrared heaters: the cleanest option when you have the wiring

Electric infrared is the closest thing to "set and forget." The heaters produce no flame, no combustion gases, no fuel tank — just infrared radiation from a quartz or tungsten element. They heat objects and people directly, not the air, which means they are unaffected by wind and work under a covered patio, in a screen porch, or even inside a glass-walled sunroom.

**Watt output**: 1,500 to 4,000 watts for residential models, with 2,000 watts (about 6,800 BTU equivalent) being the sweet spot for a small seating area. Most require a 240-volt dedicated circuit (a 20-amp 240V line), though some 1,500-watt units run on a standard 120V outlet.

**Coverage**: a 2,000-watt wall-mount electric heater warms a 6-foot-by-8-foot seating area comfortably in temperatures down to 50°F. Below 40°F they become supplemental rather than primary heat. Multiple units (or higher-watt models) are needed for larger spaces.

**Operating cost**: at the 2026 U.S. average residential electricity rate of about $0.17 per kWh, a 2,000-watt infrared heater costs about $0.34 per hour. That is significantly cheaper to run than propane, but more than a natural gas heater at full output.

**Brand picks**: Infratech (the brand contractors specify, $400 to $700 for a residential W-series 2,000-watt unit, plus $150 to $400 for electric installation), Bromic (Tungsten and Platinum electric lines, $700 to $1,500), and TPI (the commercial-grade value brand). Budget brands from Fire Sense and AZ Patio Heaters run $120 to $350 but have shorter element lives and 1-year warranties.

**Clearance and codes**: electric infrared has the most generous clearance ratings of all types — typically 12 inches from any surface and no overhead restrictions for residential models rated under 4,000 watts. They are the only outdoor heater rated for use under a low ceiling, inside a screened-in room, or in a partially enclosed space, with the caveat that you follow the manufacturer's published clearances exactly.

## Fire pits: the social center of the yard

Fire pits are not primarily a heat source — they are an ambiance source that happens to produce heat. The practical heat output is concentrated around the rim of the bowl.

**Wood-burning fire pits** produce 50,000 to 150,000 BTU at peak, depending on fire size, but the heat is mostly radiant from the top of the flame, so it is felt within a 4-to-8-foot radius and only when the wind cooperates. They are illegal during most burn bans and in many urban jurisdictions outright, so check local code before buying or building one.

**Propane fire pits** are the homeowner's fire pit: 40,000 to 65,000 BTU, ignition by switch, lava rock or glass media, and zero combustion particulates. They produce usable radiant heat within a 5-to-10-foot radius and are the only fire pit type legal in nearly every jurisdiction.

**Natural gas fire pits** are the same as propane but plumbed to the gas line. Once installed, no refilling. The heat output is identical. Brands include Real Flame, Outdoor Great Room, and HPC Fire (the dominant brand for custom gas fire features).

**Running cost**: a 50,000-BTU propane fire pit running at full output consumes a 20-pound tank in 6 to 8 hours. At $4.00 per gallon, that is $2.50 to $3.50 per hour. A natural gas equivalent runs $0.70 to $1.40 per hour. Turn down the flame, and most fire pits run quietly at half-output for twice the time.

**Brand picks**: propane fire pits from Real Flame ($250 to $700 for the propane table-top and bowl designs), Endless Summer (the Hampton Bay line at Home Depot, $200 to $500), and Outland Living ($300 to $600). Custom-built gas fire features using HPC fire kits start around $1,800 for a 36-inch round bowl in copper or stainless.

## Chimineas: the traditional cast-iron or clay option

Chimineas are the clay or cast-iron freestanding fireplaces with a vertical chimney. They produce 30,000 to 50,000 BTU of radiant heat, mostly directional — out of the front opening — so they can be turned to face the seating area.

**Clay chimineas** (Sun Joe, Gardeco, Blue Rooster) are the traditional Mexican style. They are decorative but crack under thermal shock (cold rain on a hot chiminea is the failure mode) and last 2 to 5 years with moderate use. Prices run $120 to $400.

**Cast-iron chimineas** (Blue Rooster, Pilgrim, Gardeco) are the modern practical version. Heavier, less likely to crack, and built to last 15+ years. Prices run $250 to $700.

A chiminea heat radius is roughly 5 to 8 feet. Plan on one for a small seating cluster, not a full patio. Use only seasoned hardwood (kiln-dried oak, hickory, or manufactured fire logs) — softwoods like pine resin up the chimney and cause flare-ups.

## BTU sizing that actually works

A common rule of thumb: 5,000 BTU per square foot for a covered patio; 10,000 BTU per square foot for an open patio with wind exposure. But patio heaters are radiant, and the rule under-counts because it assumes convective heating. A more practical guide:

- **Two-person bistro set, 6×8-foot seating area**: 30,000 to 40,000 BTU propane, or 1,500 to 2,000 W electric.
- **Four-person dining set, 10×10-foot area**: 41,000 BTU standing propane (the standard size), or 2,500 W wall-mount electric.
- **Six-person sectional, 12×14-foot area**: 50,000 BTU standing propane in still air, or a 2,000 W Bromic/Infratech ceiling-mount unit at each end of the seating.
- **Outdoor restaurant patio or open-air commercial space**: 50,000 to 90,000 BTU ceiling-mount units, 12-foot ceiling minimum, spaced every 12 to 14 feet.

If wind is a factor at your site (most backyards have at least 6 to 12 mph afternoon wind), the real coverage radius of any radiant heater shrinks by 30 to 50 percent. Plan for redundancy: two smaller units arranged around the seating, not one large unit at the center.

## Safety clearance and code numbers

Every certified outdoor heater carries a manufacturer-rated clearance number — read the label.

**Standing propane/natural gas mushroom**: 24 inches side, 36 inches overhead.

**Wall-mount electric infrared**: 12 inches side, 18 inches forward (since they emit IR forward, not omnidirectionally).

**Propane fire pit**: typically 24 inches side, 60 inches overhead under a covered structure. Most require a 36-inch non-combustible surface (concrete, pavers, or a manufacturer's heat shield pad, $90 to $200).

**Chiminea**: 36 inches side, 84 inches overhead. Use a spark arrestor mesh screen (often included) and place on a stone or concrete pad.

**Wood-burning fire pit**: 36 to 48 inches side, 84 inches overhead, and never under any combustible overhang.

UL-listed units from the brands listed above meet these numbers. Off-brand heaters from online marketplaces often do not — fail in 5 years, the way we evaluate them, is the gas valve seals and the ignition control boxes.

## The bottom line

For a suburban patio used weekly with a standard seating area, a 41,000-BTU standing propane heater from AZ Patio Heaters is the cheapest answer and runs acceptably for $1.50 to $2.40 per hour. For a fully plumbed outdoor kitchen or daily use, the natural gas installation from Bromic or Infratech halves the operating cost and never needs refilling. For covered porches and windy lots, an electric infrared unit from Infratech ($400 to $700 installed) is the cleanest, quietest, and most controllable option, with the lowest clearances of all the technologies.
